🔑 MASTER KEY-FACTS SHEET (keep for revision)

Unit 1 (Introduction):

  • Biology = scientific study of living things (bios = life, logos = study).
  • Scientific method: Observation → Question → Hypothesis → Experiment → Conclusion → Communication.
  • Magnification = eyepiece × objective. Resolution = ability to distinguish two points.
  • Light microscope uses light; electron microscope uses electrons (higher magnification).

Unit 2 (Classification):

  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
  • Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • Five kingdoms (Whittaker 1969): Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia.
  • Binomial name: genus (capital) + species (small), italicized.

Unit 3 (Cells):

  • Cell theory: all organisms = cells; cells = smallest unit of life; cells arise from pre-existing cells.
  • Mitochondria = powerhouse (respiration); ribosomes = protein; chloroplasts = photosynthesis; lysosomes = digestion.
  • Plant: cell wall (cellulose), chloroplast, large vacuole. Animal: centriole, lysosome.
  • Diffusion = high→low (no energy); Osmosis = water through membrane; Active transport = low→high (uses energy).
  • Hypertonic → shrink/plasmolyse; Hypotonic → turgid/burst; Isotonic → no change.
  • Levels: atom → molecule → organelle → cell → tissue → organ → system → organism.

Unit 4 (Reproduction):

  • Asexual = one parent, no gametes; Sexual = two parents, gametes, fertilization → zygote.
  • Fission (2 or many equal parts); Budding (unequal); Fragmentation (break into parts); Parthenogenesis (unfertilized egg).
  • FSH (follicle/sperm), LH (testosterone/ovulation), estrogen (female secondary), progesterone (thickens lining).
  • Menstrual cycle: flow → follicular → luteal.
  • Abstinence = most effective natural; condoms = dual protection; vasectomy (male)/laparotomy (female) = sterilization.
  • HIV infects T-lymphocytes.

Unit 5 (Health, Nutrition, Disease):

  • Energy: carbohydrate 16 kJ/g, protein 17 kJ/g, lipid 37 kJ/g.
  • Marasmus = carbohydrate deficiency; Kwashiorkor = protein; Anemia = iron; Rickets = vitamin D; Scurvy = vitamin C.
  • BMI = weight(kg)/height²(m²); ≥30 = obese.
  • Cigarette: tar, carbon monoxide, nicotine. Alcohol = depressant. Cannabis = THC. Khat = cathinone.
  • Infectious: HIV/AIDS, TB (Mycobacterium tuberculosis), malaria (Plasmodium, Anopheles vector), COVID-19.

Unit 6 (Ecology):

  • Abiotic (non-living) vs Biotic (living) factors.
  • Levels: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere.
  • Biomes: tropical rain forest, desert, savanna, temperate grassland, taiga, temperate broad-leaf forest, tundra; aquatic (~75% of Earth).
  • Primary vs secondary succession; pioneer species → climax community.
  • Relationships: Competition (−/−), Predation (+/−), Herbivory (+/−), Parasitism (+/−), Mutualism (+/+), Commensalism (+/0).

UNIT 1: INTRODUCTION TO BIOLOGY

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Biology Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

1.1 Definition of Biology

  • Biology is the scientific study of life or living things.
  • The word biology comes from two Greek words: bios (meaning life) and logos (meaning study).
  • What does it mean to be alive? It is difficult to define life in a single sentence, but we recognize life by common characteristics shared by living systems:
  • Living things are composed of one or more cells.
  • They can grow.
  • They can reproduce.
  • They transmit genetic information to their offspring.
  • They need energy to accomplish work.
  • They maintain relatively constant internal conditions (homeostasis).
  • They are capable of evolutionary adaptation to the environment.

Scientific study: Biologists study living things using a scientific method — asking questions, suggesting possible answers, and testing them through experimentation. They verify/observe/measure structures and processes either directly or indirectly with tools such as microscopes.

Biology and other sciences: Biology is a merging point of information and tools from all natural sciences. For example, the study of elements, molecules, acids, and bases in living organisms uses the tools and principles of chemistry; conversion of radiant energy into organic molecules (photosynthesis) is studied using knowledge of chemistry and physics.

1.2 Why Do We Study Biology?

  • Humans have inborn curiosity about the natural world that leads them to study living organisms and their habitats.
  • Biology helps us understand how living things work, function, and interact with the environment.
  • Examples of biological application:
  • Discovery of drugs to treat human diseases.
  • Understanding how our bodies, ecosystems, genetics, and cell biology work — transforming medicine and agriculture.
  • Use of forensic biology to investigate crime.
  • Understanding global warming through ecology.
  • Fermentation used to produce beer and wine.
  • Treating patients with AIDS, tuberculosis, and some types of cancer.

1.3 The Scientific Method

Biology is a science of inquiry. Scientists come up with new theories to explain new findings using the scientific method.

Steps of the scientific method:

  1. Observation — careful observation (often of a problem) leads to a question. Can be direct (sense organs) or indirect (tools like microscopes).
  2. Asking questions — observations lead the scientist to ask questions (inquiry).
  3. Forming a hypothesis — a hypothesis is a proposed scientific explanation (possible answer) for a set of questions. Several hypotheses can be proposed; scientific hypotheses must be testable.
  4. Testing the hypothesis — through experimentation. An experiment must be duplicable so other scientists can repeat it and get similar results. If not supported, propose a new hypothesis.
  5. Making conclusions — scientists ask whether their original hypothesis could still be right in light of new information; if not, they reject it and try alternate explanations.
  6. Communicating the findings — scientists write a paper explaining what they did and the results, and submit it to a scientific journal.
✍️ Must-know: Hypothesis = a proposed, testable scientific explanation. The correct order of the scientific method: Observation → Question → Hypothesis → Experiment → Conclusion/Results → Communication.

Example: A torch (hand lamp) that won't work → observe, ask why, propose hypotheses (batteries dead? bulb broken?), test, conclude.

Note: Activity example — seed germination: Germinate bean seeds at different temperatures (room, cold, hot), measure germination times and seedling lengths, draw a bar graph (X axis = time/day of germination; Y axis = length), interpret, conclude, and report.

1.4 Tools of a Biologist

Biologists use different tools in the laboratory and field — some for measuring, some for observation, some for culturing micro-organisms.

1.4.1 Laboratory Tools

  • Hand lens: a convex lens in a circular metallic loop with a handle; gives a higher magnification than the naked eye and provides an enlarged image, but is not sufficient to observe details in cells.
  • Magnification: the number of times larger an image is than the real size of the object.
  • Resolution: the ability to distinguish between two separate points.
  • Microscope: the most important tool of a biologist; used to observe objects too small to be seen with the naked eye (e.g., shape of bacteria, fungi, parasites).
  • Light microscope: uses a beam of light to view specimens. Two types: simple (single lens) and compound (two or more glass lenses, magnifies up to 2000×). A compound microscope with one eyepiece = monocular; with two = binocular.
  • Electron microscope: uses a beam of electrons and electromagnets instead of light and glass lenses; higher magnification; used for very small micro-organisms such as viruses.
  • Glass slides and cover slips: slides support specimens; cover slips (thin square/circular glass) cover the specimen to protect it from chemicals/dirt, keep moisture in, and provide a better view.
  • Autoclave: equipment used to sterilize (kill micro-organisms) contaminated materials including culture media and bacterial spores, by high temperature and highly pressurized steam. Important because some bacteria form spores resistant to chemical disinfectants.
  • Incubator: maintains the temperature best suited for the growth of micro-organisms (for culturing).
  • Petri dishes: flat dishes with a slightly larger cover; used with gelling culture media such as agar; sterilized in the autoclave.
  • Culture tubes: used to culture micro-organisms with solid (gel) or liquid (broth) media.
  • Flasks: flat-bottomed, long narrow neck; allow easy mixing and gentle heating; large-volume culture tubes used with broth media to produce large populations.
  • Balance: for making up solutions accurately and weighing specimens.
  • Dropper: glass tube with a vacuum rubber bulb; used to control the amount of solution added.
  • Tongs: metallic, scissors-shaped; used to lift/pick up hot objects (crucibles, beakers, dishes, flasks).
  • Dissecting tool kit: used to dissect animals (frogs, fetal pigs, mice); includes scalpel, scissors, forceps, pins, etc.
  • Dissecting pan: pan on which a specimen is kept while being dissected.
  • Crucible: small ceramic or metal container able to withstand high temperatures; used to melt elements.
  • Beaker: cylindrical glass container for making up solutions, holding hot/cold water or ice baths.
  • Hotplate: for heating solutions, making up agar culture medium.
  • pH meter: measures pH (acidity/basicity) of substances.
  • Thermometer: measures temperature.
  • Forceps: hold or pick up small objects.
  • Spatula: spoon-shaped; for mixing, stirring, and scraping.
  • Wash bottles: flexible; used to rinse laboratory materials (squeeze to adjust water pressure).
  • Bunsen burner / alcohol burner: produces a single open flame for heating and sterilization.

1.4.2 Field Tools

  • Insect nets: net bag of cloth/fine mesh on a wire loop attached to a pole; used to collect insects.
  • Fishing nets: nets (fish traps) made from fibres woven in a grid-like structure.
✍️ Must-know: Magnification = how many times larger; Resolution = ability to distinguish two separate points. Electron microscope gives higher magnification than a light microscope.

1.5 The Light Microscope

  • Most microscopes have several powerful lenses allowing viewing at more than 100× actual size.
  • Light rays are focused onto the specimen on a slide; light transmitted through the specimen is focused by eye (ocular) and objective lenses.
  • Total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification. (e.g., ×10 eyepiece × ×40 objective = ×400 total.)

1.5.2 Handling and Using a Light Microscope

  • Grip the microscope by the arm with one hand and support the base with the other; hold upright.
  • Make sure the slide is clean and dry; place the most promising region in the middle of the stage hole.
  • Always focus on low power (4×) first; use coarse knob first, then fine knob.
  • Use immersion oil only with the 100× objective (one drop only).
  • Always focus by moving the lens and specimen further apart, never closer.
  • Never touch lens surfaces with fingers.
  • Clean with lens paper and alcohol when done; place the 4× objective over the stage, lower the stage, turn off light, wrap cord, and return to cabinet with oculars toward the back.

1.6 General Laboratory Safety Rules

Safety = the condition of being protected from harm or danger; control of recognized hazards.

Important general laboratory safety rules:

  1. Follow the instructions — read/listen to the procedure and be familiar with all steps; know how to use all equipment.
  2. Know the location of safety equipment — fire extinguisher and first-aid kit must be readily available; know how to use them; review lab safety signs.
  3. Dress for the laboratory — lab coat, safety goggles, gloves, hearing protection, long pants, long-sleeve shirt, leather shoes/boots (no sandals). Wear a disposable respirator mask when handling toxic chemicals.
  4. Never eat or drink in the laboratory.
  5. Never taste or sniff chemicals — label containers and read before use.
  6. Act responsibly — never randomly mix chemicals (could cause explosion, fire, or toxic gas).
  7. Clean the experiment area and store waste properly.
  8. Handle chemicals properly — never allow chemicals to contact bare skin.
  9. Know what to do with accidents — if burned or exposed to chemicals, immediately flood the area with cold tap water for several minutes.
  10. Always emphasize safety and chemical hygiene — never leave chemical containers open; all containers must be labeled; never use unlabeled chemicals.

UNIT 2: CHARACTERISTICS AND CLASSIFICATION OF ORGANISMS

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Biology Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

2.1 Characteristics of Living Things

We cannot define life in a single sentence, but we can list characteristics shared by living systems:

  1. Made of one or more cells — unicellular (one cell, e.g., bacteria) or multicellular (many cells, e.g., plants).
  2. Require energy — all organisms use energy for metabolic activities.
  • Producers/autotrophs: make their own food using sunlight (photosynthesis), e.g., plants.
  • Consumers/heterotrophs: cannot make their own food, consume others.
  1. Respond to stimuli — detect/sense changes (stimuli) in the internal or external environment and make appropriate responses.
  2. Grow — growth is a permanent increase in size and mass due to an increase in cell number, cell size, or both.
  3. Reproduce — process that makes more of the same kind of organism (sexually or asexually).
  4. Excrete — removal of metabolic wastes (e.g., carbon dioxide) produced by chemical reactions.
  5. Display ordered complexity — living things are both complex and highly ordered (atoms → molecules → ...).
  6. Maintain homeostasis — regulation of internal conditions to maintain stability (e.g., stable body temperature).
  7. Possess adaptations that evolve over time — organisms evolve adaptations to their environments.

2.2 Taxonomy of Living Things

2.2.1 Principles of Classification

  • Classification: the process of grouping things based on their similarities.
  • Taxonomy: the science of naming, identifying, and classifying organisms.
  • Taxonomists: scientists who study taxonomy.
  • Biologists classify organisms by degrees of apparent similarities and differences — external and internal structures, where the organism lives, and genetic makeup to reveal evolutionary relationships.
  • The greater the physical similarity, the closer the biological relationship is assumed.

Key terms: Identification = identifying organisms using characteristic features; Nomenclature = the aspect of taxonomy dealing specifically with naming organisms.

2.2.2 Taxonomic Hierarchies

  • Aristotle (384–322 BC), Greek philosopher, developed the first widely accepted classification system — grouped plants into trees, shrubs, herbs; animals into those with/without red blood.
  • Carolus Linnaeus (1707–1778), Swedish botanist, "father of taxonomy," introduced a taxonomic hierarchy in the 1700s.
  • Taxonomic hierarchy: arranging organisms into successive levels in decreasing or increasing order.
  • Ranks from highest to lowest: Kingdom → Phylum (Division) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
  • Each rank is a taxon (plural: taxa).
  • Linnaeus developed a two-kingdom system (Plantae and Animalia), which did not distinguish eukaryotes/prokaryotes, unicellular/multicellular, or photosynthetic/non-photosynthetic organisms.
  • Three domains (rank above kingdom): Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • Bacteria: prokaryotic cells (no nucleus), small; cell wall contains peptidoglycans; use chlorophyll for photosynthesis; divide by binary fission.
  • Archaea: prokaryotic; cell wall does NOT contain peptidoglycans; often live in extreme environments.
  • Eukarya: cells with a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; includes protists, fungi, plants, animals; DNA as linear chromosomes with histone proteins; reproduce asexually and sexually.
✍️ Must-know: Taxonomic hierarchy: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. (Plant taxonomists use "division" instead of phylum.) Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.

2.3 Relevance of Classification

  • Identification of harmful and beneficial organisms.
  • Reduces energy and time to study organisms.
  • Helps understand relationships between organisms (e.g., birds and dinosaurs have hollow bones → dinosaurs more closely related to birds than reptiles).
  • Useful in agriculture, forestry, and medicine.
  • Helps the economy — taxonomists can discover new sources of lumber, foods, medicines, energy (e.g., Moringa/Shiferaw Moringa stenopetala contains good disinfectants).
Note: Species definition (note box): A species is the smallest natural group of organisms — a group that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring. Members often resemble each other closely.

2.4 Linnaean System of Nomenclature

  • Linnaeus published Systema Naturae in 1735, describing 7700 plant species; grouped species into genera, genera into classes, classes into orders; classified over 4000 animals.
  • Binomial nomenclature (binomial system): an internationally agreed system where the scientific name of an organism has two parts — the genus and the specific epithet (species).
  • "Binomial" means "two names."
  • A genus (plural genera) consists of a group of similar species.
  • Example: maize = Zea mays; human = Homo sapiens; Enset (false banana) = Ensete ventricosum; Banana = Musa acuminata (same family Musaceae).

Rules for writing scientific names:

  • The first letter of the genus is capitalized; the specific epithet starts with a small letter.
  • Underlined when handwritten; italicized when printed.

2.6 The Five-Kingdom System of Classification

  • The two-kingdom system was found inadequate (many organisms didn't fit).
  • Whittaker (1969) proposed the five-kingdom classification based on: cell structure, body organization, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic relationships.
  • The five kingdoms: Animalia, Plantae, Fungi, Monera, Protista.
✍️ Must-know: Whittaker's five kingdoms = Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia.
Note: Viruses note box: Viruses are NOT included in any kingdom — not considered living because they lack key characteristics (don't grow, develop, or respire on their own). They are made of nucleic acids (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat; they replicate only inside a living host cell.

2.6.1 Kingdom Monera

  • Includes eubacteria (true bacteria) and archaebacteria (archaea).
  • Eubacteria: strong cell walls; various shapes; heterotrophic or autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic); live in most habitats; some cause disease (strep throat, pneumonia), most are harmless/helpful.
  • Archaebacteria: very different cell walls; make their own food; live in extreme environments (swamps, deep-ocean hot-water vents, evaporating ponds), often with no oxygen.

Important characteristics of Monera:

  • Simple prokaryotic unicellular organisms.
  • Lack a well-defined nucleus or any membrane-bound organelles.
  • Most have a rigid cell wall.
  • Various modes of nutrition (autotrophs or heterotrophs).
  • Known as decomposers and mineralizers in the biosphere.

2.6.2 Kingdom Protista

  • The most diverse kingdom; no such thing as a typical protist.
  • Single-celled or multicellular; microscopic or very large; some autotrophs, some heterotrophs.
  • All protists are eukaryotes — this is their one common feature.
  • Protozoans: animal-like, one cell.
  • Algae: plant-like, photosynthetic autotrophs (no roots/stems/leaves); unicellular algae are the basis of aquatic food chains and produce much of Earth's oxygen.
  • Some protists resemble fungi but have different cell walls.

2.6.3 Kingdom Fungi

  • Eukaryotic organisms including yeasts, moulds, and mushrooms.
  • Bodies made of thread-like structures called hyphae.
  • Cell wall made of chitin and polysaccharides.
  • Heterotrophic — absorb nutrients from the environment (extracellular digestion: secrete enzymes, digest, then absorb).
  • Saprophytes (decomposers): absorb soluble organic matter from dead substrates (fallen logs, animal corpses, wastes).
  • Can live as parasites (pathogenic — Candida albicans causes thrush, Tinea pedis causes athlete's foot) or mutualists (e.g., fungi inside termites digest wood).
  • Can be symbionts: lichens (with algae) and mycorrhiza (with plant roots).
  • Reproduce asexually by vegetative means (fragmentation, fission, budding), by spores, and sexually; spores produced in fruiting bodies.
  • Yeast (Saccharomyces) is used to make injera rise and produce alcohol (Tej, Tella, Beer).

2.6.4 Kingdom Plantae

  • All eukaryotic, multicellular, autotrophic organisms.
  • Make their own food by photosynthesis; have chloroplasts and chlorophyll; produce oxygen.
  • Cells have a rigid cell wall made of cellulose.
  • Nearly all live on land; sexual reproduction predominates; reproduce asexually by vegetative propagation or sexually.
  • Over 250 000 species (flowering plants, mosses, ferns, coniferous plants).

Plant key terms: Angiosperms = seed plants that make flowers/fruits (monocots, dicots); Gymnosperms = seed plants that do not make flowers/fruits (conifers); Bryophytes = mosses, liverworts, hornworts (no vascular tissue, seedless); Vascular plants = have xylem and phloem; Monocots = one seed leaf, parallel veins; Dicots = two seed leaves, branching leaf veins.

2.6.5 Kingdom Animalia

  • All multicellular, heterotrophic, eukaryotic organisms.
  • Heterotrophs that ingest food and digest it within their bodies (unlike fungi that absorb).
  • Lack cell walls; held together by structural proteins, most abundant being collagen (found only in animals).
  • Have specialized muscle cells and nerve cells (organized into muscle and nervous tissue) — central to what it means to be an animal.
  • Most reproduce sexually.
  • Classified into invertebrates (no backbone) and vertebrates (with a backbone).
  • Invertebrates: sponges, flatworms, roundworms, annelida, Mollusca, Arthropoda (Insects, Arachnids, Crustacea, Myriapods).
  • Vertebrates: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals.
✍️ Must-know: Plants = cell walls of cellulose, autotrophic. Fungi = cell walls of chitin, heterotrophic (absorb). Animals = no cell walls (collagen), heterotrophic (ingest), have muscle + nerve cells.

UNIT 3: CELLS

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Biology Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

3.1 What is a Cell?

  • Cell: the smallest and basic unit of living things. All living things are composed of cells.
  • Unicellular organisms: consist of a single cell (e.g., paramecium); most invisible to the naked eye (exceptions: chicken egg cells).
  • Multicellular organisms: consist of many specialized cells that work cooperatively (plants, animals, humans).
  • In the human body, muscle cells move, nerve cells gather information, brain cells interpret messages.
  • Cells vary in size: an ostrich egg is a single cell about 6 inches in diameter (≈1.36 kg); nerve cells can stretch several feet long; giant squid nerve cells can be 30+ feet long.

3.2 Cell Theory

  • Robert Hooke (English scientist, 1665) was the first to observe cells and use the word "cell" — examining thin slices of cork; he saw pore-like regular structures he called cells, without realizing it was the fundamental unit of all living things.
  • Anton van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723), Dutch microbiologist, "father of microbiology" — built microscopes with magnification approaching 300×; observed protozoa (1674) and bacteria.
  • Matthias Schleiden (German botanist, 1838): all plants are made up of cells.
  • Theodor Schwann (German zoologist, 1839): all animals are made up of cells.
  • Rudolf Virchow (German pathologist, 1856): cells arise only by the division of existing cells.

Cell theory states:

  1. Living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
  2. Cells are the smallest unit of life.
  3. Cells arise only by the division of a pre-existing cell.

Common features of cells:

  • Surrounded by a membrane that separates cell contents from outside.
  • Contain genetic material storing instructions for activities.
  • Have their own energy release system — the smallest living structures.

3.3 Cell Structure and Function

The major structures plant and animal cells have in common: Nucleus, Cytoplasm, Cell (plasma) membrane.

Organelles = distinct structures within a cell having a specific function (some have a membrane, some don't).

Structures and Functions

  • Cell wall: rigid structure providing structural support and shape; found external to the cell membrane; in plant cells, fungal cells, some protists (algae), and bacteria. Animal cells have no cell wall. Made of cellulose (plants).
  • Cell membrane: outer covering that separates the cell's interior from the environment; a thin selectively (semi) permeable structure; controls what enters and leaves the cell (organic molecules, ions, water, oxygen, carbon dioxide).
  • Cytoplasm: thick fluid-filled region containing organelles; 70–80% water with organic (proteins, carbohydrates) and inorganic (sodium, potassium, calcium ions) molecules; site of chemical reactions such as protein synthesis.
  • Nucleus: the largest organelle, surrounded by a double membrane; contains chromosomes made of long DNA molecules; determines what the cell will be; controls cell division; contains the nucleolus (site where new ribosomes are made).
  • Ribosomes: small structures found in large numbers in all cells; synthesize proteins; found free in the cytoplasm or attached to the rough ER.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): complex system of interconnected double membranes with fluid-filled spaces for transport. Two types:
  • Rough ER: dotted with ribosomes; makes and stores proteins, transported in small sacs called vesicles.
  • Smooth ER: no ribosomes; important in manufacturing lipids.
  • Vesicles: small membranous sacs; store, transport, and ship materials (e.g., a protein made by the rough ER is shipped to the Golgi in a transport vesicle).
  • Golgi apparatus: smooth, concave membranous structure; collects, packages, and distributes molecules within or outside the cell; modifies proteins and lipids, packages them into vesicles.
  • Mitochondria: relatively large, double-membrane organelles; outer membrane controls entry/exit; inner membrane folds (where respiration reactions occur); "powerhouse of the cell" — sites of respiration.
  • Lysosomes: membrane-bound packages of digestive enzymes; digest bacteria/other cells taken into the cell; break down unwanted or damaged organelles.
  • Vacuoles: single-membrane organelles with fluid inside. Plant cells have large central vacuoles (often > half the cell volume); animal cells have small vacuoles. Functions: food vacuoles digest food; contractile vacuoles (in some unicellular eukaryotes) expel excess water (osmoregulation).
  • Chloroplasts: double-membrane organelles in green plant cells (mostly leaves); site of photosynthesis; contain green pigment chlorophyll that traps sunlight energy to synthesize carbohydrates.
  • Centrioles and microtubules: microtubules are small cylindrical fibres that move chromosomes during cell division; animal cells have centrioles that anchor to microtubules.
  • Cilia and flagella: organelles for locomotion in unicellular organisms.
  • Cilia: short, hair-like, numerous; used for locomotion or creating currents (e.g., on the lining of air tubes serving the lungs).
  • Flagella: long, thread-like, fewer in number; enable entire cell to move (one or a few per cell).

3.4 Types of Cells

  • Prokaryotic cells: usually much smaller; simpler structure; thought to be the first cells to evolve; lack a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles (e.g., bacterial cells). Organisms are unicellular. Cell wall protects, maintains shape, prevents dehydration.
  • Eukaryotic cells: have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; usually multicellular organisms (plants, animals, fungi, protists).
  • Greek: eu = true, karyon = kernel/nucleus → "true nucleus." Prokaryotic = pro (before) + karyon → "before nucleus."
  • Eukaryotic cells are usually 10–100 µm wide; prokaryotes 1–10 µm.

3.5 Animal and Plant Cells

Similarities: cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus.

Differences:

  • Cell wall, chloroplast, and large permanent vacuoles are found in plant cells.
  • Centriole and lysosome are found in animal cells.
  • Plant cells are usually larger and more easily visible under a light microscope.
  • The plant vacuole contains a solution of pigments, enzymes, sugars, organic compounds, mineral salts, oxygen, and carbon dioxide; helps regulate osmotic properties.
  • Chloroplasts are in the green parts of the plant, mainly leaves.

3.6 Observing Cells under a Microscope

  • A microscope magnifies the image of small objects (even cells) through at least one lens.
  • Plant cell activity (onion): peel epidermal tissue from an onion, place on slide, add iodine solution for staining (makes the nucleus/cell wall more visible), cover with cover slip at 45°, view under microscope, draw and label cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus.
  • Animal cell activity (cheek cell): collect cheek cells with a cotton bud, place on slide, add methylene blue dye for staining, cover slip at 45°, view, draw and label cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus. (Use disinfectant/autoclave on used slides; precautions due to small risk of AIDS transmission.)

3.7 The Cell and its Environment

The cell membrane is selectively (semi) permeable — it controls what enters and leaves. Materials pass through by passive transport (diffusion, osmosis) or active transport.

3.7.1 Passive Transport

Movement of substances down a concentration gradient (high → low concentration) without input of energy.

Diffusion: net movement of molecules/ions from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration. Important for movement of gases (e.g., oxygen for respiration enters by diffusion).

Factors influencing the rate of diffusion:

  • Concentration gradient: higher difference → faster diffusion.
  • Temperature: higher temperature → more kinetic energy → faster movement.
  • Mass of the molecule: heavier molecules diffuse more slowly; lighter diffuse faster.
  • Distance travelled: farther → slower.
  • Surface area: greater surface area → faster total diffusion (e.g., microvilli on cells of the kidney/intestine increase absorbing surface).

Osmosis: a special case of diffusion — the diffusion of water molecules from a region of their higher concentration (dilute solution) to a region of their lower concentration (concentrated solution) across a partially permeable membrane.

Tonicity terms:

  • Hypertonic solution: high solute concentration, low water concentration → water moves out of the cell → cell shrinks/plasmolyses.
  • Isotonic solution: equal solute and water → no net movement, no change in cell size.
  • Hypotonic solution: low solute concentration, high water concentration → water moves into the cell → cell becomes turgid (plant cells resist bursting due to cell wall; animal cells may burst = hemolysis).

Osmosis in plant cells: brings strength/rigidity (turgor pressure). In a hypotonic solution water enters the vacuole, presses on the cell wall → turgid. In a hypertonic solution the plant cell loses water, the plasma membrane shrinks away (plasmolysis), the cell wilts and dies.

Osmosis in animal cells: the concentration of blood water is monitored by the brain and adjusted by the kidneys. In a hypotonic solution, water enters and animal cells (no cell wall) may burst (hemolysis) — e.g., red blood cells. In a hypertonic solution, water leaves and the cell shrinks.

Osmosis in amoeba: freshwater unicellular protozoans have contractile vacuoles that accumulate and expel excess water (osmoregulation), preventing the cell from bursting.

✍️ Must-know: Diffusion = high → low concentration (any substance). Osmosis = water moving through a membrane. Passive = no energy. Hypertonic → cell loses water (shrink/plasmolyse); Hypotonic → cell gains water (turgid/burst); Isotonic → no net change.

3.7.2 Active Transport

Movement of molecules/ions across the cell membrane against the concentration gradient (low → high concentration), using energy released during respiration.

  • Enables cells to concentrate molecules inside or outside the cell.
  • Examples: ion uptake by root hairs, uptake of glucose by epithelial cells of villi.
  • Example: the sodium-potassium pump — nerve cells pump sodium out and potassium in (vital to the nervous system).

3.8 Levels of Biological Organization

Life forms a hierarchy of organization from atoms to complex multicellular organisms:

Atoms → Molecules → Organelles → Cells → Tissues → Organs → Organ systems → Organism → Populations → Communities → Ecosystems → Biosphere

  • Cellular level: atoms joined by chemical bonds into molecules; complex biological molecules assembled into organelles within cells. Most cells become specialized (one particular job).
  • Tissue: a group of similar cells that act as a functional unit (e.g., nerve tissue, muscle). Leaf tissues include epidermis, palisade tissue, spongy tissue, xylem, phloem.
  • Organ: a body structure composed of several different tissues acting as a structural/functional unit (e.g., brain, stomach, heart, lungs, intestines, eyes; in plants the root, stem, leaves).
  • System: different organs working together (e.g., digestive system = stomach + intestine). Different systems collectively form an organism.
  • Beyond organisms: populations → communities → ecosystems → biosphere.
✍️ Must-know: Levels of organization: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism (→ population → community → ecosystem → biosphere).

UNIT 4: REPRODUCTION

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Biology Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

4.1 Introduction to Reproduction

  • Reproduction is one of the unique characteristics of life — the ability to reproduce their own kind best distinguishes living things from non-living matter.
  • Two modes: asexual and sexual.
  • Asexual reproduction: one parent, no special reproductive organs or cells; produces identical copies of itself.
  • Sexual reproduction: involves two parents, each contributing special germ cells (gametes: egg or sperm) that unite in fertilization to develop a new individual.

4.2 Asexual Reproduction

  • Production of individuals without gametes (eggs or sperm); without sex or a second parent.
  • Appears in bacteria, unicellular eukaryotes, many invertebrates, fungi, plants. Absent among vertebrates.
  • Basic forms: fission (binary and multiple), budding, fragmentation.
  • Advantages: no mate needed; no gametes needed; all good characteristics of the parent pass to offspring; offspring grow in the same favourable environment; plants store food for survival.
  • Disadvantages: little variation → adaptation (evolution) unlikely; if parent has no disease resistance, offspring won't either; lack of dispersal → competition for nutrients, water, light.

4.3 Types of Asexual Reproduction

4.3.1 Fission

  • The organism divides into two (binary) or more (multiple) equal parts.
  • Binary fission: common among bacteria, algae, protozoa. The unicellular parent divides by mitosis into two approximately equal parts; the bacterial nucleus is replicated first so each daughter gets one copy. Rapid — some bacteria divide every 20–30 minutes, forming a bacterial colony.
  • Multiple fission: the nucleus divides repeatedly; each daughter nucleus breaks away with a small portion of cytoplasm, producing many daughter cells (common among some parasitic protozoa, e.g., malarial parasites).

4.3.2 Fragmentation

  • Breakdown of a parent organism into parts that develop into whole organisms.
  • Observed in fungi, plants, animals, algae.
  • Example: Spirogyra (filamentous green algae) fragments into many filaments, each growing into a mature filament. Multicellular animals (worms) can break into parts, each regenerating into a complete individual.

4.3.3 Budding

  • The organism divides into two unequal parts; common in fungal species and invertebrates (hydra).
  • A bulge (bud) forms on the side of the cell; the nucleus divides mitotically; one daughter nucleus migrates into the bud; the bud detaches from the mother cell.
  • Example: yeast buds on the surface of the cell or hypha.

Sporulation

  • Most fungi reproduce asexually by forming spores. Spores disperse by air currents and grow new hyphae → mycelium. A swelling at the tip (sporangium — spore sac) forms. Examples: Penicillium and Mucor moulds.

4.3.4 Vegetative Propagation

  • A method of asexual reproduction in plants where structures with lateral meristems (roots, stems, buds, leaves) give rise to new self-supporting individuals.

Natural vegetative propagation:

  • Stolons (runners): grow along the soil surface; roots, flowers, leaves produced at nodes (e.g., cultivated strawberry).
  • Rhizomes: horizontal underground stems; buds at nodes produce shoots (e.g., couch grass).
  • Corms: rounded, fleshy, similar to rhizomes; store food (e.g., gladiolus).
  • Tubers: modified stems that store starch (e.g., potato, Solanum); swollen ends of stolons with buds.
  • Bulb: modification of a stem with enlarged fleshy leaves (underground storage unit, e.g., onion).

Artificial vegetative propagation:

  • Grafting: combines favourable stem and root characteristics; the stem to be grafted = scion, the root = stock.
  • Cutting: a portion of stem with nodes/internodes placed in moist soil (e.g., hibiscus); some leaves root in water (crotons).
  • Layering (runner): a stem attached to the plant is bent and covered with soil.
  • Also marcotting and micropropagation.

4.3.5 Parthenogenesis

  • "Virgin birth" — a form of asexual reproduction in which an unfertilized egg develops into an adult organism.
  • Example: honeybees (Apis mellifera) — unfertilized eggs develop into males (drones). Whip tail lizard (Aspidoscelis uniparens) produces only females via parthenogenesis (each is a clone of its mother).
✍️ Must-know: Fission = divides into 2+ equal parts; Budding = divides into 2 unequal parts; Fragmentation = breaks into parts; Parthenogenesis = unfertilized egg develops; Vegetative propagation = plant parts give rise to new plants.

4.4 Sexual Reproduction in Humans

  • Involves the production of sex cells (gametes) made in reproductive organs through meiosis.
  • Almost always two parents.
  • Starts with the union of sperm and egg = fertilization, which can be internal or external.
  • Fertilization results in a single cell called a zygote, which grows into a new individual.
  • Female gametes (eggs) are always larger than male gametes (sperm) and are not mobile.

4.5 Primary and Secondary Sexual Characteristics

  • Primary sexual characteristics: present at birth.
  • Males: penis, scrotum, testicles.
  • Females: vagina, uterus, ovaries.
  • Puberty (adolescence): begins in the early teen years; the time when secondary sex characteristics begin to develop; sexual maturity is reached (potential for sexual reproduction).
  • Secondary sexual characteristics in males: growth/maintenance of male sex organs, increase in body hair, increase in muscle mass, increased growth of long bones of arms/legs, deepening of the voice.
  • Secondary sexual characteristics in females: increased growth of long bones, more hair (underarms, pubic area), hips broaden, more fat deposited in breasts/buttocks/thighs, menstrual cycle begins.

Hormonal control of puberty:

  • The hypothalamus produces hormones that cause the pituitary gland to release FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinizing hormone).
  • In males: FSH → production of sperm cells; LH → production of testosterone in the testes (responsible for secondary sex characteristics).
  • In females: LH → causes eggs to be released into the oviduct (ovulation); FSH → stimulates follicle development and causes estrogen to be released (responsible for female secondary sex characteristics).

Psychological/social changes during puberty: body image changes; early/late puberty can cause low self-esteem and psychological pain; adolescents should seek medical/psychological advice if needed.

  • Adolescence = the period between puberty and adulthood, when a good deal of social learning takes place.
  • Biologically adult = reached puberty (capable of having children); economically adult = can support themselves; morally adult = responsible for actions.

4.6 Male Reproductive Structures

Organs: paired testes, epididymis, vas deferens, accessory glands (seminal vesicles, prostate gland, bulbourethral glands), and penis.

  • Testes: sites of sperm production; composed of seminiferous tubules (where sperm develop); between the tubules are cells producing testosterone. Housed in the scrotum (outside the abdominal cavity, providing a slightly lower temperature needed for viable sperm).
  • Epididymis: where sperm maturation occurs.
  • Vas deferens: the ejaculatory duct that joins the urethra (carries both sperm and urine through the penis).
  • Accessory glands: seminal vesicles (secrete fructose-rich fluid), prostate gland (produces alkaline secretions that raise the pH of the female reproductive tract), bulbourethral glands (secrete lubricating mucus that clears the urethra of residual urine). These secretions provide food, lubricate, and counteract vaginal acidity.
  • Semen: a mix of sperm, proteins, nutrients, ions, signalling molecules. Sperm constitute less than 5% of semen volume.

Sperm structure (relation to function):

  • Head: packed with genetic material, covered by an enzyme that helps penetrate the egg.
  • Mid-section: contains many mitochondria supplying energy for flagellar movement.
  • Flagellum: used to swim toward the egg.
✍️ Must-know: Sperm produced in the seminiferous tubules of the testes; mature in the epididymis; travel via vas deferens and urethra. Seminal vesicles = fructose; prostate = alkaline; bulbourethral = lubricating mucus. Testosterone = male hormone.

4.7 Female Reproductive Structures

Organs: paired ovaries, oviducts (fallopian/uterine tubes), uterus, cervix, vagina, and external genitals (labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, hymen).

  • Ovaries: female gonads; produce and release eggs; secrete estrogens (trigger secondary sexual characteristics, maintain reproductive tract lining) and progesterone (thickens the lining in preparation for pregnancy). A girl is born with about 2 million eggs; only 300–400 reach maturity during her fertile years (about 30–35 years).
  • Oviducts: hollow tubes connecting ovaries to uterus; lined with cilia for propelling the egg; usual site of fertilization.
  • Uterus (womb): hollow, pear-shaped organ specialized for housing the embryo; has thick muscular walls, blood vessels, and lining = endometrium.
  • Cervix: narrowed region at the bottom of the uterus, opens into the vagina.
  • Vagina: extends from the cervix to the body's surface; organ of intercourse and the birth canal.
  • External genitals: labia majora (thick outer folds of adipose tissue), labia minora (thin inner folds), clitoris (contains erectile tissue, sensitive), hymen (membrane reducing vaginal opening).

Ovulation:

  • Egg production occurs before birth. At puberty, hormonal changes prompt eggs to mature one at a time in an approximately 28-day ovarian cycle.
  • About two weeks after the follicle begins to mature, its wall ruptures → ovulation (egg + follicle cells ejected into the oviduct).
  • After ovulation, the ruptured follicle develops into a hormone-secreting corpus luteum. If pregnancy does not occur, the corpus luteum breaks down and a new follicle begins to mature.

4.8 The Menstrual Cycle

  • The approximately monthly changes in the uterus, coordinated with the ovarian cycle. The first day is marked by the onset of menstruation (flow of bits of uterine lining and blood out through the vagina).

Three phases:

  1. Flow phase (menstruation): the first phase — shedding of the uterine lining.
  2. Follicular phase: the second phase — FSH stimulates maturation of an ovarian follicle; follicle cells secrete estrogens that stimulate the endometrium to thicken; rising estrogens encourage the pituitary to release more LH.
  3. Luteal phase: the third phase (after ovulation) — LH stimulates formation of the corpus luteum, which secretes some estrogens and a lot of progesterone; these thicken the uterine lining and grow blood vessels, preparing the uterus for pregnancy.
  • Menopause: when all follicles have been released or disintegrated; estrogen and progesterone production diminishes and menstrual cycles cease (known only in humans and two species of whales).

Menstrual hygiene & health benefits:

  • Wear lightweight, breathable clothing (cotton underwear).
  • Change menstrual products regularly.
  • Keep the genital area clean; wipe front to back; use only water to rinse the vulva.
  • Use unscented products.
  • Drink enough liquids.
  • Track/monitor your period.
  • Annual check-up (pap smear, pelvic exam, breast exam).
✍️ Must-know: Menstrual cycle phases: flow → follicular → luteal. FSH → follicle maturation; LH → ovulation + corpus luteum; progesterone → thickens uterine lining. Menopause = end of menstrual cycles.

4.9 Fertilization and Pregnancy

Mating/copulation: sexual arousal → male erection (blood into erectile tissue); female vagina produces mucus to lubricate; male inserts the penis and ejaculates semen into the top of the vagina.

Fertilization:

  • Sperm swim through the cervix and uterus into the oviduct; one sperm bumps into the egg, enters its cytoplasm, and its nucleus fuses with the female nucleus = fertilization.
  • The released egg survives about 24 hours; sperm can fertilize an egg for about 2–3 days; so there is only a short fertile period of about 4 days each month when fertilization might occur.

Twins (multiple births):

  • Identical (monozygotic) twins: from one zygote; strikingly alike, always the same sex.
  • Non-identical (dizygotic/fraternal) twins: from two zygotes; resemble each other like any siblings.
  • Triplets, quadruplets, quintuplets may include a pair of identical twins.

Pregnancy and development:

  • The zygote divides (2 → 4 → ... ) into a solid ball of cells (early embryo), travels down the oviduct to the uterus, and sinks into the uterine lining = implantation.
  • After 8 weeks, when all organs are formed, the embryo is called a fetus.
  • The embryo is enclosed in a fluid-filled sac = amnion (water sac); the fluid = amniotic fluid (protects from damage).
  • The placenta attaches to the uterine lining and connects to the embryo by the umbilical cord; oxygen and nutrients (glucose, amino acids) pass across the placenta to the embryo; carbon dioxide passes back. Blood of mother and embryo do not mix.
Note: Neural tube defects (NTDs) note box: birth defects of the brain, spine, or spinal cord; occur when the neural tube does not close properly in the first month of pregnancy. Women should take folic acid rich foods before and during pregnancy (at least 3 months before).

4.10 Methods of Birth Control

Benefits of family planning/contraception: prevent pregnancy-related health risks; reduce infant mortality; empower people and enhance education; reduce adolescent pregnancies; slow population growth; condoms help prevent STIs/HIV; reduce unsafe abortion.

4.10.1 Natural Methods of Family Planning

  • Abstinence: avoiding sexual intercourse; the most effective method, also prevents exposure to STI pathogens.
  • Fertility awareness / natural family planning: relying on understanding when ovulation occurs to avoid or use a barrier. Includes Standard Days Method, Calendar Rhythm Method, Symptothermal Method, Ovulation Method. Effectiveness 98% (perfect) to 78% (typical).
  • Withdrawal (coitus interruptus): pulling the penis out before ejaculation. Effectiveness 96% (perfect) to 82% (typical). No STI protection.
  • Monitoring body temperature: temperature rises ~0.5 °C around ovulation; calculate fertile period.
  • Cervical mucus method: mucus becomes more fluid near ovulation.

Advantages of natural methods: no side effects, permitted by most religions. Disadvantages: depend on full cooperation of both partners; not always easy to pinpoint ovulation.

4.10.2 Artificial (Barrier & Other) Methods of Family Planning

  • Female condom: polyurethane sheath; 95% (perfect) / 80% (typical); dual protection against pregnancy and STI/HIV.
  • Male condom: latex, lambskin, or polyurethane; 98% (perfect) / 85% (typical); protects against pregnancy and STI/HIV (lambskin not recommended for infection prevention).
  • Diaphragm: thin rubber disc covering the cervix; stops sperm entering; ~95% effective with spermicides; does not prevent STIs.
  • Spermicides: chemicals that kill or immobilize sperm (cream, gel, foam); not very reliable alone but effective with condoms/diaphragms.
  • IUD (intra-uterine device): small T-shaped plastic + copper device ("coil") inserted into the uterus; prevents implantation; ~98% effective; small risk of uterine infections; no STI protection.
  • IUS (intra-uterine system): similar to IUD, releases progesterone slowly (up to 5 years), prevents ovulation; no STI protection.
  • Contraceptive pill: contains chemicals like estrogen and progesterone; suppresses ovulation; ~99% effective; long-term use of some types may increase risk of breast/cervical cancer; no STI protection.
  • Contraceptive implant: small plastic tube (~4 cm) under the skin of the upper arm; releases progesterone; lasts ~3 years; >99% success; no STI protection.
  • Contraceptive injection: contains progesterone; effective 8–12 weeks; thickens cervical mucus, thins uterine lining; no STI protection.
  • Surgical methods:
  • Vasectomy (male sterilization): sperm ducts cut and sealed; semen contains no sperm; testosterone still produced; sexual function unaffected.
  • Laparotomy / tubal ligation (female sterilization): oviducts tied, blocked, or cut; sperm can no longer reach the egg; sexual desire and menstruation continue.
  • Both are almost 100% effective and permanent; can sometimes be reversed by microsurgery.
✍️ Must-know: Abstinence = most effective natural method. Condoms = dual protection (pregnancy + STI/HIV). Vasectomy = male (cut vas deferens); laparotomy/tubal ligation = female (block oviducts). Sterilization is permanent.

4.11 Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs): Transmission and Prevention

  • STIs spread through sexual contact. Common STIs in Ethiopia: Trichomoniasis, syphilis, gonorrhea, chancroid, HIV/AIDS (also chlamydia).

Trichomoniasis: caused by the flagellated protozoan Trichomonas vaginalis. Many infected people have no symptoms; some women have yellowish discharge, sore/itchy vagina; untreated infection can cause infertility. Cured with antiprotozoal drugs; both partners treated.

Chlamydia: caused by the small bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis. Often undetected in women; some men experience painful urination and penile discharge. Untreated → can scar the reproductive tract and cause infertility; can pass to newborn (pneumonia, conjunctivitis). Cured with antibiotics.

Gonorrhea: caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Men develop symptoms within a week (yellow pus, painful frequent urination); most women have no early symptoms; can cause sterility. Treated with antibiotics (resistant strains increasing); can harm joints, skin, heart, liver.

Syphilis: caused by the spiral-shaped bacterium Treponema pallidum. Enters through tiny cuts on genitals/cervix/vagina/oral cavity. Untreated → systemic; skin chancres appear; liver, bones, and eventually brain can be damaged. Treated with antibiotics.

HIV/AIDS:

  • HIV = Human Immunodeficiency Virus; kills immune cells (T-lymphocytes). AIDS = Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome.
  • Spread: through blood or body fluids — sexual contact, contaminated intravenous needles, blood transfusion, mother to fetus/through breast milk.
  • Symptoms: may not appear for up to 10 years; early symptoms include swollen lymph nodes, loss of appetite, weight loss, fever, rashes, night sweats, fatigue.
  • Prevention/control: abstinence; don't share needles; test blood before transfusion; condoms; test for HIV antibodies (a person with antibodies is HIV-positive); retroviral drugs slow the disease.

Control of spread of STIs:

  • Avoid sexual intercourse with an infected person (prostitutes, those with many partners, casual acquaintances).
  • Be faithful to one partner.
  • Condoms act as barriers.
  • Seek treatment at once; ensure partners also treated.
  • Bacterial STIs (syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia) can be treated with antibiotics; viral STIs (HIV) cannot be cured by antibiotics.

UNIT 5: HUMAN HEALTH, NUTRITION, AND DISEASE

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Biology Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

5.1 What is Food?

  • Food: any beneficial substance eaten, drunk, or otherwise taken into the body to sustain life, provide energy, promote growth, etc. Usually of plant, animal, or fungal origin and contains essential nutrients (carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, minerals).

Importance of food in living things:

  1. Growth: source of substances for making new cells, tissues, and organs.
  2. Energy: fuel for all biological activities (running, jumping, growing, reproducing).
  3. Replacement of damaged tissues: making new cells and repairing damaged ones.
  4. Protection from diseases: prevents deficiency diseases.

5.2 Nutrition

  • Nutrition: the process of taking in food and converting it into energy and other vital nutrients required for life.
  • Food provides nourishment for growth, repair of body cells, and maintenance of good health.

5.3 Nutrients

  • Nutrients: important chemical substances found in food needed for growth, energy, and health.
  • Five/six important classes: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids (fats and oils), vitamins, minerals, and water.

Macronutrients vs micronutrients:

  • Macronutrients (needed in large quantities): carbohydrates, proteins, fats.
  • Micronutrients (needed in small quantities): minerals and vitamins.
  • Vitamins and minerals don't supply energy but regulate metabolic activity and help utilize proteins/fats/carbohydrates; minerals are used to form body structures and skeleton.

Carbohydrates

  • Provide energy; composed of carbon, oxygen, hydrogen. Obtained from green plants (photosynthesis → starch).
  • Sources: potatoes, bread, maize, rice, other cereals; sugars (sucrose) in fruits and vegetables.
  • 1 gram of carbohydrate ≈ 16 kJ of energy.
  • Dietary fiber (roughage): indigestible carbohydrate (mainly cellulose from plant cell walls); not a source of energy but prevents constipation and keeps the colon healthy.
  • Three types:
  • Monosaccharide (simplest, hexose sugars, C₆H₁₂O₆): glucose, fructose, galactose.
  • Disaccharide (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁): maltose (glucose+glucose), sucrose (glucose+fructose), lactose (glucose+galactose).
  • Polysaccharide ((C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ): cellulose and starch in plants; glycogen in animals.

Fats and Oils

  • High-energy nutrients providing 35–45% of caloric intake; composed of carbon, oxygen, hydrogen.
  • 1 gram of lipid ≈ 37 kJ of energy (more than double carbohydrates).
  • Fats = animal source, solid at room temperature; oils = plant source, liquid at room temperature.
  • Sources: meat, milk, cheese, butter, egg yolk; oils from fruits, seeds (sunflower).
  • Functions: form fatty (adipose) tissue under the skin (reduces heat loss); vehicle for absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

Proteins

  • Provide growth and build new cells; made of amino acids composed of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen.
  • Structural part of cells is protein.
  • Sources: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese (animal); beans, chickpea, soy beans, nuts (plant).
  • 1 gram of protein ≈ 17 kJ of energy.

Vitamins

  • Essential in small quantities; organic substances needed for chemical reactions.
  • Plants make vitamins in leaves; humans/animals must obtain many ready-made.
  • Deficiency → vitamin-deficiency diseases.
  • Examples: vitamin A, vitamin E, thiamine (B1), riboflavin (B2), vitamin C, vitamin D.

Minerals

  • Inorganic substances essential in small quantities.
  • Calcium: builds teeth and bones, muscle contraction, nerve impulse transmission (sources: milk, cheese).
  • Iron: synthesizes hemoglobin of red blood cells (carries oxygen) (sources: red meat, liver, kidney, eggs, groundnuts, Tikur teff).
  • Iodine: makes the thyroid gland work properly (source: iodized salt, seafood).
  • Phosphorus: builds bones.

Water

  • Body fluids (blood, lymph, tissue fluid) are mainly water.
  • Acts as a solvent and transport medium; transports digested food to cells; excretes excess salt and urea.
✍️ Must-know: Energy values: carbohydrate 16 kJ/g, protein 17 kJ/g, lipid 37 kJ/g. Macronutrients = carbs, proteins, fats; micronutrients = vitamins, minerals.

5.4 Balanced Diets

  • Balanced diet: the diet that contains all of the nutrients in the correct amount and proportion. It must contain enough carbohydrates and fats for energy, enough protein of the right kind for growth, plus vitamins, mineral salts, plant fiber, and water.

Balanced diet depends on age, sex, activities, and lifestyle:

  • Pregnant woman: eats more food (development of baby); more calcium for bone/teeth.
  • Children: greater energy requirement than adults (growth); more protein (new cells).
  • Elderly people: generally lower energy and protein needs, but still need a balanced diet.
  • Women vs men: women have higher fat content and lower metabolic rate, so generally a lower energy requirement; men should eat relatively more energy food.
  • Active jobs/athletes: require high energy and high protein diets (extra protein for muscle development).

5.5 Deficiency Diseases

  • Deficiency disease: occurs when a person does not have enough of one particular nutrient and suffers health problems.
  • Examples: Kwashiorkor, Marasmus, Anemia, Rickets, Scurvy.

Marasmus: caused by inadequate carbohydrate (energy) intake, especially in children. Symptoms: thin arms/legs, little muscle, old-looking face, extremely thin with reduced fat and muscle, skin thin and hangs in folds. Treatment: energy-rich, balanced diet. (From Greek "decay.")

Kwashiorkor: caused by inadequate protein intake, especially in children. Symptoms: dry/flaky skin, swelling of legs and abdomen, changes in hair color, weakness, irritability. Preventable/curable with protein intake.

Anemia: caused by lack of iron; insufficient hemoglobin → less oxygen transport → less energy. Symptoms: feeling weak, tired, irritable. Treatable with iron capsules and iron-rich food.

Rickets: caused by shortage of vitamin D; results in deformed bones in the legs of children. Vitamin D is the only vitamin the body can make (skin exposure to morning/evening sunlight); also from oily fish, butter, milk, cheese, egg yolk. Vitamin D helps absorb calcium and phosphorus.

Scurvy: caused by lack of vitamin C (ascorbic acid); connective tissue fibers don't form properly. Symptoms: bleeding under the skin (especially at joints), bleeding gums, poor wound healing. Vitamin C can't be stored, so take it daily. Sources: oranges, lemons, grapefruit, tomatoes, fresh green vegetables.

5.6 Malnutrition

  • Malnutrition: the insufficient, excessive, or imbalanced consumption of nutrients, which leads to health problems.
  • Causes: poverty, famine (drought/flood), soil erosion, wars, too little land for too many people, ignorance of proper nutritional requirements.
  • Dual burden of malnutrition: undernutrition + overweight/obesity + diet-related non-communicable diseases.

Under nutrition (most common form — lack of protein and dietary energy):

  • Wasting (low weight-for-height): recent and severe weight loss; higher risk of death in children if untreated.
  • Stunting (low height-for-age): chronic/recurrent undernutrition; prevents reaching physical and cognitive potential.
  • Underweight (low weight-for-age).
  • Micronutrient deficiencies: lack of essential vitamins and minerals.

Over-nutrition / obesity:

  • When diet contains too much fat → coronary heart disease; fatty substances build up in arteries → blood clots → heart attack.
  • Obesity: excess food stored as fat. Caused by imbalance between energy stored and energy released (high-energy foods without equal physical activity, or decreased physical activity).
  • Health risks: heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, arthritis.
  • Controlled by: eating less high-energy food, more exercise, balanced diet with lower energy intake.

Body Mass Index (BMI):

  • BMI = body mass (kg) / height² (m²).
  • Status: below 18.5 = underweight; 18.5–24.9 = normal; 25.0–29.9 = overweight; 30.0 and above = obese.
  • Being 20% above the recommended weight also indicates obesity.
  • Developed by Adolphe Quetelet (Belgian). Note: muscular athletes may show high BMI but not be obese.
Note: Example: BMI of 80 kg, 1.85 m tall: BMI = 80/(1.85)² = 80/3.4225 ≈ 23.4 → normal weight (not obese).
✍️ Must-know: BMI = weight(kg)/height²(m²). <18.5 underweight; 18.5–24.9 normal; 25–29.9 overweight; ≥30 obese. Wasting = low weight-for-height; stunting = low height-for-age; underweight = low weight-for-age.

5.7 Substance Abuse

  • Drug: any substance taken into the body that changes/affects chemical reactions in the body. Can be medicinal (legal) or recreational (illegal).
  • Drugs present in food: tea, coffee, energy drinks (caffeine), tobacco (nicotine), alcoholic drinks (alcohol).
  • Substance abuse: using legal or illegal drugs/substances in the wrong way or in excessive amounts.

1. Cigarette Smoking

Cigarettes are made from tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum). Main components: tar, carbon monoxide, nicotine.

i. Tar — black sticky substance that collects in the lungs when smoke cools.

  • Chronic bronchitis: tar irritates bronchioles, stimulates excess mucus; cilia (which remove dust/mucus) are damaged; mucus/dirt/bacteria collect and block bronchioles; airways thicken and narrow → difficult to breathe → infections (pneumonia).
  • Lung cancer: tar contains carcinogens (cancer-producing compounds) that change epithelial cells → tumor.

Emphysema: weakened alveoli walls burst during coughing; absorbing surface of lungs reduced → cannot oxygenate blood properly → breathlessness. Bronchioles collapse, trapping air in alveoli.

ii. Carbon monoxide — poisonous gas.

  • Combines permanently with hemoglobin forming carboxy-hemoglobin; reduces oxygen in blood → less oxygen to heart → heart beats faster; may damage blood vessel linings → fatty build-up → coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke.

iii. Nicotine — stimulating, addictive compound.

  • Absorbed through alveoli into blood → brain; activates release of dopamine (associated with pleasure); long-term exposure depresses pleasure → addiction.
  • Increases heart rate and blood pressure; narrows arterioles; decreases blood flow; increases stickiness of blood platelets (promotes clotting).

2. Drinking Alcohol

  • Socially acceptable in many countries (Tella, Arekie/Katicala, Beer, Wine in Ethiopia).
  • Absorbed through the stomach and small intestine into blood; broken down by liver enzymes.
  • Standard drink (WHO) = 10 grams of pure ethanol; no more than two drinks/day recommended.
  • Alcohol is a depressant — slows nerve impulse transmission.
  • Larger quantities → loss of coordination, loss of self-control, loss of judgment, slower reaction times.
  • Alcoholics become dependent; develop tolerance (need more for same effect); can cause family pain, aggression, crime, marital breakdown, child neglect, absenteeism.
  • Long-term: stomach ulcers, heart disease, brain damage, cirrhosis (liver tissue replaced by scar tissue) — may lead to death.

3. Heroin

  • A powerful depressant; chemical structure similar to endorphins (brain chemicals that relieve pain/stress).
  • Binds to endorphin receptor sites, blocking nerve transmission → pain relief + addiction; body develops tolerance → need ever-greater quantities.
  • Often injected; sharing needles → infections such as HIV/AIDS.

4. Cannabis (marijuana/weed/pot)

  • Most commonly used illegal drug in the world; from the Cannabis plant.
  • Contains a psychoactive component called tetrahydrocannabinol (THC).
  • Effects: enjoyment, altered states of mind and sense of time, difficulty concentrating, reduced short-term memory, body relaxation, increased appetite. High amounts → nervousness, panic, false beliefs, hallucinations, suspicion, psychosis. Effects last 2–6 hours.

5. Chewing Khat

  • Khat (Catha edulis): bushy plant whose leaves are chewed for stimulant effect; grown in southern Arabia and Eastern Africa (Ethiopia, Somalia, Kenya).
  • Contains cathinone, which affects the central nervous system (CNS); found only in fresh leaves.
  • Effects: state of joy, increased alertness/stimulation; then depressed mood, irritability, loss of appetite, gastritis and peptic ulcer, difficulty sleeping; social problems like family fragmentation, multiple sexual practices, STI spread.

6. Doping

  • Doping: use of prohibited medications, drugs, or treatments in competitive sports to improve athletic performance (performance-enhancing drugs, PEDs).
  • WADA (World Anti-Doping Agency) uses blood and urine tests; key tool = biological passport program.
  • Health risks: cardiovascular (irregular heart rhythm, high blood pressure, heart attack, sudden death), CNS (insomnia, anxiety, depression, aggression, addiction), respiratory, hormonal (infertility, enlarged breasts, decreased testicular size, acromegaly, cancer).
  • Banned classes of drugs: street drugs, stimulants, anabolic steroids, peptide hormones (hGH), alcohol and beta-blockers (archery/rifle only), diuretics, beta-2 agonists, anti-estrogens, blood doping, gene manipulation.
  • Criteria for adding to the prohibited list (must meet any 2 of 3): potential to enhance performance; health risk; violates the Spirit of Sport.
  • Consequences of doping: physical/mental health damage, psychological effects, social consequences (loss of fame/respect), financial (loss of income/sponsorship), sporting (loss of results/medals), legal (suspension).

Problems of using drugs: eat more/less than normal, change friends, stop caring for self, sleep at unusual hours, lack of concern, problems at work/family, isolation, financial problems, strong desire for the substance.

Recovering from addiction: admit the problem; find support from others; rewrite daily routine; enjoy small successes; recognize and avoid relapse; reach freedom and stay there.

✍️ Must-know: Cigarette components: tar (bronchitis, lung cancer), carbon monoxide (binds hemoglobin → carboxy-hemoglobin, reduces O₂), nicotine (addictive stimulant). Alcohol = depressant. Heroin = depressant (like endorphins). Cannabis = THC. Khat = cathinone.

5.8 Types of Diseases

  • Disease: a condition in which the body does not function normally, producing symptoms (headache, fever, pain, weakness).

5.8.1 Infectious Diseases

  • Caused by disease-causing organisms (bacteria, virus, protozoa, fungi); transmitted from person to person (e.g., HIV-AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria).
  • Differentiated by: type of host, mode of transmission, type of causative organism, severity.

Epidemiological terms (note box):

  • Endemic: disease-causing agent exists permanently in a specific place/population (e.g., malaria is endemic in Afar region).
  • Epidemic: diseases that occur suddenly and spread in a specific area/population (e.g., Ebola epidemic in West Africa, 2015).
  • Pandemic: an epidemic that spreads far more widely throughout the whole world (e.g., COVID-19).

Transmission of infectious diseases: direct contact with a patient; drinking contaminated water/eating contaminated food; insect bite; sexual intercourse.

Prevention/control:

  1. Vaccination — major control measure.
  2. Personal hygiene — wash hands after using the toilet and before handling food.
  3. Hygienic food preparation — cover food, clean kitchen surfaces, cook thoroughly.
  4. Boiling/cooking water to kill pathogens.
  5. Proper waste disposal — covered bins, regular collection.
  6. Sewage treatment — water pipes far from toilet drainage.

HIV/AIDS

  • Caused by HIV; HIV infects and enters T-lymphocytes (immune cells that defend the body).
  • HIV replicates in and destroys T-lymphocytes → immune resistance declines → AIDS (result of opportunistic diseases like pneumonia, TB, cancers, weight loss, diarrhea).
  • Transmission: sexual intercourse (fluid transfer); blood transfusion; sharing needles in intravenous drug users; mother to fetus across placenta / during birth; mother to infant in breast milk.
  • Prevention: test blood before transfusion; sterile single-use needles; avoid multiple sexual partners; use condoms; HIV-positive mothers treated with retroviral drugs.

Tuberculosis (TB)

  • Caused by bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis (rarely M. bovis); bacteria live inside human cells, mainly in the lungs.
  • Symptoms: cough, chest pain, shortness of breath, fever, sweating, weight loss.
  • Transmission: airborne droplets (cough/sneeze) inhaled by uninfected people; also undercooked meat and unpasteurized milk (from infected animals).
  • Treatment: isolation during the infectious stage (2–4 weeks); several drugs over 6–9 months; drug-resistant TB needs second-line drugs.
  • Vaccine: BCG vaccine (from animal TB bacteria), protects 70–80%, given in early childhood.
  • Prevention: cover mouth/nose when coughing/sneezing, good ventilation, avoid overcrowding, avoid undercooked meat and unpasteurized milk.

Malaria

  • Caused by protozoa Plasmodium; transmitted by the bite of the female Anopheles mosquito (the vector).
  • Very common in tropical/subtropical regions with high humidity.
  • Life cycle: two hosts (human and mosquito); sexual reproduction in mosquito, asexual reproduction in human. The parasites enter red blood cells and multiply.
  • Prevention/control:
  1. Reduce mosquito population (remove breeding water, biological controls like fish that eat mosquito larvae, insecticides).
  2. Avoid mosquito bites (sleep under a mosquito net, use insect repellent).
  3. Use anti-malarial drugs (Artemisinin combination therapy/ACT, chloroquine); drug resistance is widespread (e.g., chloroquine resistance in Plasmodium falciparum).

COVID-19

  • Coronavirus, family Coronaviridae; causes disease in mammals and birds.
  • Structure: lipid envelope (lipid bilayer), membrane (M), envelope (E), and spike (S) proteins (S needed for interaction with host cells).
  • SARS (2003, China); MERS (2012, Middle East). COVID-19 virus (SARS-CoV-2) first emerged in China (Wuhan) in December 2019; WHO declared it a pandemic in March 2020.
  • Spread: primarily through droplets/particles released when an infected person breathes, talks, laughs, sings, coughs, or sneezes; also by touching contaminated surfaces.
  • Symptoms (2–14 days after exposure): fever, cough, tiredness, shortness of breath, muscle aches, chills, sore throat, headache, chest pain, loss of taste/smell.
  • Diagnosis: laboratory test of a fluid sample from nose or mouth.
  • Prevention: keep at least 6 feet (2 m) distance; avoid crowds and poor ventilation; wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds or use 60%+ alcohol sanitizer; wear a mask; cover mouth/nose when coughing; avoid touching eyes/nose/mouth; clean/disinfect surfaces.
  • Vaccines: e.g., AstraZeneca AZD1222, BioNTech BNT162.

5.8.2 Non-infectious Diseases

  • Caused by malnutrition, chemical effects, inherited/genetic factors — NOT by disease-causing organisms.
  • Examples: deficiency diseases (Kwashiorkor, Scurvy, night blindness, Marasmus), diseases from cigarette smoke (lung cancer, chronic bronchitis), sickle cell anemia, cancer, allergies, diabetes, podoconiosis.
  • Tobacco use, physical inactivity, harmful use of alcohol, and unhealthy diets increase the risk of dying from non-infectious diseases.
  • Unhealthy diets + lack of activity → raised blood pressure, increased blood glucose, elevated blood lipids, obesity → cardiovascular disease (leading non-infectious cause of premature death).
  • Detection, screening, treatment, and palliative care are key components of the response.

6.1 Ecology

6.1.1 Definitions of Common Ecological Terms

  • Ecology: the branch of biology that deals with the study of the interactions among organisms and with their environment.
  • Ecologists: scientists who study ecology.
  • Ecosystem: consists of the physical environment (abiotic factors) and all the living things (biotic factors) within it.
  • Habitat: the place in which each type of living thing lives.
  • Niche: the combination of the job an organism does and the place in which it lives.
  • Producers: plants and algae that trap sunlight energy and produce their own food.
  • Consumers: animals (cannot make their own food, obtain it from producers or other consumers).
  • Decomposers: bacteria and fungi; eat dead plant and animal remains and convert them into reusable substances.
  • Ecologists use both qualitative (observation) and quantitative (measurements, experiments) research; study organisms in the laboratory and field.

6.1.2 Biotic and Abiotic Components

Abiotic factors (non-living components that influence the distribution of organisms):

  • Energy: solar energy captured by chlorophyll during photosynthesis powers most ecosystems. Light can't penetrate deep in aquatic environments → most photosynthesis near the surface. Dark ecosystems are powered by bacteria deriving energy from oxidation of inorganic chemicals (e.g., hydrogen sulfide).
  • Temperature: affects metabolism. Few organisms are active near 0 °C; temperatures above 45 °C destroy the enzymes of most organisms. Most organisms function best within a specific temperature range.
  • Water: essential to all life. Terrestrial organisms face drying out (reptilian scales, waxy leaf coatings reduce water loss); aquatic organisms face water balance problems.
  • Nutrients: distribution of photosynthetic organisms depends on availability of inorganic nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus). Soil structure, pH, and nutrient content determine plant distribution.
  • Salinity, oxygen, currents, tides: important in aquatic ecosystems (oxygen dissolved in water is critical for fish; cold, fast-moving water has higher oxygen).
  • Wind: important on land; increases water loss by evaporation.
  • Natural disturbances: storms or fire play a role in distribution.

Biotic factors (living components that influence distribution):

  • Predators (kill prey) and herbivores (eat plants/algae).
  • Presence/absence of pollinators, food resources, parasites, pathogens, and competing organisms act as biotic limitations.
✍️ Must-know: Abiotic = non-living (energy, light, temperature, water, nutrients, salinity, wind); Biotic = living (predators, herbivores, parasites, decomposers, competitors).

6.1.3 Ecological Levels

Levels of ecological study: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere.

  • Population: a group of interbreeding organisms of the same species living in the same area at the same time. Members may compete for food, water, mates. Adaptations reduce competition (e.g., tadpoles eat algae, adult frogs eat insects).
  • Biological community: the different species within an area and the interactions within and among them (predation, parasitism, herbivory, competition, pollination). Changes in one population may affect others (more hawks → fewer mice).
  • Ecosystem: the community of organisms in a habitat plus the non-living part of the environment (air, water, soil, light). A lake, woodland, etc. are examples. An ecosystem is self-supporting.
  • Biosphere: the whole of that part of the Earth's surface which contains living organisms — may be regarded as one vast ecosystem. Depends on constant energy input from the Sun and recycling of chemical elements.

Sampling populations: a quadrat — marking off square areas within a habitat (by staking with sticks/string or a wood/plastic/metal square) — is used for immobile or slow-moving organisms; count individuals within boundaries.

6.1.4 Ecosystems

  • Types of ecosystems:
  • Terrestrial ecosystems: on land (forests, fields, a rotting log).
  • Aquatic ecosystems: freshwater (ponds, lakes, streams) and salt water (oceans = marine).

6.1.5 Biomes

  • Biome: a major terrestrial or aquatic life zone characterized by vegetation type (terrestrial) or the physical environment (aquatic).

Terrestrial Biomes:

  1. Tropical rain forests — between latitudes 10° N and S (equatorial Africa, East Indies, SE Asia, South America, Central America). Annual rainfall 130–200 cm; average temperature 25 °C; photosynthesis year-round. Plants are vertically layered; broad-leaf evergreen trees dominant. Highest animal diversity of any terrestrial biome. Threat: deforestation.
  1. Deserts — average < 10 cm rain/year; cover about one-fifth of Earth's land surface; many at about 30° N/S latitude (Atacama, Gobi). Hot deserts > 50 °C; cold deserts < −30 °C. Plants: succulents (cacti/euphorbs), deep-rooted shrubs, herbs. Plant adaptations: tolerance of heat/desiccation, water storage, reduced leaf surface area. Fauna: snakes, lizards, scorpions, ants, beetles, birds, seed-eating rodents (many nocturnal). Water conservation is a common adaptation.
  1. Savannas — occur in equatorial/sub-equatorial regions, between tropical forests and hot deserts (Africa, India, Australia). Precipitation 30–50 cm/year; dry season up to 8–9 months; temperature 24–29 °C year-round. Scattered trees; fire-adapted plants; grasses common; tolerate grazing. Herbivores: giraffes, zebras, elephants, antelopes, wildebeest herds; carnivores: lions, hyenas. Humans may have lived in savannas; fires and cattle ranching affect them.
  1. Temperate grasslands — warm summers, cold winters; annual rainfall 25–100 cm. Grass roots hold topsoil in place. North American grasslands = shortgrass and tallgrass prairies. Once supported elk, pronghorn, bison (prey to wolves).
  1. Boreal forests (taiga / coniferous forest) — south of the Arctic Circle (Canada, Alaska, Russia, N Europe). Cold, dry winters; short, cool, wet summers; precipitation 40–100 cm (mostly snow). Cold-tolerant coniferous plants (pines, spruce) with needle-shaped leaves. Soils acidic with little nitrogen; lower species diversity.
  1. Temperate broad-leaf forest — at mid-latitudes in the Northern Hemisphere (also Chile, S Africa, Australia, NZ). Precipitation 70–200+ cm/year; winters ~0 °C, summers up to 35 °C. Dominant plants = deciduous trees (drop leaves before winter). Many mammals hibernate; birds migrate. Heavily settled/logged but recovering.
  1. Tundras — cover about 20% of Earth's land surface (Arctic; alpine tundra on high mountains). Precipitation 20–60 cm/year (arctic). Winters below −30 °C; summer < 10 °C. Vegetation: herbaceous (mosses, grasses, forbs), dwarf shrubs, lichens. Permafrost (permanently frozen soil) restricts root growth. Grazers: musk oxen (resident), caribou/reindeer (migratory); predators: bears, wolves, foxes. Major human impact: mineral and oil extraction.

Aquatic Biomes:

  • Occupy roughly 75% of Earth's surface; determined by salinity and other physical factors.
  • Freshwater biomes (lakes, streams/rivers, wetlands): salt concentration typically less than 1%.
  • Marine biomes (oceans, intertidal zones, coral reefs): salt concentrations generally around 3%.
  1. Lakes — standing bodies of water (ponds to lakes covering thousands of km²). Rooted/floating aquatic plants near shore; phytoplankton (cyanobacteria) and zooplankton in open water; fishes in zones with oxygen. Nutrient enrichment (runoff, waste) → algal "bloom," oxygen depletion, fish kills.
  1. Wetlands — soil permanently or periodically saturated with water; shallow bodies of water. Types: marshes, swamps, bogs, mudflats, salt marshes. High organic production; water and soils periodic low in dissolved oxygen. High capacity to filter dissolved nutrients and chemical pollutants; among the most productive biomes. Swamps dominated by woody plants; bogs by sphagnum mosses. Draining/filling has destroyed up to 90% of wetlands in Europe.
  1. Streams and rivers — speed and volume of flow are key. Headwater streams: cold, clear, swift, turbulent; downstream rivers: warmer, more turbid (suspended sediment). Salt/nutrient content increases from headwaters to mouth; headwaters rich in oxygen. Pollution, damming, and flood control degrade them (threaten migratory species like salmon).
  1. Estuaries — a transition area between river and sea; seawater flows up/down with tides; salinity varies. Nutrients from the river make estuaries among the most productive biomes. Producers: saltmarsh grasses, algae, phytoplankton. Support worms, oysters, crabs, many fish; breeding grounds for marine life; crucial feeding areas for waterfowl. Filling, dredging, and pollution disrupt them.

Ocean zone terms (note box): Photic zone = zone of sufficient light for photosynthesis; Aphotic zone = little light penetration; Pelagic zone = photic + aphotic together; Abyssal zone = the part of the ocean 2000–6000 m below the surface.

6.1.6 Ecological Succession

  • Succession: the orderly, natural changes that take place in the communities of an ecosystem.
  • Primary succession: begins in a virtually lifeless area (new volcanic island, rubble/moraine left by a retreating glacier). Only life-forms initially present are prokaryotes and protists; lichens and mosses are commonly the first macroscopic photosynthesizers (pioneer species); soil develops gradually; then grasses, shrubs, trees; eventually a stable climax community. Takes hundreds or thousands of years.
  • Secondary succession: the re-colonization of an area after a major disturbance that removed most but not all organisms (e.g., a forest cleared for farming then abandoned). Early plants are herbaceous species; then woody shrubs; then forest trees may replace shrubs.
  • Early- and later-arriving species linked by facilitating, inhibiting, or tolerating processes.

Key terms: Pioneer species = first organisms to grow on new patch; Climax community = a stable community that undergoes little or no change; Limiting factor = any biotic or abiotic factor that restricts existence, numbers, reproduction, or distribution; Tolerance = ability of an organism to withstand changes.

6.2 Ecological Relationships

Inter-specific interactions in a community include: competition, predation, herbivory, parasitism, mutualism, commensalism.

  1. Competition (−/−): occurs when individuals of different/same species each use a resource that limits the survival and reproduction of both (weeds competing with garden plants; predators competing for prey).
  2. Predation (+/−): an individual of one species (the predator) kills and eats an individual of another species (the prey). (e.g., a rotifer eating a protist.) Adaptations of both refined through natural selection.
  3. Herbivory (+/−): an exploitative interaction in which an organism (a herbivore) eats parts of a plant or alga, harming it but usually not killing it (cattle, sheep, giraffe, goat; most herbivores are invertebrates — grasshoppers, caterpillars, beetles).
  4. Parasitism (+/−): one organism (the parasite) derives nourishment from another (the host), which is harmed. Endoparasites live inside (parasitic roundworms); ectoparasites feed while attached to the outside (a tick). Complex life cycles may involve multiple hosts (blood fluke needs humans + freshwater snails).
  5. Mutualism (+/+): interaction that benefits both interacting species (cellulose digestion by microorganisms in termites/ruminants; animals that pollinate flowers or disperse seeds; mycorrhizae nutrient exchange; photosynthesis by unicellular algae in corals; acacia-ant).
  6. Commensalism (+/0): one species benefits while the other is neither helped nor harmed (cattle egrets feeding on insects flushed by grazing bison/cattle — birds benefit; herbivores mostly unaffected).
✍️ Must-know: Competition (−/−), Predation (+/−), Herbivory (+/−), Parasitism (+/−), Mutualism (+/+), Commensalism (+/0).
