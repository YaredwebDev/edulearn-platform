1.1 Definition and Scope of Chemistry

1.1.1 Definition of Chemistry

Chemistry is the science that deals with the properties, composition, and structure of substances (elements and compounds), the transformations they undergo, and the energy that is released or absorbed during these processes.

Key terms:

  • Substance: a particular kind of matter with uniform properties (e.g., gold, silver, water, soap, table salt).
  • Matter: a physical substance that occupies space and possesses rest mass (e.g., book, pencil, television).
  • Property: the attribute, quality, or characteristic by which a substance is distinguished from others.
  • Composition: the nature of something's ingredients/constituents; how a whole or mixture is made up (e.g., table salt = sodium + chlorine).
  • Structure: the arrangement and relationships between the parts of something complex (e.g., a building's parts arranged in order).
  • Transformation: a marked change in form, nature, or appearance, accompanied by energy changes.

1.1.2 Scope of Chemistry

The study of modern chemistry has five main disciplines:

BranchWhat it studies
Physical chemistryMacroscopic and atomic properties; rates of reactions, energy transfers, molecular structure.
Organic chemistrySubstances containing carbon (over 20 million carbon-based chemicals).
Inorganic chemistrySubstances not primarily based on carbon (rocks, minerals; energy & IT materials).
Analytical chemistryThe composition of matter — separating, identifying, and quantifying components.
BiochemistryChemical processes occurring in living things (cellular processes, disease states).

The scope of chemistry extends to agriculture, medicine, food production, and building construction, explaining the natural world, preparing people for careers, and producing informed citizens. Chemistry can also produce dangerous substances (e.g., chlorofluorocarbons, oxides of nitrogen, carbon, and sulphur) that harm life and the environment.

✍️ Must-know: The five branches of chemistry are physical, organic, inorganic, analytical, and biochemistry. Chemistry = central science.

1.2 The Relationship Between Chemistry and Other Natural Sciences

Chemistry is one branch of science and is called the "central science" because it links all fields that involve matter.

  • Biology (study of living things) + Chemistry = Biochemistry (chemical processes in living matter).
  • Geology (study of rocks/earth) + Chemistry = Geochemistry (processes controlling abundance, composition, and distribution of chemical compounds and isotopes in geologic environments).
  • Physics (nature and properties of matter and energy) + Chemistry = Physical chemistry and Chemical physics.
  • Medicine + Chemistry = Medicinal chemistry (design, development, and synthesis of pharmaceutical drugs).
✍️ Must-know: Chemistry is the "central science." Overlaps: Biochemistry, Geochemistry, Physical/Chemical physics, Medicinal chemistry.

1.3 The Role Chemistry Plays in Production and in the Society

A. Agriculture: Chemical fertilizers (calcium superphosphate, urea, ammonium sulphate, sodium nitrate) increase crop yield. Pesticides (fungicides, herbicides, insecticides) reduce crop damage. Better-quality plastic pipes for irrigation.

B. Food Production: Food preservatives preserve food longer; methods to test for adulterants ensure pure foodstuff; new technologies improve availability, appearance, nutritional content, and flavor.

C. Medicine: Chemistry provides life-saving medicines. Discovered drugs:

  • Sulphur drugs and penicillin → cure for dysentery and pneumonia.
  • Cisplatin and Taxol → cancer therapy.
  • AZT → fights multiplication of the HIV virus (does not cure, prolongs life).

Classes of drugs:

  • Disinfectants — kill microbes in toilets, floors, drains (e.g., Covid-19 sanitizers).
  • Analgesics — painkillers (relief from pain).
  • Anesthetics — relieve pain for effective medical operations.
  • Antibiotics — control infection and cure diseases.
  • Antiseptics — prevent contamination of wounds by bacteria.
  • Tranquilizers — reduce tension; bring calm to patients with mental diseases.

D. Building Construction: Chemistry provides glass, steel, and cement for safer houses, multi-story structures, dams, and bridges. Example: the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) — a 6,450 MW hydro power project on the Blue Nile in Benishangul-Gumuz, ~30 km upstream of the Sudan border; the largest hydro power project in Africa.

✍️ Must-know: Chemistry aids agriculture, food, medicine, and construction. Drugs: analgesics (pain), anesthetics (operations), antibiotics (infection), antiseptics (wounds), disinfectants (surfaces), tranquilizers (mental calm).

2.1.2 Measurements of Mass, Volume, and Density

  • Mass: amount of matter in an object (SI unit kg).
  • Volume: the amount of space occupied; 1 L = 1 dm³ and 1 mL = 1 cm³.

2.1.4 Measurement Uncertainty

  • Uncertainty: the doubt that exists about the result of every measurement (no measurement is perfect).
  • Systematic errors: caused by incorrect technique or faulty instruments; reproducible but wrong; not reduced by taking more readings (bias).
  • Random errors: caused by the operator or fluctuations; scatter readings; reduced by averaging.

Reporting uncertainty (three approaches):

  1. ± half of the range (e.g., 66, 65, 61 mm → mean 64, range 5, so 64 ± 2.5 mm).
  2. ± the furthest reading from the mean (61 is furthest: 3 away → 64 ± 3 mm).
  3. ± resolution of the instrument (±0.5 of the smallest division).
  • Percent uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty / measurement) × 100.
  • Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other (reproducibility).
  • Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true/accepted value.
  • A measurement can be precise but inaccurate, or accurate but imprecise.
Note: Example 2.4 (archery target): Precision = tight grouping (arrows clustered); accuracy = hitting the bull's-eye (true value).

2.1.5 Significant Figures

Rules for counting significant figures:

  1. Non-zero digits are always significant (e.g., 394 cm has 3).
  2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (e.g., 5.03 g has 3).
  3. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant (e.g., 0.714 m has 3; 2.720×10²² has 4).
  4. Leading zeros (before the first non-zero digit) are NOT significant — they only place the decimal (e.g., 0.052 kg has 2).
  5. Zeros without a decimal point are ambiguous (e.g., 3000 mL — unclear; write in scientific notation).

Rounding rule: if the digit is < 5, drop it; if ≥ 5, increase the preceding digit by 1.

Calculations:

  • Addition/Subtraction: round to the fewest number of decimal places.
  • Multiplication/Division: round to the fewest number of significant figures.

Scientific notation: N × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ N < 10 (e.g., 6.02×10²³; 3.27×10⁻²²).

Note: Example 2.5 (sig figs): 394 cm = 3; 5.03 g = 3; 0.714 m = 3; 0.052 kg = 2; 2.720×10²² = 4; 3000 mL = ambiguous.
Note: Example 2.6 (unit conversion): 1.10 Å = 1.10×10⁻⁸ cm = 1.10×10⁻¹ nm.

2.2 Chemistry as an Experimental Science

2.2.1 Scientific Method

Steps in the scientific method:

  1. Observation and asking a question.
  2. Data collection and forming a hypothesis (a tentative explanation).
  3. Testing the hypothesis through experiments.
  4. Analysis of results and drawing a conclusion.
  • A hypothesis that is confirmed by repeated experiments may be promoted to a theory (a well-tested explanation).

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Chemistry Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

3.1 Historical Development of the Atomic Theories of Matter

  • The concept of the atom dates to early Indian and Greek philosophers (~400 B.C.). The word atom comes from the Greek 'a-tomos' meaning "uncuttable" / "non-divisible."
  • A scientific theory: a well-tested, broad explanation of a natural phenomenon (NOT just an educated guess).

The Indivisible Atom (Greek philosophers):

  • Empedocles (mid-5th century BC): all materials made of four elements — earth, air, water, fire.
  • Plato: adopted Empedocles' theory, coined the term "element."
  • Aristotle: adopted the four elements; differentiated them by hot/cold, wet/dry. The Greek concept lasted 2,000+ years.
  • Leucippus (c. 440 BC): originated the atom concept.
  • Democritus (460–370 BC): taught atomism — indivisible, indestructible atoms are the basic components of all matter.

Five major points of the Greek atomic idea:

  1. All matter is composed of atoms (too small to be seen, cannot be further split).
  2. There is a void — empty space between atoms.
  3. Atoms are completely solid.
  4. Atoms are homogeneous, with no internal structure.
  5. Atoms differ in size, shape, and weight.
  • The Greek atomic model = the "solid sphere."
  • Aristotle and others opposed atomism, so it was buried until the 16th–17th centuries.
  • Lavoisier (18th century) proved that in burning there is no net gain or loss of weight, establishing modern chemistry.

Drawback of Greek philosophers: they relied on reason and logic, not experiment and observation ("all thought, no action").

3.2 Fundamental Laws of Chemical Reactions

  • Law (scientific law): a generalized statement, based on repeated experiments/observations, that describes or predicts natural phenomena. It does not explain why (that's a theory).

3.2.1 The Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Discovered by Antoine Lavoisier (1789). Also called the "Law of Indestructibility of Matter."
  • Statement: In any closed system, mass is neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions. The total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
Note: Example: Reactant 1 + Reactant 2 → Product 1 + Product 2 (mass reactants = mass products).
Note: Problem: 224.4 g KOH + 88.0 g CO₂ → K₂CO₃ + 36.0 g H₂O. Mass of K₂CO₃ = (224.4 + 88.0) − 36.0 = 276.4 g.

Experiments: AgNO₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + HNO₃(l); Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq).

3.2.2 The Law of Definite Proportions (Proust's Law / Law of Constant Composition)

  • Proposed by Joseph Louis Proust.
  • Statement: every chemical compound contains fixed and constant proportions (by mass) of its constituent elements.
Note: Example: H₂O always has H:O by mass = 1:8 (2 g H : 16 g O), whatever the amount of water.
Note: CO₂ has C:O by mass = 12:32 = 3:8.

Molecular weight = sum of atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule (H=1, C=12, N=14, O=16).

  • e.g., NH₄⁺ = 14 + (1×4) = 18.

Mole: amount of substance containing as many elementary entities as atoms in 0.012 kg of carbon-12. Symbol "mol."

  • Number of moles = given mass / molecular mass. (2 g NH₄⁺ = 2/18 = 0.11 mol)

3.2.3 The Law of Multiple Proportions

  • Statement: when two different compounds form from the same two elements, the masses of one element reacting with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers.
Note: Example (CO & CO₂): 1.33 g O in CO, 2.66 g O in CO₂ per 1.00 g C. Ratio 1.33 : 2.66 = 1 : 2.
Note: Example (nitrogen oxides): 2.62 g N and 0.656 g N each combine with 1.50 g O → ratio 2.62 : 0.656 = 4 : 1 (small whole numbers).

3.3 Atomic Theory

3.3.1 Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803/1808)

John Dalton (English chemist) proposed the theory based on experiments/observations. Five tenets:

  1. Elements are made of small particles called atoms.
  2. Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed.
  3. All atoms of the same element are identical (same mass and size).
  4. Atoms of different elements have different masses and sizes.
  5. Atoms combine in small whole numbers to form compounds.
  • Dalton also proposed symbols for elements (hard to remember/draw). Berzelius later suggested using alphabets as symbols.

3.3.2 Modern Atomic Theory

Drawbacks of Dalton's theory:

  • Atoms are divisible into protons, neutrons, electrons (though atoms are the smallest particle that takes part in chemical reactions).
  • Atoms of the same element can differ in mass → isotopes (e.g., Cl-35, Cl-37).
  • Atoms of different elements can have the same atomic mass → isobars (e.g., Ar-40 and Ca-40).
  • Atoms don't always combine in simple whole-number ratios (e.g., complex organic compounds like sugar C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁).

Modern Atomic Theory postulates:

  1. Elements are made of atoms.
  2. Atoms cannot be created or destroyed during ordinary chemical reactions.
  3. All atoms of the same element have the same atomic number but may vary in mass number (isotopes).
  4. Atoms of different elements are different.
  5. Atoms combine in small whole numbers to form compounds.

Timeline of discoveries:

  • 1886 Goldstein predicted proton (anode/canal ray experiment).
  • 1897 J.J. Thomson discovered the electron (charge-to-mass ratio).
  • 1904 Thomson: "plum pudding" model.
  • 1909 Robert Millikan: charge and mass of the electron.
  • 1911 Rutherford (+ Geiger & Marsden): planetary model.
  • 1913 Niels Bohr: electrons in concentric circular orbits.
  • 1920 Rutherford: discovered proton in the nucleus.
  • 1932 James Chadwick: discovered the neutron.

3.4 Discoveries of the Fundamental Subatomic Particles and the Atomic Nucleus

3.4.1 Discovery of the Proton

  • Goldstein (1886) predicted positive particles from the electrical neutrality of the atom, using a perforated cathode discharge tube.
  • Rays passing through the cathode perforations = anode rays / canal rays (streams of positive ions).

Properties of anode rays:

  1. Travel in straight lines.
  2. Consist of material particles.
  3. Deflected opposite to cathode rays in electric/magnetic fields.
  4. e/m ratio depends on the gas in the tube.
  5. Simply positively charged gaseous ions.
  • With hydrogen, canal-ray particles were the lightest with the highest e/m ratio; Rutherford showed they were identical to the hydrogen ion → named protons (present in all matter).

3.4.2 Discovery of the Electron

  • Heinrich Geissler (1855) made the first good vacuum tubes; Sir William Crookes designed the Crooke's Discharge Tube / Cathode Ray Tube.
  • The tube has two electrodes: anode (+), cathode (−). At low pressure (~0.0001 mm Hg) and high voltage (10,000–20,000 V), cathode rays produce a greenish glow.

J.J. Thomson's experiments (1897):

  1. Shadow experiment: placing an object between cathode and anode cast a sharp shadow → cathode rays travel in straight lines.
  2. Paddle-wheel experiment: cathode rays rotated a light paddle wheel → rays are material particles with mass and kinetic energy.
  3. Electric-field experiment: rays bent toward the positive plate → rays are negatively charged.
  4. Magnetic-field experiment: rays deflected perpendicular to the field → confirmed negative charge.
  • Thomson measured the mass-to-charge ratio = 1.76 × 10⁸ C/g; named the particles "corpuscles", later called electrons.
  • Cathode ray properties are independent of the gas and electrodes used.

Summary of cathode ray properties: straight lines, cast shadows, move paddle wheels (kinetic energy/material particles), bend toward positive plate, affected by magnetic fields, produce fluorescence on ZnS, ionize gases, penetrate thin metal foils, produce X-rays.

Thomson's Atomic Model (plum pudding, 1904): electrons embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge ("blueberries in a muffin"). Local analogy: watermelon (seeds = electrons, red pulp = positive charge). Could explain electrical neutrality but failed to explain how positives and negatives stay separated.

Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment (1909): measured the smallest charge on oil drops as 1.59 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (the charge of one electron). Using Thomson's e/m ratio:

  • Mass of electron = e / (e/m) = (1.59 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) / (1.76 × 10⁸ C/g) = 9 × 10⁻²⁸ g.

3.4.3 Discovery of the Nucleus

  • Rutherford predicted neutral particles to explain why carbon's mass is ~12× hydrogen despite only 6 protons.
  • Rutherford's α-particle (gold foil) experiment (1920): fired α-particles (2 protons + 2 neutrons) at thin gold foil.
  • Expected (if Thomson's model correct): pass straight through.
  • Observed: most pass through, but a small fraction deflected at large angles and some reflected back.
  • Conclusion: mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus; the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Rutherford's atomic model (Planetary/Nuclear model): protons and neutrons (most mass) in the central nucleus; electrons dispersed around, occupying most of the volume. Nucleus diameter ~10⁻¹³ cm vs atom ~10⁻⁸ cm (10⁵× smaller). If an atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be about a cat's eye.
  • Drawbacks: (1) inconsistent with classical electrodynamics — an orbiting charged particle should lose energy and collapse; (2) failed to explain atomic spectra.
  • Rutherford won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1908.

Bohr's Atomic Model (1913): based on Planck's Quantum Theory. Five principles:

  1. Electrons arranged in concentric circular orbits around the nucleus.
  2. Electrons occupy only certain stable ("stationary") orbits or shells.
  3. Each shell has a definite fixed energy → called energy levels (K, L, M, N).
  4. Energy increases with distance: E₁ < E₂ < E₃.
  5. As long as the electron revolves in a particular orbit, it does not lose energy.

3.4.4 Discovery of the Neutron

  • Heavier elements had larger atomic mass than their atomic number suggested → predicted additional neutral particles.
  • 1932, James Chadwick bombarded beryllium with α-particles:
  • A paddle wheel behind the beryllium rotated → invisible material radiation.
  • The radiation was undeflected in an electric field → neutral particles.
  • Named these neutral particles neutrons.
  • Neutrons being uncharged can enter nuclei undeflected → new tool for atomic disintegration; led to the nuclear model (protons + neutrons in nucleus).

3.5.3 Atomic Mass and Isotopes

  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element (same protons) with different numbers of neutrons.
  • Same physical/chemical properties (same protons and electrons).
  • Radioisotopes: unstable isotopes that undergo radioactive decay.
  • Hydrogen isotopes: protium (A=1), deuterium (A=2), tritium (A=3).
  • Carbon: carbon-12 and carbon-13 stable; carbon-14 is a radioisotope (used in dating).
  • Atomic mass unit (amu): 1/12 the mass of carbon-12 = 1.660539040×10⁻²⁴ g. Also called the Dalton (Da).
  • Average atomic mass: a weighted average of isotope masses.
Note: Average Atomic Mass = [(% isotope 1)(mass 1) + (% isotope 2)(mass 2) + ...] ÷ 100

Note: - Chlorine: (35×75 + 37×25)÷100 = 35.5 amu.

3.5.4 Main Energy Levels

  • Bohr (1913) proposed shells/orbits. The principal (main) energy level is the shell in which an electron is located, denoted by the principal quantum number n (1, 2, 3, 4 ... or K, L, M, N).
  • Energy increases with distance: K < L < M < N (1 < 2 < 3 < 4).

3.5.5 Electronic Configuration on Main Shells

  • Maximum electrons per shell = 2n²: K = 2, L = 8, M = 18, N = 32.
  • Electronic configuration: the systematic arrangement of electrons in the shells of an atom.
  • Fill from the lowest energy shell (K) outward.
  • Valence shell: the outermost shell with electrons.
  • Penultimate shell: the shell just inside the valence shell.
  • Anti-penultimate shell: the shell inside the penultimate shell.
  • Max electrons: valence = 8, penultimate = 18, anti-penultimate = 32.
Note: Examples:
Note: - Calcium (Z=20): 2, 8, 8, 2 (valence shell cannot exceed 8).
Note: - Argon (Z=18): 2, 8, 8.

3.5.6 Valence Electrons

  • Valence electrons: the electrons occupying the outermost shell of an atom.
  • They are furthest from the nucleus, most easily lost, and determine the element's chemical properties and how it reacts.
  • Atoms lose, gain, or share valence electrons during chemical reactions.
Note: Example: Silicon (2, 8, 4) → 4 valence electrons in the M shell. Argon (2, 8, 8) → 8 valence electrons.

4.1 Historical Development of Periodic Classification of the Elements

  • In the 19th century, chemists didn't know of electrons/protons, so they used atomic masses to arrange elements, feeling that chemical behaviour relates to atomic mass.
  • Periodicity: the repeating pattern of properties of elements when arranged in order of increasing atomic number (or mass). Analogy used in the text: a calendar (repeating days/weeks) and the octave in music.

4.1.1 Dobereiner's Triads (1829)

  • Dobereiner grouped elements into triads — sets of three elements with similar physical/chemical properties.
  • The atomic weight of the middle element is the arithmetic mean of the other two.

4.1.2 Newlands' Law of Octaves

  • Newlands arranged the 56 known elements in increasing atomic weight.
  • Observation: the properties of every eighth element are similar to the first (like the octave in music). Called Newlands' law of octaves.
  • Limitations: (1) inert gases not yet discovered; (2) beyond calcium, the repetition was not observed.

4.2 Mendeleev's Classification of the Elements

  • The earliest modern periodic table was presented simultaneously by Dmitri Mendeleev (Russia) and Lothar Meyer (Germany), arranging elements by increasing atomic weight and observing that elements with similar properties appear at regular intervals.

4.2.1 Mendeleev's Periodic Law

Note: "The physical and chemical properties of elements are the periodic function of their atomic weights."

Features of Mendeleev's table (1871):

  1. Contained only 63 elements (inert gases not yet discovered).
  2. Arranged in 7 horizontal rows (periods) and 8 vertical columns (groups).
  3. Vacant sites were left for undiscovered elements, and their properties were predicted (later verified).

Modifications after discovery of inert gases:

  • Inert gases placed in a new Group 0.
  • Each long period divided into even and odd series.
  • Groups I–VII split into subgroups A and B.
  • Group 0 not split; Group VIII had three sets of three elements.

Period names (rows): period 1 = very short (2); period 2 = first short (8); period 3 = second short (8); period 4 = first long (18); period 5 = second long (18); periods 6 and 7 = very long (32 each).

Group names (columns):

  • Group IA = alkali metals (except H).
  • Group IIA = alkaline earth metals.
  • Group VB = pnictogens.
  • Group VIB = chalcogens.
  • Group VIIB = halogens.
  • Group 0 = noble (inert) gases.

Merits: (1) systematic, easier study of elements; (2) vacant positions guided discovery of new elements.

Demerits: some elements wrongly placed (atomic weights out of order):

  • Ar (40) before K (39); Te (127.6) before I (126.9); Co (58.9) before Ni (58.6).

4.3 The Modern Periodic Table

4.3.1 The Periodic Law

Note: "The physical and chemical properties of the elements are the periodic function of their atomic number." (Modern law — based on Moseley's work.)
  • The long form of the periodic table is based on the modern periodic law.
  • Left and right corners = subgroups A; middle = subgroups B.

4.3.2 Groups and Periods

Periods (horizontal rows):

  • Elements arranged in increasing atomic number left to right.
  • 7 periods (Arabic numerals 1–7).
  • Elements in the same period have the same number of shells (period number = number of shells).
  • Periods 1, 2, 3 = short periods; 4, 5, 6 = long periods.
  • Period 1 = 2 elements (H, He); Periods 2 & 3 = 8 elements each; Periods 4 & 5 = 18 each; Period 6 = 32; Period 7 = 32 (radioactive/artificial).
  • Except period 1, all periods start with an alkali metal and end with a noble gas.

  • F (Z=9) = 1s²2s²2p⁵ (2, 7); Cl (Z=17) = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁵ (2, 8, 7). Both have 7 valence electrons; F has 2 shells, Cl has 3.

Groups (vertical columns):

  • 18 columns/groups in the modern periodic table (I–VIII with A or B).
  • Main groups (A): IA–VIIIA. Subgroups (B): IB–VIIIB.
  • Elements in the same group have the same number of outermost (valence) electrons and similar chemical properties.
  • For main-group elements, group number = number of valence electrons. (Cl has 7 valence electrons → Group VIIA.)

4.3.3 Classification of the Elements (s, p, d, f blocks)

  • Based on the sublevel into which the last electron enters:
  1. s-block: last electron enters an s-orbital; valence config ns¹⁻² ([IG]ns¹ for alkali, [IG]ns² for alkaline earth).
  2. p-block: last electron enters a p-orbital; valence config ns²np¹⁻⁶; Groups IIIA–VIIIA.
  • s-block valence electrons = electrons in highest s orbital.
  • p-block valence electrons = electrons in highest s and p orbitals.
  • Total valence electrons = group number (A & B convention).
  • He (ns²) — technically s-block, but by chemical behaviour placed in Group VIII.
  1. d-block (transition metals): last electron enters a d-orbital; valence config ns⁰⁻²(n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ (except palladium).
  • Total valence electrons = outermost (ns) + penultimate [(n−1)d].
  1. f-block (inner transition elements): last electron enters an f-orbital; config ns²(n−1)d⁰⁻¹(n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴.
  • Total valence electrons = ns + (n−1)d + (n−2)f.

4.3.4 The Representative (Main Group) Elements

  • Representative/main-group elements: Groups IA–VIIA, with incompletely filled s or p subshells of the highest principal quantum number.
  • Noble gases (VIIIA): completely filled p subshell (except He = 1s²; others ns²np⁶).
  • Traditional group names: Group I = alkali metals, II = alkaline earth metals, III = boron family, IV = carbon family, V = nitrogen family, VI = oxygen family, VII = halogens, VIII = noble gases.
  • Group IA: noble gas core + ns¹; Group IIA: noble gas core + ns²; halogens: ns²np⁵.
  • Same number of valence electrons → similar chemical behaviour.
  • Caution (Group IVA): same outer config (ns²np⁴), but properties vary: C = nonmetal, Si/Ge = metalloids, Sn/Pb = metals.

4.4 The Major Trends in the Periodic Table

Periodic trends: patterns in electronegativity, ionization energy, electron affinity, atomic radius, and metallic character — caused by the periodic nature of elements.

4.4.1 Atomic Radius

  • Atomic radius: one-half the distance between the nuclei of two adjacent atoms (network solids or diatomic molecules).
  • Determined largely by the strength of attraction between the nucleus and outer-shell electrons.
  • Trend down a group: atomic radius increases (more shells, higher principal quantum number, e.g., Li → Cs).
  • Trend across a period: atomic radius decreases left → right (increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer).
Note: Example 4.4: Arrange P, Si, N in increasing atomic radius.
Note: Same group: N < P (going down increases). Same period: P < Si (going right decreases). So N < P < Si.

4.4.2 Ionization Energy

  • Ionization energy (IE): the energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state to form a cation (kJ/mol). H(g) → H⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Successive electrons require more energy (atom becomes more positive).
  • Larger radius → lower ionization energy (electrons easier to remove).
  • Trend: IE increases left → right across a period; decreases down a group.
  • Exceptions: Group IIA > IIIA and Group VA > VIA (stable fully/half-filled configurations).
  • Shielding effect: inner electrons partially cancel the nuclear charge felt by outer electrons → lower IE. Higher shielding → lower IE. This explains why IE decreases down a group.
  • Cesium has the lowest IE; Fluorine the highest (except He and Ne).

4.4.3 Electron Affinity

  • Electron affinity (EA): the change in energy when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom to form a negative ion (kJ/mol). X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
  • First EA: energy released → negative value (e.g., Cl = −349 kJ/mol).
  • Second EA: positive (energy needed exceeds energy gained).
  • Metals have lower electron affinities (easier to lose electrons). Nonmetals have higher EA (form stable octets).
  • Trend: EA increases left → right across a period; decreases down a group.
  • Irregularity (F vs Cl): EA of fluorine (−328) is less than chlorine (−349) because fluorine's 2p subshell is small → greater electron–electron repulsion.
  • Group IA values (kJ/mol): Li −60, Na −53, K −48, Rb −47, Cs −45.
  • Halogens (kJ/mol): F −328, Cl −349, Br −324, I −295.
  • Exceptions: Be and N have EA = 0 (stable configurations).
  • Noble gases have extremely low (near zero) electron affinity.

4.4.4 Electronegativity

  • Electronegativity (EN): the tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared pair of bonding electrons toward itself. A dimensionless tendency (not a measured energy).
  • Measured on the Pauling scale.
  • Fluorine = most electronegative (4.0); Cesium/Francium = least (0.7).
  • Trend: EN increases left → right across a period (increasing nuclear charge, smaller size); decreases down a group (added shells overcome nuclear charge).
  • Metals have EN < 2.0 and are electropositive; nonmetals are electronegative.
  • Diagonal relationship: elements in period 2 resemble the next group in period 3 (small size difference in electronegativity, e.g., Li resembles Mg).

UNIT 5: CHEMICAL BONDING

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Chemistry Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

5.1 Chemical Bonding

  • Chemical bond: the force that holds atoms together to form molecules or compounds.
  • Most elements exist in a combined state because their atoms are unstable by themselves; only a few (noble gases) have independent existence and are highly stable.
  • Why do atoms bond? To achieve a stable electron configuration (usually a noble-gas configuration).

The Octet Rule (Gilbert Lewis):

Note: Atoms tend to form compounds in ways that give them eight valence electrons — the electron configuration of a noble gas (except helium, which needs 2).
  • The valence electrons of all atoms are between 1 and 8.
  • Exceptions: hydrogen and helium need only 2 electrons; some elements can have more than 8.

Three ways atoms satisfy the octet rule:

  1. Losing their own valence electrons (metals).
  2. Gaining valence electrons from other elements (nonmetals).
  3. Sharing their valence electrons with other atoms (nonmetals).
Note: Example: Sodium (2,8,1) loses 1 electron → 2,8 (stable). Oxygen (2,6) gains/shares 2 electrons → 2,8 (octet).

5.2 Ionic Bonding

5.2.1 Formation of Ionic Bonding

What are ions?

  • Ion: any atom or molecule with a net positive or negative electric charge.
  • Net electric charge = number of protons − number of electrons.
  • Ions are highly reactive; usually gaseous; form when salts interact with solvents.

Types of ions:

  • Cations: atoms/groups with more protons than electrons → positive charge (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺).
  • Anions: atoms/groups with more electrons than protons → negative charge (Cl⁻, I⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻).

5.2.3 General Properties of Ionic Compounds

  1. Form crystals — exist as crystalline solids; ions (not molecules) held by strong electrostatic forces; cation/anion alternate in a regular 3-D structure (e.g., NaCl crystal).
  2. High melting and boiling points — much heat needed to break the strong attractions (NaCl melts ~800 °C, boils ~1465 °C).
  3. Hard and brittle — striking shifts ions so like charges meet → repulsion → crystal shatters.
  4. Soluble in polar solvents — "like dissolves like"; water is a polar solvent. (Many carbonate, oxalate, phosphate, sulphide, hydroxide salts are insoluble.)
  5. Conduct electricity when dissolved or molten — free ions move to electrodes (cations → cathode, anions → anode). Solid ionic compounds do NOT conduct (ions not free). NaCl(molten) → Na⁺ + Cl⁻.
  6. High density — ions packed closely by electrostatic attraction.
Note: Experiment 5.1 (Conductivity): Distilled water and solid table salt do NOT light the bulb; a water solution of table salt DOES (free ions conduct).

5.3 Covalent Bonding

5.3.1 Formation of Covalent Bond

  • Covalent bond: a bond formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons (simultaneous attraction of two nuclei for shared electrons).
  • The shared electrons are called bonding electrons.
  • Occurs between identical atoms or between different atoms with insufficient electronegativity difference for electron transfer.

Types of covalent bonds (by shared pairs):

  1. Single bond — one pair shared (H–H).
  2. Double bond — two pairs shared (O=O).
  3. Triple bond — three pairs shared (N≡N).
  • Bonds sharing more than one pair = multiple covalent bonds.

Single bonds:

  • H₂: H· + ·H → H:H or H–H (each H attains a helium-like configuration).
  • Cl₂: each Cl contributes one electron → shared pair; the other six valence electrons remain as nonbonding / lone pair / unshared electrons.
  • H₂S: two H atoms each share one electron with sulphur (Group VIA, 6 valence electrons) → S attains octet, H attains He configuration.

Multiple bonds:

  • O₂ (double): each O (Group VIA, 6 valence electrons) shares 2 → double bond (attains neon config).
  • N₂ (triple): each N (Group VA, 5 valence electrons) shares 3 → triple bond (neon config). 78% of atmosphere is N₂.

5.3.3 Polarity in Covalent Molecules

  • No bond is 100% ionic or 100% covalent; even diatomic covalent bonds have some ionic character.

Nonpolar covalent bond: shared pair attracted equally by two identical atoms → electrons exactly between nuclei (H₂, O₂, Cl₂, N₂, F₂).

Polar covalent bond: shared pair displaced toward the more electronegative atom (e.g., HF — electrons pulled toward F). A bond with unevenly shared electrons has a slight positive end and a slight negative end.

  • Nonpolar molecules: homonuclear diatomics (H₂, N₂, O₂, Cl₂), ethane (C₂H₆, ΔEN C–H = 0.4 < 0.5), CO₂ (linear, bond dipoles cancel), CCl₄ (tetrahedral, cancel), CH₄, C₂H₄, benzene, hydrocarbon liquids.
  • Polar molecules: HCl, H₂O (bent, dipoles don't cancel), NH₃, SO₂, H₂S, methanol, ethanol.

Electronegativity difference rules (rough guide):

  • Ionic bond: ΔEN ≥ 2.0.
  • Polar covalent bond: ΔEN 0.5–2.0.
  • Covalent (nonpolar): ΔEN < 0.5.
Note: Example: HCl (ΔEN 2.1/3.0 → 0.9) = polar covalent; KF (ΔEN 3.2) = ionic; C–C in ethane (ΔEN 0) = pure covalent (nonpolar).

5.3.4 Coordinate Covalent (Dative) Bond

  • Coordinate covalent bond (dative bond): a covalent bond where the electron pair is provided by only one of the bonded atoms, but shared by both after bond formation.
  • Once formed, the origin of the electrons cannot be distinguished.
  • Two necessary conditions:
  1. One atom must have a lone pair (e.g., H₂O, NH₃, H₂S).
  2. The other atom must have an empty space in its valence shell (e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃, H⁺).

Examples:

  • BF₃ + NH₃: N shares its lone pair with B (which has only 6 valence electrons) → coordinate bond.
  • NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺: ammonia shares its lone pair with the electron-less H⁺ → ammonium ion.
  • AlCl₃ → Al₂Cl₆: Al (electron-deficient) forms a coordinate bond with Cl of an adjacent AlCl₃ → covalent dimer.

5.3.5 General Properties of Covalent Compounds

  1. At room temperature, most are gases or liquids (some soft solids). Held by weak van der Waals forces.
  2. Low melting and boiling points. Exception: diamond (melts ~4027 °C).
  3. Poor conductors of electricity (no ions in solution/molten). Exceptions: graphite (conducts), HCl in aqueous solution.
  4. Soluble in nonpolar solvents, insoluble in polar solvents ("like dissolves like"); a few polar covalent compounds dissolve in polar solvents (methanol, ethanol).
  5. Slow reactions compared to ionic compounds (bond breaking needs high energy).
  6. Low density (few molecules per unit volume in gas/liquid state).
Note: Compare: Ionic — crystalline solids, high m.p./b.p., conduct when molten/dissolved, hard & brittle, soluble in polar solvents, high density. Covalent — gases/liquids, low m.p./b.p., poor conductors, soluble in nonpolar solvents, slow reactions, low density.

5.4 Metallic Bonding

5.4.1 Formation of Metallic Bond

  • A metal atom has 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons, which it easily loses → metals are highly electropositive. The lost electrons are called free electrons.
  • Metallic bond: occurs among atoms of the same metal (not between different metals in this model). Examples: aluminium foil, copper wire.
  • In a metal, valence electrons are delocalized — they move freely over the whole lump of metal. The metal is held together by attraction between positive nuclei (ion cores) and the delocalized electrons.
  • This is the "electron sea model" — "an array of positive ions in a sea of electrons."
  • Sodium is written Na (not Na⁺) because the outer electron is not totally lost to a particular atom.
Note: Problem (Mg vs Na): Mg (2,8,2) has higher melting point (650 °C) than Na (97.79 °C) because: both valence electrons delocalize (2× electron density); ions have 2+ charge; smaller radius → stronger attraction; more near neighbours (Mg has 12, Na has 8).
Note: Transition metals have especially high melting/boiling points (more valence electrons involved in delocalization).

5.4.2 Properties of Metallic Bond

  1. Conductivity: delocalized free electrons move through the structure, conducting electricity and heat efficiently.
  2. Malleable and ductile (discussed further in Grade 10).
  3. High melting/boiling points compared to nonmetals (strong attractions).

Factors that determine the strength of a metallic bond:

  1. Number of delocalized valence electrons (more = stronger).
  2. Charge on the ion core (higher = stronger).
  3. Size/radius of the atom (smaller = stronger, electrons closer to nucleus).
✍️ Must-know: Metallic bond = electron sea model. Metals conduct electricity & heat because valence electrons are delocalized (free to move).

📚 Unit 5 Summary (Key Facts)

  • Chemical bond = force holding atoms together. Why atoms bond = to achieve stable (noble-gas) configurations.
  • Octet rule (Lewis): atoms tend to have 8 valence electrons.
  • Three ways to satisfy octet: lose, gain, or share electrons.
  • Ion, cation, anion; net charge = protons − electrons; ionization.
  • Ionic bond = electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (metal + nonmetal).
  • Lewis dot formula: symbol + valence dots; shows transfer (ionic) or sharing (covalent).
  • Ionic compound properties: crystals, high m.p./b.p., hard & brittle, soluble in polar solvents, conduct when molten/dissolved, high density.
  • Covalent bond = sharing electron pairs: single, double, triple.
  • Polar vs nonpolar; ΔEN rules (ionic ≥2.0; polar 0.5–2.0; nonpolar <0.5).
  • Coordinate (dative) bond: one atom provides both electrons (BF₃–NH₃, NH₄⁺, Al₂Cl₆).
  • Covalent compound properties: gases/liquids, low m.p./b.p., poor conductors, soluble in nonpolar solvents, slow reactions, low density.
  • Metallic bond = electron sea model; delocalized electrons; good conductivity; factors affecting strength.
