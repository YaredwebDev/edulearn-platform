6.2 Breed Improvement Methods

◌ Not started

Productivity per animal (growth rate, milk yield, egg number) can be increased by feeding, health care, management and genetic improvement (products/services are functions of the genes and the living environment). The three methods of genetic improvement: breed introduction, selection and breeding.

6.2.1 Breed Introduction

Bringing high-quality breeds (higher productive capacity + desirable traits) into a farm or country (from another farm or country) — only if they clearly exceed the local breeds. - Local breeds: usually small, mature slowly, poor producers, but adapted to the local environment and resistant to diseases. - Exotic breeds: not native to Ethiopia, often larger, mature early, good producers, but may not adapt to the local environment and fall victim to diseases. - Solution: mate exotic × local to obtain cross-breeds carrying the best of both. - Advantages: introduces characteristics not present in local breeds; enhances productivity; cross-breeds may perform better if adapted locally. - Disadvantages: may introduce new diseases and pests; exotics may struggle to adapt; may not reach full productivity in the new environment.

6.2.2 Selection

  • Selection = allowing genetically superior animals to be parents of future generations while culling others. Culling = removing animals that don't perform at the desired level from the herd. The retained breeding stock should pass good qualities to offspring. Selection over many generations increases desirable genes and decreases undesirable ones.
  • Natural selection: animals better adapted to their environment survive and produce more offspring; non-adapted animals cannot survive/reproduce.
  • Artificial selection: farmers select and mate the best animals (increases health & productivity). Methods:
  • Mass selection: selected/rejected on the basis of their own performance (from a large group).
  • Progeny selection: selected on performance of their progeny/offspring (mothers of the best-performing offspring retained; poor ones culled).
  • Family selection: selected/rejected on the basis of performance of their relatives/family (used when family size is large).
  • Pedigree selection: selected/rejected on the basis of performance of their ancestors.
  • Advantages of selection: ensures only the best naturally available animal is chosen; desirable traits selected; best breeds bred for distribution; undesirable animals detected & culled; reduces spread of diseases/parasites in breeding stock.
  • Disadvantages: costly (time & money); needs expertise; may eliminate some desirable traits of parent stock; no new desirable traits are introduced (they must already exist).

6.2.3 Breeding

Breeding = developing animals by transferring inherited qualities from parents to offspring via mating. Categorised as related or unrelated breeding. - In-breeding: mating of more closely related animals than the population average (father–daughter, son–mother, brother–sister). Produces inbred lines usable for crossbreeding to get hybrid vigour; but can produce offspring with undesirable traits because recessive genes show up = in-breeding depression. - Line-breeding: mating of distantly related animals (e.g. cousins). Same disadvantages as in-breeding but undesirable traits take longer to appear; used to consolidate traits of a particular outstanding ancestor; many high-quality commercial characters result from line-breeding. - Out-breeding: mating of unrelated animals within the same breed; opposite of in-breeding; produces greater vigour and productivity. - Cross-breeding: mating of proven-quality animals of the same species but different breeds. Because parents differ in gene composition, offspring can have higher production capacity than either parent — this is heterosis / hybrid vigour. Gives greater growth rate, withstands climatic variation, increases disease resistance, and promotes higher egg/meat/milk yields. - Related breeding advantages: production of pure breeds/lines; concentrates & preserves specific qualities. Disadvantages: reduction in vigour/performance (drop in productivity, slow growth, loss of fertility, poor disease resistance); often gives defective animals. (Example: Afar ram × Afar ewe of the ram's daughter.) - Unrelated/cross-breeding advantages: promotes heterosis/hybrid vigour; develops new breeds; grows more rapidly & economical to rear; withstands environmental variation; more disease resistant. Disadvantages: higher operating costs than local breeds; if not systematic, sizing/delivery problems. (Example: Arsi cow × Holstein-Friesian bull.)

✍️ Must-know (breeding types): In-breeding (close relatives → in-breeding depression), line-breeding (distant relatives/cousins), out-breeding (unrelated within same breed), cross-breeding (different breeds → heterosis/hybrid vigour, higher production). Cross-breeding exploits hybrid vigour; its performance exceeds the average of the parental breeds.
