3.3 Atomic Theory

3.3.1 Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803/1808)

John Dalton (English chemist) proposed the theory based on experiments/observations. Five tenets: 1. Elements are made of small particles called atoms. 2. Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed. 3. All atoms of the same element are identical (same mass and size). 4. Atoms of different elements have different masses and sizes. 5. Atoms combine in small whole numbers to form compounds.

  • Dalton also proposed symbols for elements (hard to remember/draw). Berzelius later suggested using alphabets as symbols.

3.3.2 Modern Atomic Theory

Drawbacks of Dalton's theory: - Atoms are divisible into protons, neutrons, electrons (though atoms are the smallest particle that takes part in chemical reactions). - Atoms of the same element can differ in mass → isotopes (e.g., Cl-35, Cl-37). - Atoms of different elements can have the same atomic mass → isobars (e.g., Ar-40 and Ca-40). - Atoms don't always combine in simple whole-number ratios (e.g., complex organic compounds like sugar C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁).

Modern Atomic Theory postulates: 1. Elements are made of atoms. 2. Atoms cannot be created or destroyed during ordinary chemical reactions. 3. All atoms of the same element have the same atomic number but may vary in mass number (isotopes). 4. Atoms of different elements are different. 5. Atoms combine in small whole numbers to form compounds.

Timeline of discoveries: - 1886 Goldstein predicted proton (anode/canal ray experiment). - 1897 J.J. Thomson discovered the electron (charge-to-mass ratio). - 1904 Thomson: "plum pudding" model. - 1909 Robert Millikan: charge and mass of the electron. - 1911 Rutherford (+ Geiger & Marsden): planetary model. - 1913 Niels Bohr: electrons in concentric circular orbits. - 1920 Rutherford: discovered proton in the nucleus. - 1932 James Chadwick: discovered the neutron.
