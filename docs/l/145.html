4.12 Breeding

◌ Not started
  • Breeding = sexual reproduction (male+female → offspring) and the application of genetic principles in animal husbandry, agriculture and horticulture to improve desirable qualities. Breeding occurs by selective breeding/artificial selection or natural selection.
  • Selective breeding = choosing animals/plants with the most desirable characteristics to reproduce for the next generation; uses natural variation in a population.
  • Plant breeding — science of changing plant traits; aims for superior traits (biotic/abiotic stress tolerance such as drought, heat, salinity, flooding, insect pests), better yield, quality, ease of processing. Methods:
  • Mass selection — many plants of similar phenotype selected and seeds mixed (simplest form; practised by early humans; good for an inexperienced farmer).
  • Pure line selection — many plants selected from self-pollinated crops, harvested individually, best progeny released as a pureline variety.
  • Bulk method — F2 and later generations harvested in bulk; individual plants selected and evaluated later.
  • Animal breeding — choosing quality characteristics, selecting breeding stock, and deciding the breeding system.
  • Two types of selective breeding/artificial selection:
  • Inbreeding — mating genetically similar organisms; over generations offspring become almost genetically identical (inbred/purebred). Establishes stable traits, but the negative consequence is it makes undesired recessive traits more likely to be expressed. Basic genetic effect = increased homozygosity. Used to develop a pure line.
  • Crossbreeding — mating two purebred individuals from different breeds/varieties/species (e.g., local breeds × exotic sires). Crossbreeding is incompatible with conservation of indigenous breeds.
  • Natural selection = selection of traits without human intervention; better-adapted organisms survive and reproduce more.

✍️ Must-know: inbreeding → increased homozygosity / pure line; crossbreeding → two different breeds. Desired crop varieties are raised by hybridization; a breeder wanting a disease-resistant variety first uses selection; an inexperienced farmer uses mass selection; "making diseased organisms" is NOT an aspect of animal breeding.

4.12.1 Indigenous knowledge of Ethiopian farmers

  • Ethiopia has a very large/diverse livestock & crop resource base. CSA 2020a: 65 million cattle, 40 million sheep, 51 million goats, 8 million camels, 49 million chickens — the largest livestock population in Africa.
  • Rich centre of crop diversity: coffee, safflower, tef, noug, anchote, enset, wheat, barley, sorghum, peas, linseed, castor, finger millet, lentil, oats. Five major cereals = teff, wheat, maize, sorghum, barley.
  • Farmers keep indigenous knowledge on selecting desired characteristics (growth rate, body size, disease/environmental resistance), breeding and management — accumulated over many years under traditional extensive systems.
