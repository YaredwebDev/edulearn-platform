2.1 Measurements and Units in Chemistry

2.1.1 SI Units

  • Measurement: the comparison of a physical quantity with a known standard unit of quantity.
  • A physical quantity has a numeric value and a unit (e.g., mass = 2.5 kg).
  • The International System of Units (SI) is used by scientists for clear, reproducible communication.

  • Kelvin ↔ Celsius: K = °C + 273.15 (often just + 273)
  • Celsius ↔ Fahrenheit: °C = 5/9 × (°F − 32) and °F = (9/5 × °C) + 32

2.1.2 Measurements of Mass, Volume, and Density

  • Mass: amount of matter in an object (SI unit kg).
  • Volume: the amount of space occupied; 1 L = 1 dm³ and 1 mL = 1 cm³.
  • Density (d): mass per unit volume; d = m/V.
  • Solid density: g/cm³; liquid density: g/mL; gas density: g/L.
  • Density of water = 1.000 g/cm³ at 4 °C.
  • Mass (m) = d × V; Volume (V) = m/d.
Note: Example 2.1 (density): A 30.5 g sample of a liquid occupies 35.1 mL. What is its density?
Note: d = m/V = 30.5 g / 35.1 mL = 0.869 g/mL → this is toluene.
Note: Example (top-loading balance): A balance reads 23.25 g with uncertainty ±0.05 g. Percent uncertainty = (0.05/23.25) × 100 ≈ 0.2%.

2.1.4 Measurement Uncertainty

  • Uncertainty: the doubt that exists about the result of every measurement (no measurement is perfect).
  • Systematic errors: caused by incorrect technique or faulty instruments; reproducible but wrong; not reduced by taking more readings (bias).
  • Random errors: caused by the operator or fluctuations; scatter readings; reduced by averaging.

Reporting uncertainty (three approaches):

  1. ± half of the range (e.g., 66, 65, 61 mm → mean 64, range 5, so 64 ± 2.5 mm).
  2. ± the furthest reading from the mean (61 is furthest: 3 away → 64 ± 3 mm).
  3. ± resolution of the instrument (±0.5 of the smallest division).
  • Percent uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty / measurement) × 100.
  • Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other (reproducibility).
  • Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true/accepted value.
  • A measurement can be precise but inaccurate, or accurate but imprecise.
Note: Example 2.4 (archery target): Precision = tight grouping (arrows clustered); accuracy = hitting the bull's-eye (true value).

2.1.5 Significant Figures

Rules for counting significant figures:

  1. Non-zero digits are always significant (e.g., 394 cm has 3).
  2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (e.g., 5.03 g has 3).
  3. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant (e.g., 0.714 m has 3; 2.720×10²² has 4).
  4. Leading zeros (before the first non-zero digit) are NOT significant — they only place the decimal (e.g., 0.052 kg has 2).
  5. Zeros without a decimal point are ambiguous (e.g., 3000 mL — unclear; write in scientific notation).

Rounding rule: if the digit is < 5, drop it; if ≥ 5, increase the preceding digit by 1.

Calculations:

  • Addition/Subtraction: round to the fewest number of decimal places.
  • Multiplication/Division: round to the fewest number of significant figures.

Scientific notation: N × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ N < 10 (e.g., 6.02×10²³; 3.27×10⁻²²).

Note: Example 2.5 (sig figs): 394 cm = 3; 5.03 g = 3; 0.714 m = 3; 0.052 kg = 2; 2.720×10²² = 4; 3000 mL = ambiguous.
Note: Example 2.6 (unit conversion): 1.10 Å = 1.10×10⁻⁸ cm = 1.10×10⁻¹ nm.
