3.3 Protein Structures

◌ Not started

Protein = a polymer of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, with 3-D arrangement of atoms. Four structural levels: 1. Primary structure — the linear sequence of amino acids in one polypeptide chain (via peptide bonds). Proteins with < 50 amino acids = peptides; > 50 amino acids = polypeptides. Humans need 20 amino acids; 10 are made in the body, 10 must come from the diet. 2. Secondary structure — folded structure within a polypeptide from backbone interactions via hydrogen bonding; two types: - α-helix — right-handed coiled strand; N–H of each amino acid H-bonds to the C=O of the amino acid four residues away; side chains extend outward. - β-pleated sheet — strands lying side-by-side, stretched to near-maximum extension, held by intermolecular hydrogen bonds (inter-strand/intra-strand). 3. Tertiary structure — the 3-D shape of a single polypeptide (bends/twists to achieve maximum stability/lowest energy), stabilised by bonding between side-chain groups of amino acids. 4. Quaternary structure — the arrangement of multiple folded protein subunits in a multi-subunit complex (each with its own primary/secondary/tertiary structure), held together by hydrogen bonds. Example: hemoglobin.

Table 3.2 summary: primary = linear sequence (one chain); secondary = folding into structural patterns within one chain; tertiary = 3-D arrangement of all amino acids in a single chain; quaternary = multiple chains held in a specific spatial arrangement.

✍️ Must-know: protein subunits (quaternary) are held together by hydrogen bonds; hemoglobin is the classic quaternary example. Primary level = amino-acid sequence; peptide <50 aa, polypeptide >50 aa.
