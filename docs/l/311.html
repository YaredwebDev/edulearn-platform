6.17 Fats and Oils

◌ Not started

6.6.1 Triglycerides

  • Fats and oils are esters — triesters of glycerol, called triglycerides (triacylglycerols). Glycerol has three –OH groups so it can form three ester bonds to fatty acids.
  • General structure: glyceryl unit with three fatty-acid chains R₁, R₂, R₃ (may be same or different).
  • A triglyceride is a fat if solid/semi-solid at room temperature, an oil if liquid at room temperature. Animal triglycerides are usually fats; plant triglycerides are usually oils (hence "animal fats", "vegetable oils").
  • Fats = triesters of glycerol with mostly saturated fatty acids; oils = triesters with mostly unsaturated fatty acids. The acid parts almost always have an even number of carbons.
  • Common structures: glyceryl tristearate (stearin) = a saturated fat; glyceryl trioleate (olein) = an unsaturated oil.
  • Edible vegetable oils ≠ mineral oils (mineral oils are long-chain alkanes from petroleum).

💡 Activity 6.14: butter, lard, tallow (animal, solid) vs peanut/soybean/olive oil (plant, liquid) — origin correlates with physical state because plant fats tend to be unsaturated.

6.6.2 Physical properties of fats and oils

Common: greasy to the touch, lubricating; not readily volatile; burn without residue (ash). Pure fats/oils are colourless, odourless, neutral; lighter than water and immiscible with it; soluble in organic solvents (benzene, ether, chloroform).

6.6.3 Hardening of oils (hydrogenation) and oxidation numbers

  • Oils can be converted to fats by hydrogenation — adding H₂ across the C=C double bonds at high pressure with a Ni or Pd catalyst. This is hardening of oils, used to make margarine and solid shortenings (e.g. a hydrogenated vegetable product similar to animal fat).
  • Glyceryl trioleate (oil) + 3H₂ → glyceryl tristearate (fat). (Each oleate double bond is saturated.)
  • Energy content & oxidation numbers: the carbon atoms in fats and oils have different oxidation numbers; the lower the oxidation number, the higher the energy content. In oils the double-bonded carbons are sp²; in fats all hydrocarbon-tail carbons are sp³.
  • Sources: saturated fat in animal foods (beef, chicken, lamb, pork), coconut/palm oils, dairy (butter, cheese, whole milk), lard. Monounsaturated fats in avocados, nuts/seeds, olive/canola/peanut oils; polyunsaturated in fatty fish, fish oils, and corn/flaxseed/soybean/sunflower oils.

✍️ Must-know: hydrogenation/hardening converts an oil (unsaturated, liquid) into a fat (saturated, solid). More double bonds → lower melting point → liquid at room temp. Oils = unsaturated (sp² C), fats = saturated (sp³ C).

6.6.4 Rancidity

  • On storage (especially warm, with air), fats/oils deteriorate and spoil — rancidity: development of an unpleasant odour/flavour.
  • Caused mainly by hydrolysis of the ester linkage and oxidation across double bonds. Rancidity is worse when raw materials were already decomposed, or processing/storage was slow or unsanitary.
  • To keep fats sound: process quickly & cleanly, store well (airtight/sealed containers help, as the potato-chip experiment shows).

✋ Trap: edible fats/oils are esters (triglycerides), NOT hydrocarbons; mineral oils (petroleum) are long-chain alkanes — a different class.
