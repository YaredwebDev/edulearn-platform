5F.1 The process approach to writing

  1. Pre-writing — decide the topic, generate & select ideas, plan (brainstorm, organise).
  2. Drafting — put ideas into complete sentences, organise into main ideas + supporting details, compose the first draft; add support/illustrations.
  3. Revision — read the draft; add/delete/reorder sentences; check word choice; make it clear for the reader.
  4. Editing — proofread for grammar, spelling & punctuation; get feedback.

5F.2 Structure of an essay

  • An essay = a piece of formal writing dealing with a single idea, written with a purpose (inform, persuade, entertain, or a mix). Made up of an introduction, body, conclusion.
  • Introduction: grabs attention; moves from general to specific; gives a "road map"; ends with the thesis statement (arguably the most important component — states the aim of the paper and hints at the author's examples/evidence).
  • Body: the evidence/support + the writer's ideas; each paragraph has a topic sentence relating back to the thesis; logical ordering.
  • Conclusion: wraps up the arguments; restates the main arguments simply; leaves the reader with something to think about (esp. for argumentative essays).
  • Ways to order ideas: chronological (time; narratives) · spatial (locations, top-to-bottom) · emphatic (least→most important; common in high-school writing; with examples/evidence and transitions). Always keep examples relevant and cite sources.
  • Model reading: Practical Challenges for Water in Agriculture — find the thesis statement, three topic sentences, and each paragraph's function.

5F.3 Argumentative essay writing

  • An argumentative essay presents a clear claim/position, backs views with solid evidence, addresses counter-arguments respectfully, and persuades the reader.
  • Parts: strong introduction with a clear thesis; body paragraphs substantiated with detailed evidence (each with a topic sentence); a compelling conclusion (no new info; show evidence supports the thesis). Use transitional words/phrases (similarly, on the same note, contrarily, in support of, equally important, nevertheless, with this in mind, for example, all things considered, given these points) for flow.
  • Structure for an argument (one side vs counter): present advantages of your view, address the opposing side's points courteously and show why they don't hold.
  • Model: single-sex vs mixed-sex schools (writer's position: co-educational better). Prompts: Promoting irrigation-based farming is more important than mechanized farming; Agriculture-led economy is better than industry-led economy for the present Ethiopia.
