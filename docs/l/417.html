1E.1 The simple present tense

  • Describes actions/states happening now, habitual actions, and general truths.
  • Use the base form (V1); add -s for third-person singular (he/she/it). Typical adverbials: every day, every week, every morning, on Sundays, on weekdays.
  • Uses: repeated actions (I take the train to the office), facts (A dog has four legs), habits (I get up early every day), things generally true (It rains a lot in winter).

1E.2 Describing processes: the simple present passive

  • The passive moves the object of an active sentence to subject position, so the focus is on the thing/person affected (used to describe processes/procedures, e.g. making coffee).
  • Formation: present tense of be (am/is/are) + past participle. Only transitive verbs (verbs that take objects) can be passive.
  • I am + past participle · you/we/they are + past participle · he/she/it is + past participle.
  • Examples (active → passive): We clean the rooms every day → The rooms are cleaned every day (by us); Do they serve coffee here? → Is coffee served here?
  • The "by …" phrase is optional (bracketed) when the agent is unimportant/obvious.

1E.3 The simple future tense

  • Several forms/functions:
  • will + verb = factual/uncontrollable (The winner will be awarded).
  • will + verb with belief = personal (I am sure the winner will be awarded).
  • be going to = planned/decided (The winner is going to be awarded).
  • Future arrangements & time clauses (…will be awarded when the guest of honour arrives).
  • Present simple for official timetable (takes place tomorrow); present continuous for planned arrangement (is taking place next week).

1E.4 Adverbial clauses of concession

  • A dependent clause that contrasts the main-clause idea. Conjunctions: though, although, even though, while, whereas, even if, however.
  • Examples: Although it rained, they enjoyed their walk; Joseph was very popular whereas his brother had many enemies.
  • Language tips: though and although mean the same and give an unexpected result; though can go in any part of the sentence but although cannot end a sentence; put a comma when the though/although clause comes first, none when the main clause is first. Even though is a stronger form of although.

1E.5 Types of English nouns

  • A noun names a person, place, thing or idea; it can function as subject, direct/indirect object, object/subject complement, appositive, adjective.
  • Types taught: common (friend, state, freedom) vs proper (specific) · concrete (perceivable by the 5 senses; e.g. granite, sand) vs abstract (ideas/feelings: love, hate, power, time) · collective (a group; often of animals) · compound (two words as one: softball, toothpaste) · singular vs plural · possessive (shows ownership) · countable (can be plural; with many/most/several).
