1.4 Classification of Crop Plants

◌ Not started

Crop plants fall into four major end-use categories: food crops, oil crops, fibre crops, sugar crops (forage is also commonly listed).

1.4.1 Major food crops

  • Global "big three": wheat, rice and maize (by volume). Others: sorghum, barley, oats.
  • Ethiopia (by cultivated area): teff, wheat, maize, sorghum, barley.
  • Monocots (grass/grass-like, one cotyledon) store food mainly in the endosperm (starch + a little protein). Dicots (two cotyledons; e.g. legumes & oil seeds) store food in the cotyledons (high protein, oil, some carbohydrate). Important food/feed legumes & oil-seed grains: soybean, peanut, beans, peas (their protein is low in certain essential amino acids).
  • Cotyledon = seed leaf within the embryo that serves as food for germination.
  • Endosperm = tissue inside the seed providing starch-based nutrition.

1.4.2 Major oil crops

  • Grown to extract oil from seeds. Major oil seeds: olive, linseed, sesame, sunflower; also soybean, coconut, palm, maize, peanut.
  • Plant oils supply two essential fatty acids (linoleic, alpha-linolenic) and vitamins A, K, D, E. Oils/fats are triglycerides = glycerine + three fatty acids (fatty acids = long chains of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen).

1.4.3 Major fibre crops

  • Used for clothing & the textile industry. Cotton lint is the most important cloth fibre. Fibres come from cotton and from the stems/inner barks of flax, jute, hemp, ramie, kenaf, nettle, bamboo. Jute is hard and used for ropes/packaging.

1.4.4 Major forage crops

  • Forage crops = plants used as feed for livestock/farm animals. Examples: alfalfa, Sesbania sesban, Leucaena leucocephala, clovers, Timothy; also elephant grass, Desho grass, Rhodes grass, Panicum grass, Sudan grass, Johnson grass. Cereal grains at early growth stages can also be fed. Provide protein, fibre, vitamins A & E, and minerals (Na, K, Ca, P, Mg).

1.4.5 C3 and C4 plants (photosynthetic pathway)

  • Plants turn atmospheric CO₂ into carbohydrates via photosynthesis.
  • C3 plants: first stable compound has 3 carbons. Photosynthesis rate increases with higher CO₂; they lack a CO₂-accumulation mechanism, so they cannot reach high rates at low CO₂.
  • C4 plants: first stable compound has 4 carbons; they have a CO₂-accumulation mechanism in the chloroplast, so their rate does not rise with more CO₂, they reach high rates at low CO₂, and are more water-efficient and need less nitrogen; higher photosynthetic rates at a given temperature than C3.

✋ Trap (memorise the examples): C3 crops = wheat, barley, teff, soybean, groundnut, sweet potato. C4 crops = sorghum, maize, sugar cane, and most weeds. Under climate-change (rising CO₂), C3 plants respond more to increased CO₂, whereas C4 plants do not increase photosynthesis with added CO₂.

  • Photosynthesis = process by which plants use sunlight, water and CO₂ to make oxygen and energy (sugar).
