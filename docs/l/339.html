5.3 Covalent Bonding

◌ Not started

5.3.1 Formation of Covalent Bond

  • Covalent bond: a bond formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons (simultaneous attraction of two nuclei for shared electrons).
  • The shared electrons are called bonding electrons.
  • Occurs between identical atoms or between different atoms with insufficient electronegativity difference for electron transfer.

Types of covalent bonds (by shared pairs): 1. Single bond — one pair shared (H–H). 2. Double bond — two pairs shared (O=O). 3. Triple bond — three pairs shared (N≡N). - Bonds sharing more than one pair = multiple covalent bonds.

Single bonds: - H₂: H· + ·H → H:H or H–H (each H attains a helium-like configuration). - Cl₂: each Cl contributes one electron → shared pair; the other six valence electrons remain as nonbonding / lone pair / unshared electrons. - H₂S: two H atoms each share one electron with sulphur (Group VIA, 6 valence electrons) → S attains octet, H attains He configuration.

Multiple bonds: - O₂ (double): each O (Group VIA, 6 valence electrons) shares 2 → double bond (attains neon config). - N₂ (triple): each N (Group VA, 5 valence electrons) shares 3 → triple bond (neon config). 78% of atmosphere is N₂.

5.3.2 Lewis Formulas of Covalent Molecules

  • Lewis structures: drawings that describe bonding in molecules.
  • Number of bonds an atom forms = number of electrons needed to reach an octet (true for 2nd-period nonmetals C, N, O, F).
  • Group IVA (e.g., C): 4 bonds (CCl₄, SiH₄). Group VA (N): 3 bonds (NH₃). Group VIA (O): 2 bonds.
  • H needs only 2 electrons (exception to octet rule).

5.3.3 Polarity in Covalent Molecules

  • No bond is 100% ionic or 100% covalent; even diatomic covalent bonds have some ionic character.

Nonpolar covalent bond: shared pair attracted equally by two identical atoms → electrons exactly between nuclei (H₂, O₂, Cl₂, N₂, F₂).

Polar covalent bond: shared pair displaced toward the more electronegative atom (e.g., HF — electrons pulled toward F). A bond with unevenly shared electrons has a slight positive end and a slight negative end.

  • Nonpolar molecules: homonuclear diatomics (H₂, N₂, O₂, Cl₂), ethane (C₂H₆, ΔEN C–H = 0.4 < 0.5), CO₂ (linear, bond dipoles cancel), CCl₄ (tetrahedral, cancel), CH₄, C₂H₄, benzene, hydrocarbon liquids.
  • Polar molecules: HCl, H₂O (bent, dipoles don't cancel), NH₃, SO₂, H₂S, methanol, ethanol.

Electronegativity difference rules (rough guide): - Ionic bond: ΔEN ≥ 2.0. - Polar covalent bond: ΔEN 0.5–2.0. - Covalent (nonpolar): ΔEN < 0.5.

Example: HCl (ΔEN 2.1/3.0 → 0.9) = polar covalent; KF (ΔEN 3.2) = ionic; C–C in ethane (ΔEN 0) = pure covalent (nonpolar).

5.3.4 Coordinate Covalent (Dative) Bond

  • Coordinate covalent bond (dative bond): a covalent bond where the electron pair is provided by only one of the bonded atoms, but shared by both after bond formation.
  • Once formed, the origin of the electrons cannot be distinguished.
  • Two necessary conditions: 1. One atom must have a lone pair (e.g., H₂O, NH₃, H₂S). 2. The other atom must have an empty space in its valence shell (e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃, H⁺).

Examples: - BF₃ + NH₃: N shares its lone pair with B (which has only 6 valence electrons) → coordinate bond. - NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺: ammonia shares its lone pair with the electron-less H⁺ → ammonium ion. - AlCl₃ → Al₂Cl₆: Al (electron-deficient) forms a coordinate bond with Cl of an adjacent AlCl₃ → covalent dimer.

5.3.5 General Properties of Covalent Compounds

  1. At room temperature, most are gases or liquids (some soft solids). Held by weak van der Waals forces.
  2. Low melting and boiling points. Exception: diamond (melts ~4027 °C).
  3. Poor conductors of electricity (no ions in solution/molten). Exceptions: graphite (conducts), HCl in aqueous solution.
  4. Soluble in nonpolar solvents, insoluble in polar solvents ("like dissolves like"); a few polar covalent compounds dissolve in polar solvents (methanol, ethanol).
  5. Slow reactions compared to ionic compounds (bond breaking needs high energy).
  6. Low density (few molecules per unit volume in gas/liquid state).

Compare: Ionic — crystalline solids, high m.p./b.p., conduct when molten/dissolved, hard & brittle, soluble in polar solvents, high density. Covalent — gases/liquids, low m.p./b.p., poor conductors, soluble in nonpolar solvents, slow reactions, low density.
