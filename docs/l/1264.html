6.4 Sound Waves

  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects; every source of sound is in a state of vibration. Sound travels in all directions as a wave.
  • Sound is the most common example of a longitudinal wave.
  • Sound needs a material medium for transmission (e.g., air). It cannot travel through a vacuum.

Speed of sound:

  • Depends on the compressibility and density of the medium — different speeds in different media.
  • Speed in air at 0 °C ≈ 331 m/s.
  • v = 331 m/s × √(1 + T_c/273°C) (6.7), where T_c is the air temperature in °C.
  • Sound travels slowest in gases, faster in liquids, fastest in solids (particles closest in solids).
  • Speed in liquids and solids is not significantly affected by temperature change, but by body structure.

Speed of sound in various materials: Air (0 °C) 331, Air (25 °C) 343, Sea water (25 °C) 1533, Water (25 °C) 1493, Iron 5950, Lead 1960, Rubber 1600 m/s.

Note: Example 6.4: Speed of sound at 20 °C: v = 331×√(1+20/273) ≈ 343 m/s.

Wave behaviors:

  • Reflection (echo): sound bounces back from hard surfaces; reflection of sound from hard surfaces is called an echo. Used in SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging) to find depth of seas/submarines. Hard polished surfaces are good reflectors; soft surfaces (wool, cloth) absorb sound.
  • Refraction: the bending/change in direction of a sound wave due to a change in its speed (e.g., uneven winds, air of uneven temperature). During the day warm air near ground makes sound bend away; at night cold air makes it bend toward the ground (so we hear better at night).
  • Diffraction: the spreading out or change in direction of a wave as it passes through an opening or around a barrier. Because of diffraction we hear people in adjacent rooms. More pronounced with longer wavelengths (we hear low frequencies better around obstacles).
  • Interference: see section 6.5.
