11.6 Indigenous Knowledge in Natural Resources Management

◌ Not started
  • Ethiopia is a land of diverse altitudes & climates with varied natural vegetation; indigenous knowledge contributes to forest conservation.
  • North Shewa farmers apply indigenous soil & water conservation practices to combat land degradation:
  • Constructing a wide waterway (40–50 cm wide) between adjacent farms to control runoff from upper slopes.
  • A medium traditional ditch dissecting the farm and connecting two wide waterways (reduces runoff speed by diverting excess water).
  • A single line of stones across the farm on slopes up to 2% (reduces runoff speed, retains sediments).
  • Planting tree species at the farm edge to protect soil from erosion.
  • Using local vegetative barriers that filter sediments & reduce runoff speed.
  • Combining traditional stone bunds + vegetation (grasses or perennial species) to control erosion on steep slopes.
  • Gumuz community (north-western Ethiopia): fertile soils, enormous vegetation with diverse plants/animals, abundant minerals (gold); livelihood is highly natural-resource-based. Their indigenous knowledge is tied to traditional belief systems:
  • Vital natural resources are sacred.
  • Natural resources are a gift, blessing & creation of Yamba (God).
  • Yamba gave the community knowledge of proper use, management & responsibility to pass to future generations.
  • Natural resources are seen as ancestral heritage.
  • Ownership is vested in the whole community — resources cannot be privately possessed or controlled by single members.
  • This knowledge is mostly norms, values, ethics & taboos, institutionalised as customary laws & conventions, enforced by elders.
