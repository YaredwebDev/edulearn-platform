6.2 Population size, density and dispersal

◌ Not started

Population size = the total number of individuals present in a particular habitat (designated "N"). Because counting every individual is hard, scientists use sampling techniques (count in sample areas and infer the total). Methods: - Quadrat method — for immobile/slow organisms (plants, small slow-moving animals). A quadrat (square frame of wood/plastic/metal) is placed on the ground; individuals within each quadrat are counted; many quadrats are placed randomly. Formula: Population estimate = (total area A / sampled area a) × number counted n Example: A = 50 m², a = 10 m², n = 50 plants → (50/10)×50 = 250 plants. - Mark–recapture method — for mobile animals. (1) Capture & mark a sample, release; (2) capture a second sample, count marked & unmarked; (3) estimate total population by the ratio. Formula: Population estimate = (M × n) / X, where M = marked released, n = total in 2nd capture, X = marked in 2nd capture. Example: M = 60, n = 100, X = 20 → (60×100)/20 = 300 birds.

Studying the human population — census: A census = systematic collection of information from all units/individuals of a population (complete enumeration), incl. age, sex, ethnic group, religion, marital status, household, occupation, etc. Conducted (Ethiopia) at least once every ~10 years. Two methods: 1. De Facto method — the government fixes one date (often a full-moon night) and counts everyone present in the country at that moment. 2. De Jure method — enumeration over 2–3 weeks; enumerators visit households; only permanently residing persons are counted (temporary residents are not).

Population density = number of individuals within a specific area/volume, i.e., population size ÷ total land area (e.g., people per 10 km²). Density is dynamic: birth & immigration increase it; death & emigration decrease it. Example: plant A density = 27 individuals/10 m² = 2.7.

Population dispersal patterns (how individuals are distributed in space at a given time): 1. Uniform dispersion — spaced more or less evenly. 2. Random dispersion — distributed randomly, no predictable pattern. 3. Clumped dispersion — clustered in groups (most common in nature).
