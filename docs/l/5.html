1.5 Cropping Systems

◌ Not started

A cropping system = the types and sequences of crops plus the practices used to grow them. Forms: mono cropping, mixed cropping (intercropping & relay planting), and crop rotation.

1.5.1 Mono cropping

  • Growing a single crop type on a plot (common in large-scale commercial production).
  • Disadvantages: risk of total crop failure from pests/diseases; reduced soil fertility.
  • Advantages: uniform management; more efficient planting & harvesting.

1.5.2 Mixed cropping (intercropping & relay planting)

  • Growing two or more crops simultaneously on the same land.
  • Advantages: reduces risk of total yield loss; more efficient use of soil nutrients, water and light; helps break the pest/disease cycle.
  • Intercropping = planting more than one crop in regular rows on the same field at the same time (often a cereal like maize alongside a legume). Intercrop & relay systems give better soil cover, reduce soil erosion, and spread crop diseases more slowly than monocropping.
  • Relay planting = a second crop is planted after the first has grown alone for a period.

1.5.3 Crop rotation

  • Growing different crop species sequentially on the same plot (e.g., maize → beans → teff across seasons).
  • Objectives: improve soil fertility (especially when legumes are included) and control some crop pests/diseases.
  • Possible downsides: may develop resistant weeds/diseases; changing climates can challenge rotations.

✍️ Must-know (cropping systems): mono = single crop on a plot; intercropping = ≥2 crops in rows at the same time; relay = second crop planted later into the standing first crop; rotation = different crops in sequence over seasons. Advantages centre on spreading risk, better nutrient/light use, pest-disease control, soil cover/erosion control.
