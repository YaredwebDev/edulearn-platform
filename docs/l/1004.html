5.4 Metallic Bonding

5.4.1 Formation of Metallic Bond

  • A metal atom has 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons, which it easily loses → metals are highly electropositive. The lost electrons are called free electrons.
  • Metallic bond: occurs among atoms of the same metal (not between different metals in this model). Examples: aluminium foil, copper wire.
  • In a metal, valence electrons are delocalized — they move freely over the whole lump of metal. The metal is held together by attraction between positive nuclei (ion cores) and the delocalized electrons.
  • This is the "electron sea model" — "an array of positive ions in a sea of electrons."
  • Sodium is written Na (not Na⁺) because the outer electron is not totally lost to a particular atom.
Note: Problem (Mg vs Na): Mg (2,8,2) has higher melting point (650 °C) than Na (97.79 °C) because: both valence electrons delocalize (2× electron density); ions have 2+ charge; smaller radius → stronger attraction; more near neighbours (Mg has 12, Na has 8).
Note: Transition metals have especially high melting/boiling points (more valence electrons involved in delocalization).

5.4.2 Properties of Metallic Bond

  1. Conductivity: delocalized free electrons move through the structure, conducting electricity and heat efficiently.
  2. Malleable and ductile (discussed further in Grade 10).
  3. High melting/boiling points compared to nonmetals (strong attractions).

Factors that determine the strength of a metallic bond:

  1. Number of delocalized valence electrons (more = stronger).
  2. Charge on the ion core (higher = stronger).
  3. Size/radius of the atom (smaller = stronger, electrons closer to nucleus).
✍️ Must-know: Metallic bond = electron sea model. Metals conduct electricity & heat because valence electrons are delocalized (free to move).
