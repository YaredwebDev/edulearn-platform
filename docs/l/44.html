8.1 Introduction to Animal Health and Disease Control

◌ Not started
  • The best economic returns come when animals are kept in good health and disease problems are minimised.
  • Health = the state of wellbeing of an animal. Any departure from health is disease.
  • A healthy animal attains an acceptable level of production within the farming system. Disease consequences: loss of appetite & weight, slow growth, reduced production, reproduction loss, death.

8.1.1 Routes of Transmission of Pathogens

Pathogens = disease-causing microorganisms. Animals can be infected by: - Direct contact with an infected animal or its tissues/fluids (blood, saliva, urine, droppings). - Ingestion of pathogens from contaminated feed, water and farm equipment (e.g. feed & water troughs). - Spread during mating, or before birth through the placenta (uro-genital route). - Inhalation of droplets or dust containing pathogens. - Vectors — living organisms (e.g. insects) that carry pathogens.

8.1.2 Symptoms of Sick Animals

Symptom = a sign or indication of the existence of a disease/disorder. Sick animals may show: - Loss of appetite, weight loss, coughing, inability to rise, slow movement, lameness, isolation from the herd/flock. - Standing with head & neck down; tired or lazy look. - Watery, bad-smelling or blood-stained faeces and urine. - Discharges from body openings, sunken eyes, raised hair coat, rough skin, dry muzzle, swelling in joints. - Variation in body temperature, pulse rate, respiration rate. Disease may lead to death.

✋ Key distinction — Infectious vs non-infectious diseases: - Infectious — caused by pathogenic organisms (virus, bacteria, protozoa); communicable animal→animal or animal→human. Examples: anthrax, foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), Newcastle disease. - Non-infectious — neither caused by pathogens nor passed animal to animal; caused by hereditary factors or environment. Examples: ketosis, rickets, poisoning.
