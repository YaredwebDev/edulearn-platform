6.4 Electric Current, Resistance and Ohm's Law

◌ Not started

Electric current: the rate of flow of charge. I = Q/t; SI unit ampere (A) (1 A = 1 C/s). Smaller units: mA, μA. Conventional current direction is opposite to electron (drift) motion.

Current density: J = I/A (current per unit cross-sectional area; A = area), unit A/m².

Drift velocity: the slow average velocity of electrons through a conductor. Current in a wire: I = n A v_d e (n = number density of free electrons, A = area, v_d = drift velocity, e = electron charge). Drift speeds are very small (a fraction of a mm/s), yet the bulb lights instantly because the electric field (signal) propagates through the wire almost at light speed.

Resistance and Ohm's law: V = I R (for an ohmic conductor). R = V/I; SI unit ohm (Ω). 1 Ω = 1 V/A. - Ohmic conductors: obey Ohm's law (constant R — I vs V is a straight line). Non-ohmic devices (e.g., diodes, filament bulbs) don't.

✍️ Must-know: I = Q/t = nAv_de; V = IR (Ohm's law); R in Ω = V/A. Example 6.11: headlight carrying 2.50 A at 12.0 V has R = 12/2.5 = 4.8 Ω.

Resistors in series: same current through each; total R_s = R1 + R2 + R3 + … (larger than any individual). Resistors in parallel: same voltage across each; 1/R_p = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + … (equivalent smaller than the smallest individual). Current divides among branches.

✋ Trap: series → add resistances (total greater); parallel → reciprocal add (total smaller than the smallest). In series, resistors get the same current and split voltage; in parallel they have the same voltage and split current.

Worked examples: Example 6.12 (mixed series/parallel circuit — equivalent resistance, currents and potential differences across each resistor). Review: equivalent resistance between points; current through a 5 Ω resistor.

Measuring instruments: - Ammeter measures current and is connected in series (low resistance so it doesn't disturb the circuit). - Voltmeter measures potential difference and is connected in parallel across the component (high resistance). - Ideal ammeter: near-zero resistance; ideal voltmeter: very large resistance.

Converting a galvanometer to an ammeter: connect a low shunt resistance in parallel so most current bypasses the coil. (Example 6.14a: convert a 5 mA, 50 Ω galvanometer to a 10 A ammeter by adding a shunt.) Converting a galvanometer to a voltmeter: connect a high multiplier resistance in series with the coil. (Example 6.14b: convert a 3 mA, 40 Ω galvanometer to a 0–10 V voltmeter.)

Potential divider & potentiometer: a potential-divider circuit splits the supply voltage across series resistors (voltage drops proportional to resistance). A potentiometer can measure a cell's emf, its internal resistance, compare emfs, or act as a variable resistor (balance length AY used to find unknown emf).

Wheatstone bridge: used to measure an unknown resistance precisely. Balance condition (no current through the galvanometer): R1/R2 = R3/R4 (ratio of opposite arms equal). Example 6.15: find R so that there is no current in the 50 Ω resistor.

Comparing bulb brightness: brightness depends on power (and hence current/voltage). Bulbs in parallel have the full battery voltage and glow brighter; bulbs in series divide the voltage and are dimmer. In a mixed circuit, the bulb taking full battery current is brightest (Example 6.16/6.17). Brightness is measured in lumens (luminous flux). CFL/LED bulbs give more lumens per watt (more efficient) than incandescent.

✋ Trap: two bulbs in parallel glow brighter than the same two in series, because in series the voltage is divided. Adding a third identical bulb in parallel does not dim the others (each still has full voltage).

Kirchhoff's rules (for complex circuits): - Junction (first) rule (conservation of charge): the sum of currents entering a junction = sum of currents leaving it. ΣI_in = ΣI_out. - Loop (second) rule (conservation of energy): around any closed loop, the algebraic sum of the potential differences is zero. ΣV = 0.

Sign conventions (from textbook): - EMF of a cell is positive when traversing from − to + terminal; negative from + to −. - IR across a resistor is positive if moving opposite to the current; negative if moving with the current.

Solving Kirchhoff problems: label branch currents; apply the junction rule at junctions; apply the loop rule to independent loops; solve simultaneous equations. (Example: find I1, I2, I3 in a two-loop circuit — negative answer means the assumed direction was wrong.)

EMF vs terminal voltage: a real cell has internal resistance r, so the terminal voltage V = ε − I r (less than the emf ε when supplying current). Emf = energy per unit charge supplied; terminal voltage = voltage across terminals. ε = I(R + r).

✍️ Must-know: series R_s = ΣR; parallel 1/R_p = Σ1/R; Ohm's law V = IR; power P = VI = I²R = V²/R; terminal voltage V = ε − Ir; Wheatstone balance R1/R2 = R3/R4.

Worked examples in text: Example 6.10 (current density), Example 6.11 (resistance), Example 6.12 (equivalent resistance), Example 6.14a/6.14b (galvanometer conversions), Example 6.15 (Wheatstone), Example 6.17 (Kirchhoff two-loop). Review: cell emf 1.5 V internal 0.5 Ω on 2.5 Ω — current, terminal pd, power to resistor, power wasted; using two readings to find internal resistance & emf.
