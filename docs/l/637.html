4.2 Frictional Force

◌ Not started

Friction = a force that opposes the motion of one surface over another (a contact force). Depends on the nature of the surfaces in contact and the normal force. - Static friction: friction between two contacting surfaces at rest. Its maximum value fs,max = μs FN. - Kinetic (sliding) friction: friction while one surface slides over the other. fk = μk FN. - Coefficient of friction μ = frictional force / normal force. Static coefficient > kinetic coefficient (μs > μk), for the same pair. μ is dimensionless.

✍️ Must-know: fs ≤ μs FN (static friction adjusts up to a max); once sliding, kinetic friction fk = μk FN is generally smaller. Coefficients depend on the pair of materials (e.g. rubber on dry asphalt vs wet asphalt — wet is lower). Driving slower in the rain reduces accidents because wet roads lower friction and lengthen stopping distance.

✋ Trap: friction does not always oppose motion — it is needed to move: your shoes grip the ground (static friction lets you walk); car tyres need friction to accelerate/brake. "Friction opposes relative/sliding motion or tendency to slide" is more accurate.

Free-body diagram (FBD): a drawing that includes only the forces acting directly on the object of interest. Arrows show magnitude (length) and direction. This is the single most important step for Newton's-second-law problems.

Worked examples (all should be mastered): - Example 4.3: 8 kg block, μs=0.4, μk=0.35 on a horizontal surface. (A) force just enough to start it = μs mg = 0.4×8×9.8 = 31.4 N. (B) force to keep it accelerating at 4 m/s²: F − fk = ma ⇒ F = ma + μk mg = 8×4 + 0.35×8×9.8 = 59.4 N. - Example 4.4/4.5: 6 kg block at rest on a 30° ramp ⇒ fs = mg sin 30° = 6×9.8×0.5 ≈ 29 N; μs = tan 30° ≈ 0.577 (for block about to slide on an incline, μs = tan θ, where θ is the critical/limiting angle). - Example 4.6: two blocks m1=4 kg, m2=? (text: m2 given), pulled by 36 N string ⇒ a = 3 m/s² and tension T = m2 a = 24 N. - Example 4.7: same but with kinetic friction included. - Example 4.8: Atwood's machine — two masses (m1=4 kg, m2=6 kg) over a frictionless pulley ⇒ a = (m2−m1)g/(m1+m2), tension found from the equations. (Atwood's machine can be used to measure g.)

Tension forces: a rope/string pulls with tension T. - Vertical tension on a static hanging object: T = mg. - Vertical tension on an accelerating mass (e.g. in an elevator): if the elevator accelerates upward with a, T = m(g+a); if it moves up with constant velocity, T = mg; if the cable breaks and it falls freely, T = 0 (weightlessness). In review: elevator accelerating upward increases scale reading; free fall ⇒ reading 0.

✋ Elevator trap: scale reads the normal force. Accelerating up: N = m(g+a) (heavier). Constant velocity: N = mg. Accelerating down: N = m(g−a). Free fall (a=g down): N = 0.
