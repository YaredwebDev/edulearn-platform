4.4 The Major Trends in the Periodic Table

◌ Not started

Periodic trends: patterns in electronegativity, ionization energy, electron affinity, atomic radius, and metallic character — caused by the periodic nature of elements.

4.4.1 Atomic Radius

  • Atomic radius: one-half the distance between the nuclei of two adjacent atoms (network solids or diatomic molecules).
  • Determined largely by the strength of attraction between the nucleus and outer-shell electrons.
  • Trend down a group: atomic radius increases (more shells, higher principal quantum number, e.g., Li → Cs).
  • Trend across a period: atomic radius decreases left → right (increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer).

Example 4.4: Arrange P, Si, N in increasing atomic radius. Same group: N < P (going down increases). Same period: P < Si (going right decreases). So N < P < Si.

4.4.2 Ionization Energy

  • Ionization energy (IE): the energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state to form a cation (kJ/mol). H(g) → H⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Successive electrons require more energy (atom becomes more positive).
  • Larger radius → lower ionization energy (electrons easier to remove).
  • Trend: IE increases left → right across a period; decreases down a group.
  • Exceptions: Group IIA > IIIA and Group VA > VIA (stable fully/half-filled configurations).
  • Shielding effect: inner electrons partially cancel the nuclear charge felt by outer electrons → lower IE. Higher shielding → lower IE. This explains why IE decreases down a group.
  • Cesium has the lowest IE; Fluorine the highest (except He and Ne).

Table 4.6 (Period 2, kJ/mol): Li 520, Be 899, B 800, C 1086, N 1402, O 1314, F 1680. Table 4.7 (Group I, kJ/mol): Li 520, Na 496, K 419, Rb 408, Cs 376, Fr 396.

Example 4.5: (a) Sulfur has a smaller first IE than oxygen (same group, S's 3p electron is farther). (b) Be has a lower second IE than Li (Be⁺ loses a shielded 2s electron more easily than Li⁺ loses a 1s electron).

4.4.3 Electron Affinity

  • Electron affinity (EA): the change in energy when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom to form a negative ion (kJ/mol). X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
  • First EA: energy released → negative value (e.g., Cl = −349 kJ/mol).
  • Second EA: positive (energy needed exceeds energy gained).
  • Metals have lower electron affinities (easier to lose electrons). Nonmetals have higher EA (form stable octets).
  • Trend: EA increases left → right across a period; decreases down a group.
  • Irregularity (F vs Cl): EA of fluorine (−328) is less than chlorine (−349) because fluorine's 2p subshell is small → greater electron–electron repulsion.
  • Group IA values (kJ/mol): Li −60, Na −53, K −48, Rb −47, Cs −45.
  • Halogens (kJ/mol): F −328, Cl −349, Br −324, I −295.
  • Exceptions: Be and N have EA = 0 (stable configurations).
  • Noble gases have extremely low (near zero) electron affinity.

4.4.4 Electronegativity

  • Electronegativity (EN): the tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared pair of bonding electrons toward itself. A dimensionless tendency (not a measured energy).
  • Measured on the Pauling scale.
  • Fluorine = most electronegative (4.0); Cesium/Francium = least (0.7).
  • Trend: EN increases left → right across a period (increasing nuclear charge, smaller size); decreases down a group (added shells overcome nuclear charge).
  • Metals have EN < 2.0 and are electropositive; nonmetals are electronegative.
  • Diagonal relationship: elements in period 2 resemble the next group in period 3 (small size difference in electronegativity, e.g., Li resembles Mg).
