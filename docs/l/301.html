5.2 Equilibrium, Equilibrium Constants (KC, KP), and the Law of Mass Action

◌ Not started

5.2.1 Reversible and irreversible reactions

  • Irreversible (one-way) reactions: reactants → products essentially completely; written with a single arrow (→). E.g. C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g).
  • Reversible (two-way) reactions: proceed in both the forward and reverse directions; written with a double arrow (⇌). General form aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD. E.g. N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃; H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI.
  • Forward reaction = reactants → products; reverse (backward) reaction = products → reactants.

5.2.2 Dynamic chemical equilibrium

  • At the start the forward rate (rf) is high and the reverse rate (rr) is ~0; as the reaction runs, rf falls (reactants used) and rr rises (products form). Equilibrium is reached when rf = rr.
  • At equilibrium macroscopic properties (concentrations, pressure, temperature) no longer change, but the reaction continues at the molecular level — it is a dynamic equilibrium.
  • Equilibrium is only reached in a closed vessel.

Characteristics of equilibrium: - Reaction is reversible; no net change in concentrations; forward and reverse rates are equal; closed vessel; dynamic; concentrations of reactants and products are generally not equal (only the rates are equal).

✍️ Must-know: at equilibrium the rates of forward and reverse are equal — NOT necessarily the concentrations. Example: 2NO₂(g, dark brown) ⇌ N₂O₄(g, colourless) — the brown colour stops changing once equilibrium is set, yet NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄ interconversion continues.

5.2.3 Law of mass action and the equilibrium constant

Proposed by Guldberg and Waage (1864): the rate at which A and B combine is directly proportional to the product of their concentrations, each raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation.

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD: - rf = kf[A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ; rr = kr[C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ. - At equilibrium rf = rr → kf[A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ = kr[C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ, so:

✍️ Must-know: KC = ([C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ)/([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ). Products go in the numerator, reactants in the denominator, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient. Use molar concentrations (mol L⁻¹) for KC; use partial pressures for KP.

  • KC units: (mol L⁻¹)^[(c+d)−(a+b)]. KP units: (atm)^[(c+d)−(a+b)] (or kPa power).
  • Worked Example 5.1(4): H₂ + F₂ ⇌ 2HF; at 425 °C, [H₂]=[F₂]=4.79×10⁻⁴ M, [HF]=3.53×10⁻³ M. KC = [HF]²/([H₂][F₂]) = (3.53×10⁻³)²/(4.79×10⁻⁴ × 4.79×10⁻⁴) = 5.43.
  • Worked Example 5.2(4): 2NO + Cl₂ ⇌ 2NOCl; equilibrium partial pressures P_NOCl=1.2 atm, P_NO=5.0×10⁻² atm, P_Cl₂=3.0×10⁻¹ atm. KP = P_NOCl²/(P_NO²·P_Cl₂) = (1.2)²/((5×10⁻²)²(3×10⁻¹)) = 1.92×10² atm⁻¹.

5.2.4 Heterogeneous equilibria — omit pure solids and pure liquids

When reactants/products are in more than one phase (e.g. CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g)), do NOT include pure solids or pure liquids in the KC/KP expression (their concentrations are constant). - CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g): KC = [CO₂]; KP = P_CO₂. - NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g): KC = [NH₃][HCl]; KP = P_NH₃·P_HCl. - CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq): KC = [H⁺][HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂] (H₂O omitted).

5.2.5 Relationship between KC and KP

Using the ideal gas law, partial pressure P = (n/V)RT = CRT for each gas:

✍️ Must-know: KP = KC(RT)^Δn, where Δn = (c+d) − (a+b) for gaseous species only. If Δn = 0 then KP = KC.

Worked Example 5.4(1): 2SO₃ ⇌ 2SO₂ + O₂, KP = 1.8×10⁻³ kPa (1.8 Nm⁻²... treat as kPa), T = 427 °C = 700 K. Δn = (2+1)−2 = 1. KC = KP/(RT) → KC ≈ 3.1×10⁻⁴ (using R in matching units). Worked Example 5.4(2): CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g) with P_CO₂ = 3.87 atm. KP = 3.87. Δn = +1. KC = KP/(RT) = 3.87/(0.082×1273) = 0.037 M. (Note ΔH of reaction treated as given ~1273 K context.) Worked Example 5.4(3): H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI. Δn = 2−1−1 = 0, so KP = KC = 64. If P_H₂=0.20 atm, P_I₂=0.50 atm: P_HI² = KP·P_H₂·P_I₂ = 64×0.20×0.50 → P_HI = 2.53 atm.

5.2.6 Combining reactions — multiply the constants

If a reaction is the sum of two or more steps, the overall equilibrium constant is the product of the individual constants: K_overall = K₁ × K₂ × K₃…. Reversing a reaction gives 1/K; multiplying an equation by ½ raises K to the ½ power.

Worked Example 5.5: Find KC for ½N₂ + ½O₂ + ½Br₂ ⇌ NOBr from (i) 2NO ⇌ N₂ + O₂, K₁ = 2.4×10³⁰ and (ii) NO + ½Br₂ ⇌ NOBr, K₂ = 1.4. Reverse & halve (i): ½N₂ + ½O₂ ⇌ NO, K₁' = (1/2.4×10³⁰)^½ = 6.5×10⁻¹⁶. Overall KC = K₂ × K₁' = 1.4×6.5×10⁻¹⁶ = 9.1×10⁻¹⁶.

5.2.7 Using KC/KP — extent and direction

Predicting extent: very large KC/KP (> ~10¹⁰) → equilibrium lies far right (mainly products, reaction near completion). Very small KC/KP (< ~10⁻¹⁰) → lies far left (mainly reactants, essentially no reaction). KC > 1 favours products; KC < 1 favours reactants; KC = 1 → equal amounts.

Reaction quotient Q: same form as KC/KP but using concentrations at any moment (not necessarily at equilibrium). Compare Q with K:

✍️ Must-know: if Q = K reaction is at equilibrium; if Q < K it proceeds forward (right, → more products); if Q > K it proceeds reverse (left, → more reactants).

Worked Example 5.6: CO + Cl₂ ⇌ COCl₂, KC = 13.8. [CO]₀=2.5 M, [Cl₂]₀=1.2 M, [COCl₂]₀=5.0 M. QC = 5.0/(2.5×1.2) = 1.67. Since QC (1.67) < KC (13.8), the mixture is not at equilibrium; it proceeds forward (right) to form more product.

Calculating equilibrium concentrations (systematic steps): (1) write balanced eqn & KC/KP; (2) list initial concentrations; (3) compute Q to find direction; (4) define change x (using coefficient ratios) and write equilibrium concentrations; (5) substitute into KC/KP and solve (may be a quadratic); (6) check that the value(s) satisfy the expression; discard unreasonable x (e.g. x > initial concentration).

Worked Example 5.7: H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI, KC = 69. Start with 1 mol H₂ and 2 mol I₂ in 0.5 L → [H₂]₀=2.0 M, [I₂]₀=4.0 M, [HI]₀=0. Let x = [H₂] consumed. Equilibrium: [H₂]=2.0−x, [I₂]=4.0−x, [HI]=2x. KC = (2x)²/[(2.0−x)(4.0−x)] = 69 (textbook notes ~64–69). Solving the quadratic 60x² − 384x + 512 = 0 gives x = 1.9 M or 4.5 M; since x cannot exceed 2.0 M (initial [H₂]), x = 1.9 M. Equilibrium: [H₂] = 0.1 M, [I₂] = 2.1 M, [HI] = 3.8 M. Check: (3.8)²/(0.1×2.1) ≈ 68.8 ≈ 69 ✓.
