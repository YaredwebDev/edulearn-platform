6.2 Alcohols and Ethers

◌ Not started

6.2.1 Alcohols — definition & classification

  • Alcohols are derivatives of hydrocarbons in which one or more H atoms are replaced by a hydroxyl group (–OH). The –OH group is directly attached to a saturated (aliphatic) carbon.
  • Only ethanol is present in all alcoholic beverages. Methanol (industrial), 2-propanol etc. are different.

Classified by number of –OH groups: - Monohydric (one –OH): ethanol, 1-propanol, 1-butanol. - Dihydric (two –OH, glycols/diols): ethane-1,2-diol (ethylene glycol), 1,3-propanediol. - Trihydric (three –OH): propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol/glycerine). - Polyhydric (≥3 –OH): e.g. glycerol, longer sugar alcohols.

Classified by carbon bearing –OH (for monohydric alcohols): - Primary (1°): the –OH carbon is attached to one alkyl group (R–CH₂–OH). E.g. ethanol, 1-propanol, 1-butanol, 2-methyl-1-butanol. - Secondary (2°): –OH carbon attached to two alkyl groups (R₂CH–OH). E.g. 2-propanol, 2-butanol, 3-pentanol. - Tertiary (3°): –OH carbon attached to three alkyl groups (R₃C–OH). E.g. 2-methyl-2-propanol, 2-methyl-2-butanol.

✍️ Must-know: count alkyl groups on the carbon holding –OH to classify 1°/2°/3° alcohol. The only alcohol in drinks is ethanol; antifreeze = ethane-1,2-diol; glycerol = propane-1,2,3-triol.

6.2.2 Naming alcohols (IUPAC)

  • Common name: alkyl group name + "alcohol" (methyl alcohol, ethyl alcohol, isopropyl alcohol).
  • IUPAC: replace the terminal "e" of the alkane with "ol" (methane → methanol; ethane → ethanol). For chains of 3+, number the longest chain containing –OH from the end nearest the –OH; give the position of –OH and substituents. Examples: propan-1-ol vs propan-2-ol; 3-methylbutan-1-ol (careful: numbering from –OH end).
  • Polyhydric: keep the "e" of the alkane and add "ol" with a multiplicative prefix: 1,2-ethanediol; 1,2,3-propanetriol.
  • General formula for monohydric series: CnH2n+1OH.

6.2.3 Physical properties of alcohols

  • The –OH bond is polar (highly electronegative O), so alcohols show significant hydrogen bonding → higher melting/boiling points than hydrocarbons of comparable mass and even the low members are liquids at room temperature.
  • Solubility: lower alcohols (methanol, ethanol) are miscible with water in all proportions (H-bond with water); solubility decreases as carbon number increases.
  • Physical constants of the first six monohydric alcohols (memorise pattern): | Alcohol | mp °C | bp °C | density g/mL | |---|---|---|---| | Methanol | −97 | 64.7 | 0.792 | | Ethanol | −117 | 78.3 | 0.789 | | 1-Propanol | −126 | 97.2 | 0.804 | | 1-Butanol | −90 | 117.7 | 0.810 | | 1-Pentanol | −78.5 | 138 | 0.817 | | 1-Hexanol | −52 | 156.5 | 0.819 |

💡 Trends (Group Assignment 6.1): di/tri-hydric alcohols boil higher than mono-hydric of similar mass (more H-bonding); solubility falls with chain length; branched isomers boil lower than straight-chain isomers (less contact → weaker dispersion).

6.2.4 Preparation of alcohols (laboratory)

  • A. Acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes: add water across a double bond with dilute acid (H₂SO₄/H₃PO₄). The H of water goes to the carbon with more H atoms. (Markovnikov-style.) E.g. 2-methylpropene + H₂O → tert-butyl alcohol.
  • B. Hydrolysis of alkyl halides: R–CH₂X + NaOH (heat) → R–CH₂OH + NaX.
  • C. Hydrolysis of esters: RCOOR′ + KOH (heat) → RCOOK + R′OH (ester → acid salt + alcohol). Methods B & C are substitution reactions (one group replaced by –OH).

6.2.5 Industrial preparation of ethanol

  1. Fermentation of carbohydrates (sugar/starch/cellulose) by enzymes: - Sucrose → glucose + fructose (invertase): C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆. - Glucose → ethanol: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2CH₃CH₂OH + 2CO₂ (zymase). Fermentation tops out at ~12–15% alcohol (yeast dies); stronger drinks are made by distillation. - Starch brewing route: starch → (diastase) → maltose → (maltase) → glucose → (zymase) → ethanol + CO₂. - Ethiopia's liquor factories use molasses (sugar-industry by-product); brewers use malt (germinated barley).
  2. Catalytic hydration of ethene (main modern route): CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O(g) → CH₃CH₂OH over H₃PO₄ catalyst at ~573 K (300 °C) and ~60 atm.

✍️ Must-know (Experiment 6.1): yeast supplies zymase enzymes; ammonium phosphate/sulphate feeds the yeast (nitrogen source); CO₂ gas turns Ca(OH)₂ (limewater) milky; after ~3 days the mixture smells of ethanol; the distillate is flammable (alcohol) — it burns.

6.2.6 Chemical reactions of alcohols

Reactions can break the O–H bond or the C–O bond.

1. Cleavage of the O–H bond: - (a) Reaction with active metals (Na, K, Li, Mg): releases H₂ and forms a metal alkoxide. 2CH₃CH₂OH + 2Na → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + H₂ (ethanol → sodium ethoxide). (Test for H₂: burns with a pop.) - (b) Oxidation: - Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. - Mild oxidant (e.g. Cu at 360 °C): primary → aldehyde (ethanol → ethanal, CH₃CHO). - Strong oxidant (acidified KMnO₄ / K₂Cr₂O₇ / CrO₃–H₂SO₄): primary → first aldehyde, then carboxylic acid (ethanol → ethanal → ethanoic acid). It is hard to stop at the aldehyde with strong oxidants. - Secondary alcohol → ketone (2-propanol → propanone/acetone) with [O]/KMnO₄. - Tertiary alcohols are generally resistant to oxidation (no H on the –OH carbon).

✍️ Must-know (alcohol oxidation ladder): 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° alcohol → ketone; 3° alcohol → no reaction under normal conditions.

2. Cleavage of the C–O bond: - (a) Dehydration (removal of H₂O) → alkene. Needs concentrated H₂SO₄ (or H₃PO₄) + heat. - Ethanol → ethene at 170 °C with conc. H₂SO₄. - Dehydration is hardest for primary, easiest for tertiary (order: 3° < 2° < 1° difficulty); 2-propanol → propene at 167 °C with 85% H₃PO₄; 2-methyl-2-propanol → 2-methylpropene at 85 °C with 20% H₃PO₄. - (b) Reaction with hydrogen halides → alkyl halide: R–OH + HX → R–X + H₂O (ethanol + HBr → ethyl bromide/1-bromoethane + water).

6.2.7 Ethers — structure, naming, properties

  • Ethers = R–O–R′ (O bonded to two alkyl groups). Symmetrical if R = R′, unsymmetrical if different.
  • Common names: list the two alkyl groups (alphabetical) + "ether" → ethyl methyl ether, diethyl ether, ethyl isopropyl ether.
  • IUPAC: name as an alkane with an RO– (alkoxy) substituent: replace "yl" with "oxy" → methoxy, ethoxy, isopropoxy. Examples: 2-methoxybutane; 1-ethoxypentane.

Physical properties: ethers are polar (C–O–C with δ− on O) but cannot hydrogen-bond to each other → their boiling points are much lower than isomeric alcohols (ethanol bp 78 °C vs dimethyl ether bp −24 °C). They can hydrogen-bond with water → more soluble in water than comparable hydrocarbons.

Preparation: 1. Dehydration of alcohols: 2CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃ + H₂O at 140 °C with H₂SO₄ (vs 170 °C → ethene). Limited to primary alcohols. 2. Williamson ether synthesis: alkyl halide + alkoxide → ether + halide (R′–X + R–O⁻ → R–O–R′ + X⁻). Works for both symmetrical & unsymmetrical ethers (e.g. ethyl bromide + methoxide → ethyl methyl ether).

Chemical properties: ethers are generally unreactive (resemble hydrocarbons) — not oxidised by K₂Cr₂O₇/KMnO₄, unaffected by bases; they react with proton donors to form oxonium salts (e.g. with HBr) and strong acids cleave the C–O–C linkage on heating. Because of inertness + good solvent power, ethers are excellent solvents and were inhalation anaesthetics in medicine.
