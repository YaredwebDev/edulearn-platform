6.1 Population Ecology

◌ Not started
  • Ecology = the study of the interaction between organisms and the physical environment. Levels of ecological organization: individual → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere → biome.
  • Population = a group of interacting individuals of the same species with common characteristics, living and interbreeding within a given area.
  • Population ecology = the study of processes of interaction and change affecting the distribution and abundance of populations in the environment. Scientists use statistical measures (demographic parameters) to investigate how populations respond to environmental change.

6.1.1 Population size, density and dispersal

Population size = the total number of individuals present in a particular habitat (designated "N"). Because counting every individual is hard, scientists use sampling techniques (count in sample areas and infer the total). Methods: - Quadrat method — for immobile/slow organisms (plants, small slow-moving animals). A quadrat (square frame of wood/plastic/metal) is placed on the ground; individuals within each quadrat are counted; many quadrats are placed randomly. Formula: Population estimate = (total area A / sampled area a) × number counted n Example: A = 50 m², a = 10 m², n = 50 plants → (50/10)×50 = 250 plants. - Mark–recapture method — for mobile animals. (1) Capture & mark a sample, release; (2) capture a second sample, count marked & unmarked; (3) estimate total population by the ratio. Formula: Population estimate = (M × n) / X, where M = marked released, n = total in 2nd capture, X = marked in 2nd capture. Example: M = 60, n = 100, X = 20 → (60×100)/20 = 300 birds.

Studying the human population — census: A census = systematic collection of information from all units/individuals of a population (complete enumeration), incl. age, sex, ethnic group, religion, marital status, household, occupation, etc. Conducted (Ethiopia) at least once every ~10 years. Two methods: 1. De Facto method — the government fixes one date (often a full-moon night) and counts everyone present in the country at that moment. 2. De Jure method — enumeration over 2–3 weeks; enumerators visit households; only permanently residing persons are counted (temporary residents are not).

Population density = number of individuals within a specific area/volume, i.e., population size ÷ total land area (e.g., people per 10 km²). Density is dynamic: birth & immigration increase it; death & emigration decrease it. Example: plant A density = 27 individuals/10 m² = 2.7.

Population dispersal patterns (how individuals are distributed in space at a given time): 1. Uniform dispersion — spaced more or less evenly. 2. Random dispersion — distributed randomly, no predictable pattern. 3. Clumped dispersion — clustered in groups (most common in nature).

6.1.2 Exponential and logistic growth in populations

Population growth rate (r) = percentage rate of change in the number of individuals over a specified period; the intrinsic rate of natural increase (r = B – D, birth rate minus death rate). Basic equation: dN/dt = rN (N = population size, t = time, r = per-capita rate). r can be positive (>0 = increase), zero (=0 = no change → "zero population growth"), or negative (<0 = decrease).

Exponential growth model — growth without environmental constraints (ideal, unlimited resources); per-capita rate stays constant positive. Graph = J-shaped curve. Equation: dN/dt = rmax·N (rmax = maximum per-capita rate under ideal conditions). Doubles each generation at r=1: 100→200→400→800…102400.

Logistic growth model — growth limited by resources; per-capita rate decreases as the population approaches a maximum. Graph = S-shaped curve. The maximum sustainable population = carrying capacity (K). Equation: dN/dt = rN((K−N)/K). When N reaches K, growth levels off (ΔN → 0). Example (r=1, K=250): 100→160→218→246→250→250 (level off at K).

✍️ Must-know: exponential = J-shaped, no limits; logistic = S-shaped, includes carrying capacity K. Logistic growth levels off at carrying capacity (NOT J-shaped). Density-dependent regulation (food, territory, predation) vs density-independent (air pollution is NOT density-dependent).

6.1.3 Demographic structure

Demography describes the dynamics of a population using parameters: population size, crude death rate, population growth rate, population density, infant mortality rate, fertility rate, crude birth rate, migration, life expectancy. - Crude birth rate = number of live births per 1000 people in a year. - Crude death rate = annual number of deaths per 1000 people. - Infant mortality rate = annual deaths of children aged <1 year per 1000 live births. - Life expectancy = number of years a human can expect to live (given current mortality). - Fertility rate = measure of number of children born. - Migration = movement of people between areas; immigration = into an area (permanent); emigration = out of an area. - Age–sex structure is important because younger people are more likely to reproduce and older people have higher death rates.

Population pyramid = a graphical representation of the age and sex composition of a population (a.k.a. age pyramid or age-and-sex pyramid). Three types: 1. Expansive — young and growing; broad base, narrow top; high fertility, lower life expectancy (many third-world countries, e.g., Ethiopia). 2. Constrictive — elderly and shrinking; smaller young-age percentage; higher economic development (access to education/health care). 3. Stationary — not growing; almost equal proportions per age group, tapering at the top (developed countries with low birth rate & high quality of life). - How to draw: compute each 5-year age group (e.g., 0–4, 5–9…) as a % of total population (e.g., 20,000/2,000,000 ×100 = 1%), plot males on the left and females on the right of a central axis.

Ethiopia's population (Ethiopian Public Health Association, 2014): 42.6 million (1984), 53.5 million (1994), 73.5 million (2007), 83.7 million (2012), currently >110 million. 2007 census: 45% under age 15; only 4.8% aged 60+; women 15–49 = 23%. Ethiopia has an expansive pyramid.

Survivorship curves — graphs of the number of individuals surviving at each age interval versus time: - Type I — few offspring, much care; low early/middle mortality, most die old (humans, most mammals). - Type II — moderate offspring, some care; death rate fairly constant across life (some birds). - Type III — many offspring, little/no care; very high early mortality, few survive to adulthood (plants, invertebrates, many fish).

✍️ Must-know: a bird releasing many eggs (most eaten) but living many years = Type III survivorship. Census carried every ~10 years. Birth rate with 80 births & mid-year population 5000 = (80/5000)×1000 = 16 per 1000. Death rate 20/7000 ×1000 = ~2.8 per 1000.

6.1.4 Population regulation

Growth ends at carrying capacity due to two kinds of resistance: - Density-dependent factors — affect the population based on its density (living/biotic factors): predation, competition (intra- and interspecific), accumulation of waste, and diseases. The denser the population, the higher the mortality rate; diseases spread quickly in dense populations. - Density-independent factors — affect growth regardless of population density (non-living/abiotic factors): weather, natural disasters (earthquakes, floods), and pollution. An earthquake kills organisms whether density is high or low.
