7E.1 Adverbs and their categories

  • Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. Five main types:
  • Manner (how) — often -ly: beautifully, generously, happily, well, formally, sincerely, loudly, fluently.
  • Degree/intensity (how much / to what degree): almost, enough, hardly, just, nearly, quite, simply, so, too, really, exceptionally, undoubtedly.
  • Frequency (how often): always, never, normally, rarely, seldom, sometimes, usually, often.
  • Place (where): above, anywhere, back, below, everywhere, here, inside, nowhere, out, outside, there.
  • Time (when): already, earlier, immediately, lately, later, now, recently, soon, tomorrow, yesterday.

7E.2 Adverbial clauses of time

  • A subordinate clause showing when something happens; introduced by when, before, after, as, by the time, while, until/till, as soon as, since, whenever, no sooner…than, as long as. It can come before or after the main clause.
  • Examples: I stopped running when I saw my friend; Mimi gets excited whenever she sees a dog; I'll do my homework before I go back; By the time we paid the bill, the service resumed; Demitu hasn't stopped working since she arrived.

7E.3 Adverbial clauses of place

  • A subordinate clause showing where something happens; introduced by where, wherever, anywhere, everywhere.
  • Examples: Wherever I go, I always see wild asses; Where there is a will, there is a way; I'm not sure where she lives; That's the town where I was born; He followed her wherever she went.

7E.4 Adjectives — order of adjectives

  • Attributive adjectives come before the noun (some are attributive-only: chief, elder, former, indoor, inner, main, mere, only, outdoor, outer, particular, principal, sole, upper).
  • Predicative adjectives come after a linking verb (be, appear, look, taste, etc.) and act as complement: The girl looks happy. Many begin with a- and are predicative-only: ablaze, adrift, afloat, afraid, aghast, alert, alike, alive, alone, aloof, ashamed, asleep, averse, aware, awake. Others: content, far, glad, ill, near, pleased, poorly, ready, sorry, unwell, upset, well.
  • Usual order when several adjectives modify one noun: Determiner → Quantity/Number → Opinion/Quality → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Noun. E.g. that little old round red box; a beautiful big old green antique car; my sister's beautiful big white cotton dress.

7E.5 Modal auxiliary verbs

  • Modals add specific meaning to the main verb and can have several meanings depending on context.
  • Can — ability/permission: I can speak English; Can I come in?
  • May — probability/permission: We may visit Aksum; May I go?
  • Must — responsibility/assumption: You must study; She must be sick.
  • Would — past habit / present unreal / polite: I would play tennis; I would buy a car.
  • Shall — educated/formal expression, contractual obligation: I shall go now; The company shall pay on 2 Jan.
  • Could — unreal or past ability: I could go if I had time; She could play the piano, but no more now.
  • Will — future: Alemu will go to Hawassa.
  • Might — small probability: I might go to Langano.
  • Should — recommendation: You should go to the doctor.
  • Ought to — formal recommendation: You ought to know about safety rules.

7E.6 Types & forms of questions

  • WH-questions elicit information. What = a thing; which = a choice/option; who/whom = a person (pronominal); whose = a person (a noun follows); why = reason; how = manner/amount/means; when = time; where = place. Word order: Q-word → operator/auxiliary → subject → lexical verb → adjunct: When do we take our leave?
  • Yes/No questions (polar/general questions) expect a two-way "yes/no" answer. Formed by moving the subject and auxiliary (do/does/did/is/are/has/have/can…). Example: Did you go to the training day?

7E.7 Tag questions

  • A tag question = a short (usually two-word) question at the end of a statement, used to confirm information or ask for new information; common in spoken English.
  • Basic rule: negative tag after an affirmative statement; affirmative tag after a negative statement — You cooked this yourself, didn't you? / You haven't done your homework, have you?
  • Imperatives: use won't for invitations, can/can't/will/would for orders: Give me a hand, will you? Hurry up, won't you? Close the door, would you? Keep quiet, can't you? Don't do that again, will you?
  • Negative adverbs (never, rarely, seldom, hardly, barely, scarcely) make a statement negative → positive tag: She hardly completes her homework on time, does she?
  • Negative subjects → often affirmative–affirmative: Nothing works, does it? None of them can help, can they?
  • Somebody/someone/everybody/nobody/no-one → tag pronoun they: Someone told you, didn't they? No-one called, did they?
  • Indefinite impersonal one → tag with one / you / they: One shouldn't judge too quickly, should one?
  • Special forms: I am your friend, aren't I? Let's go, shall we? Don't forget, will you?
  • Intonation: falling tone = confident; rising tone = checking/not fully sure.
