1.2 Dalton's Atomic Theory and the Modern Atomic Theory

◌ Not started

Dalton's theory was based on three fundamental laws of chemical combination: 1. Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier) — in a chemical reaction, mass is neither created nor destroyed. 2. Law of definite (constant) proportion (Proust) — a compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass (e.g. water is always 11.2% H and 88.8% O). 3. Law of multiple proportions (Dalton) — when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers.

The three laws as the basis of Dalton's atomic theory.

Example 1.1 (worked): Water is 11.2% H, 88.8% O by mass. - 18.0 g water → H = 0.112×18.0 = 2.02 g, O = 0.888×18.0 = 15.98 g. - Law of multiple proportions: for nitrogen oxides, masses of N combining with 1 g O were 1.750 g (A), 0.8750 g (B), 0.4375 g (C); ratios A/B = 2, B/C = 2 → small whole numbers, so the data obey the law of multiple proportions.

✍️ Must-know: Dalton proposed three laws of chemical combination total (conservation, definite proportions, and multiple proportions — the third is his own).

1.2.1 Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory

(Recall from Grade 9 — the five postulates, e.g. matter is made of indivisible atoms; atoms of a given element are identical; atoms of different elements differ; atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios; atoms are neither created nor destroyed in reactions.)

1.2.2 Postulates of the Modern Atomic Theory

The modern atomic theory corrects Dalton: - Atoms are divisible into subatomic particles (protons, neutrons, electrons). - Atoms of the same element can differ (→ isotopes). - Atoms can be transformed (nuclear reactions) and interconverted. - Not all atoms of an element have identical mass (isotopes).

Limitations of Dalton's theory (that modern theory fixes): atoms are not indivisible/indestructible; atoms of the same element may differ in mass (isotopes); atoms can be created/destroyed in nuclear reactions.

✍️ Must-know: The modern atomic theory is generally taken to begin with Dalton, but the modern physical structure of the atom began with J.J. Thomson's discovery of the electron (1897).
