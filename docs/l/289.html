3.3 The Gaseous State

◌ Not started

3.3.1 Kinetic Molecular Theory of Gases — assumptions

  1. Gas = particles in constant, straight-line motion.
  2. Gas particles do not attract or repel each other; collisions are elastic (like pool balls).
  3. Particle volume is negligible compared with the total gas volume (lots of empty space) → explains easy compressibility and mixing.
  4. Average kinetic energy ∝ kelvin temperature. KE = ½mv² (Eq. 3.1).

Gas properties recap: highly compressible, pressurise equally in all directions, very low density, volume/shape = container, mix evenly & completely.

3.3.2 Pressure, Volume, Temperature — units

  • Pressure = Force / Area. SI unit Pascal (Pa); 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  • 1 atm = 760 mmHg = 76 cmHg = 760 torr = 101,325 Pa = 101.325 kPa.
  • Volume: SI unit m³; also cm³, dm³. 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³; 1 dm³ = 10⁻³ m³ = 1 L; 1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm³ = 1 dm³.
  • Temperature: °F, °C, K. K = °C + 273; °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9.
  • STP (standard temperature and pressure) = 1 atm and 0 °C (273 K). Molar volume = 22.4 L for one mole of any (ideal) gas at STP.

✍️ Must-know conversions: K = °C + 273; 1 atm = 760 torr = 760 mmHg = 76 cmHg = 101.325 kPa = 101,325 Pa; 1 L = 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1000 mL; 1 mol gas = 22.4 L at STP.

3.3.3 Boyle's Law (Robert Boyle, 1662) — constant T, n

At constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure.

  • V ∝ 1/P; PV = k; P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
  • Doubling P halves V; tripling P gives one-third the volume. Data in Table 3.1 give constant PV = 600 (e.g. 1.0 atm × 600 mL = 600).
  • Graph of P vs V = hyperbola; graph of P vs 1/V = a straight line through the origin.

Worked Example 3.1: 1.5 L of SO₂ at 5.6 kPa → new pressure 15.0 kPa (constant T). V₂ = P₁V₁/P₂ = (5.6 kPa × 1.5 L)/(15.0 kPa) = 0.56 L.

💡 Note: Weather balloon expands as it rises because external pressure decreases (constant temperature) → gas expands (Boyle).

3.3.4 Charles's Law (Jacques Charles, 1787) — constant P, n

At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas varies directly with the kelvin temperature.

  • V ∝ T; V/T = k; V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (T always in kelvin).
  • All gases extrapolate to V = 0 at −273 °C, defined as 0 K (absolute zero) — the theoretical lowest attainable temperature, never practically reached.
  • Doubling kelvin temperature doubles the volume.

Worked Example 3.2(1): 2.58 L at 15 °C (288 K) → volume at 38 °C (311 K): V₂ = V₁T₂/T₁ = 2.58 × 311/288 = 2.78 L. Worked Example 3.2(2): 2.80 L at unknown T → 2.57 L at 0 °C (273 K): T₁ = T₂V₁/V₂ = 273×2.80/2.57 = 297.43 K = 24.43 °C.

3.3.5 Gay-Lussac's Law (pressure–temperature law) — constant V, n

At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas varies directly with the kelvin temperature.

  • P ∝ T; P/T = k; P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂.
  • Explains tyre pressure rising on a hot day / deodorant-can warning labels (keep away from heat/fire).

Worked Example 3.3: deodorant can gas at 3 atm, 25 °C (298 K) heated to 845 °C (1118 K): P₂ = P₁T₂/T₁ = 3.0×1118/298 = 11.23 atm.

3.3.6 The Combined Gas Law — one equation for all three

For a fixed mass of gas: PV/T = k, so P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂.

Worked Example 3.4: 12.0 L at 240 °C (513 K), 80.0 kPa → temperature when V = 15.0 L and P = 107.0 kPa: T₂ = P₂V₂T₁/(P₁V₁) = (107×15×513)/(80×12) = 857.67 K ≈ 858 K = 585 °C.

3.3.7 Avogadro's Law (1811) — constant P, T

At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of moles.

  • V ∝ n; V/n = k; V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂.
  • Molar volume: 1 mol of any ideal gas = 22.4 L at STP.

Worked Example 3.5: 12.2 L of 0.50 mol O₂ all converted to O₃ (3O₂ → 2O₃): - mol O₃ = 0.50 × (2/3) = 0.33 mol; V₂ = V₁n₂/n₁ = 12.2 × 0.33/0.5 = 8.0 L.

3.3.8 The Ideal Gas Equation

  • An ideal gas is a hypothetical gas that obeys all gas laws exactly; real gases obey them closely only at high temperature and low pressure (particles very far apart, negligible interactions/volume).
  • Combining Boyle (V ∝ 1/P), Charles (V ∝ T) and Avogadro (V ∝ n): V = RnT/P, i.e. the ideal gas law PV = nRT.
  • R (gas constant): computed at STP → R = PV/nT = (1 atm × 22.4 L)/(1 mol × 273 K) = 0.082 atm·L·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹.

Worked Example 3.6: pressure of 54.0 g Xe in 1.00 L at 20.0 °C (molar mass 131.3 g/mol). Use P = nRT/V with n = m/M = 54.0/131.3 = 0.411 mol; T = 293 K: P = (0.411×0.082×293)/1.00 = 9.88 atm. Worked (molar mass): 0.280 L of gas weighs 0.400 g at STP. M = mRT/PV = (0.400×0.082×273)/(1×0.280) = 31.98 g/mol (≈ O₂).

✍️ Must-know: PV = nRT with R = 0.082 atm·L/mol·K; n = m/M; always convert °C → K.

3.3.9 Graham's Law of Diffusion (Thomas Graham, 1829)

  • Diffusion = the spreading of gas molecules throughout a container. (Effusion relates to escape through a small hole — helium balloons deflate because H₂/He leak through tiny pores faster than air.)

    Graham's law: at constant T and P, the rate of diffusion r is inversely proportional to the square root of the density d (or molar mass M) of the gas.

  • r₁/r₂ = √(d₂/d₁) = √(M₂/M₁). Lighter gases diffuse faster.

  • Since rate ∝ 1/time: t₁/t₂ = √(M₁/M₂) (time is proportional to √M).

Worked Example 3.7(1): O₂ (M=32) vs CO₂ (M=44): r(O₂)/r(CO₂) = √(44/32) = 1.17 → O₂ diffuses 1.17× faster. Worked Example 3.7(2): CH₄ diffuses twice as fast as unknown X. r_CH₄/r_x = 2 = √(M_x/M_CH₄) → M_x = 2²×16 = 64 g/mol (SO₂).

✋ Trap: Graham's ratio is √(M of the other gas / M of this gas) — the heavier gas is in the numerator when you ask "how much faster is the light one". Rates are inverse to √M; diffusion times are directly proportional to √M.
