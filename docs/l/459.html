8E.1 Adverbial clause of manner

  • A clause = a group of words with a subject and predicate forming part of a complex/compound sentence. An adverbial clause is a dependent clause that functions as an adverb (modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb).
  • An adverbial clause of manner describes how / in what manner something occurs, to what degree, or how it compares. Common conjunctions: like, as, as…as, as if, the way.
  • He sings like he wants to be a rock star.
  • The teary-eyed friends embraced as long-lost siblings would.
  • She looked as if she could jump up and dance at any moment.

8E.2 Adverb clauses of comparison

  • Introduced by as…as (positive/equality) or by than / the + comparative (comparative degree; add -er for one/two-syllable words).
  • Examples: She is older than her husband; He works harder than I do; She earns more than her brother.
  • The + comparative…the + comparative: The sooner it is done, the better you feel; The more one gets, the more one wants; The higher you ascend, the colder it becomes.
  • After than, the verb is often understood/omitted: Nobody loves you more than I (do). In informal style use an object pronoun after as/than when no verb follows: She earns more than me.
  • Common comparative errors to avoid: more healthiest, the most complete vs most misuse, cleverer than intelligent vs cleverer…, using best for two items (better), some in questions, etc.

8E.3 Passive voice (all tenses)

  • Passive voice is used when the focus is on the action or when the doer is not important/known: A letter was written. It is also more polite when avoiding blame: A car was broken (vs You broke the car).
  • Form: Subject + appropriate form of to be + past participle.
  • Changing active → passive: (1) the active object becomes the passive subject; (2) use to be in the tense of the active verb + past participle; (3) the active subject becomes the object or is dropped (by…).
  • Examples across tenses: A few well-chosen words convey a great deal → A great deal of meaning is conveyed by a few well-chosen words (present); The city is disposing of waste → Waste is being disposed of (present continuous); has disposed → have been disposed of (present perfect); Ali repaired the car → The car was repaired by Ali (past); was helping → was being helped (past continuous); had repaired → had been repaired (past perfect); will finish → will be finished (future); will have completed → will have been completed (future perfect), etc.

8E.4 Quantifiers

  • Quantifiers = words that describe quantity — how much (uncountable) or how many (countable) of a noun. They behave differently in affirmative/negative/interrogative sentences.
  • Large quantity: much, many, too many, a number of, numerous, plenty of, a lot of, lots of. Small quantity: few / a few (countable), little / a little (uncountable).
  • Countable only: a few, few, a number of, several, many. Uncountable only: a little, little, a bit of, a great deal of, a large amount of, much. Both: no, none, some (of), any, a lot of, lots of, plenty of, enough.
  • few vs a few / little vs a little: Without the article, few/little are negative (not much/not many, less than hoped): Few of my friends were there, so I was disappointed; Hurry up, there's little time left. With a, they are positive (some, at least, enough): A few of my friends were there, so I was happy; We have a little time to spare.
