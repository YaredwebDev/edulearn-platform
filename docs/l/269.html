1.5 Electromagnetic Radiation and Atomic Spectra

◌ Not started

1.5.1 Electromagnetic Radiation (EMR)

  • Maxwell (1873): light = electromagnetic waves. An EM wave has an electric and a magnetic component vibrating in mutually perpendicular planes.
  • Three primary characteristics:
  • Wavelength (λ): distance the wave travels in one cycle; units m, nm, pm, Å.
  • Frequency (ν): number of cycles per second; units s⁻¹ = hertz (Hz).
  • Speed (c): in a vacuum, EM waves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s (the speed of light).
  • Relationship: c = λν (Equation 1.1).
  • The full range of frequencies = the electromagnetic spectrum. A rainbow = continuous spectrum. Visible light: red (λ ≈ 750 nm) to violet (λ ≈ 380 nm).

Example 1.3 (worked): Addis Ababa National Radio broadcasts at 2400 kHz → λ = c/ν = (3.00×10⁸)/(2.4×10⁶) = 125.0 m.

1.5.2 The Quantum Theory and the Photon

  • Max Planck (1900): energy, like matter, is discontinuous (quantized). Atoms/molecules emit/absorb energy in discrete "packets" called quanta. The energy of a quantum is proportional to frequency: E = hν (Equation 1.2), h = Planck's constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.
  • Since ν = c/λ: E = hc/λ (Equation 1.3). Energy is emitted/absorbed in integral multiples of hν (hν, 2hν, 3hν…).
  • Photons = particles/packets of light energy.

Example 1.4 (worked): blue light 600 nm from CuCl: E = hν = h(c/λ) = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 0.50×10¹⁵ = 3.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

The Photoelectric Effect (Einstein, 1905): - Electrons are ejected from a metal surface when light of at least a certain minimum frequency (threshold frequency, ν₀) shines on it. - Photons of energy hν; minimum energy to remove an electron E₀ = hν₀ (Eq. 1.4). - If ν < ν₀ no electron is emitted regardless of intensity. If ν > ν₀, excess energy becomes electron kinetic energy: KE_e = ½mv² = hν − hν₀ (Equation 1.5). - Intensity = number of photons; greater intensity (with ν>ν₀) releases more electrons. - Einstein (relativity): E = mc², so m = E/c² (Eq. 1.6–1.7). Energy has mass. Photon energy E_photon = hc/λ, so photon apparent mass m = h/(cλ) (Eq. 1.9). - Dual nature of light: EMR shows both wave and particle properties.

Example 1.5 (worked): photoelectrons KE = 1.5×10⁻²⁰ J, λ = 750 nm → ν = 4.0×10¹⁴ s⁻¹; ν₀ = 3.77×10¹⁴ s⁻¹; λ₀ ≈ 796 nm.

1.5.3 Atomic (line) Spectra

  • Passing an electric discharge through a gas excites the atoms; as electrons return to lower states, atoms emit light.
  • When this light passes through a prism, we see a series of discrete lines (not a rainbow) = the element's atomic/emission (line) spectrum. These wavelengths are characteristic of the element → used for identification.
  • A transition between two energy levels emits one photon with ΔE = hν = hc/λ.

1.5.4 The Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom (1913)

Bohr's assumptions: 1. Electron travels in a circular orbit around the nucleus. 2. Electron energy is proportional to its distance from the nucleus (further = more energy). 3. Only certain orbits (energies) are allowed — orbits are quantized. 4. Allowed orbits are those where the electron's angular momentum is an integral multiple of h/2π (quantum condition): mvr = nh/2π (Eq. 1.12). 5. While in a given orbit, the electron neither gains nor loses energy. 6. Electron absorbs energy (light) to move to a higher orbit, and emits light to fall to a lower orbit; the photon energy equals the difference between the two orbit energies.

Orbit radius: from balancing centripetal & centrifugal forces and the quantum condition, r = n²a₀, with a₀ = 0.53 Å (the first Bohr radius). So r = (0.53 Å)n². Bigger n = farther from nucleus.

Electron energy: Eₙ = −R_H/n², with Rydberg constant R_H = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J, i.e. Eₙ = −(2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J)/n² (Eq. 1.16). (Negative sign = electron bound, energy below that of a free electron = 0.)

  • n = 1 = ground state (most negative = most stable).
  • n = 2, 3… = excited states.
  • Example: E₅ = −(2.18×10⁻¹⁸)/25 = −8.72×10⁻²⁰ J; orbit radius for n=5: r = (0.53)(25) = 13.25 Å ≈ 1.52 nm.

Energy change on transition: ΔE = E_f − E_i = −R_H(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²) (Eq. 1.18), and ΔE = hν (Eq. 1.19). - If n_f > n_i: ΔE positive → energy absorbed. - If n_i > n_f: ΔE negative → energy emitted.

Example 1.7 (worked): electron from n=3 to n=2 emits ΔE = 3.03×10⁻¹⁹ J; wavelength λ = hc/ΔE = 656 nm.

Hydrogen spectral series (Table 1.2): | Series | n_f | n_i | Region | |---|---|---|---| | Lyman | 1 | 2,3,4… | Ultraviolet | | Balmer | 2 | 3,4,5… | Visible & UV | | Paschen | 3 | 4,5,6… | Infrared | | Brackett | 4 | 5,6,7… | Infrared |

Limitations of the Bohr model: - Cannot explain spectra of multi-electron atoms (not even helium). - Cannot explain further splitting of hydrogen lines in a magnetic field. - Assumes known radius and orbit, impossible per Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

1.5.5 The Wave-Particle Duality of Matter (de Broglie, 1924)

  • Louis de Broglie: if energy is particle-like, perhaps matter is wavelike. Combining E=mc² with E=hν=hc/λ he derived the de Broglie wavelength for any particle of mass m moving at speed v: λ = h/(mv) (Equation 1.20).
  • Matter & energy show both wave and particle character; the distinction disappears at the atomic level (wave-particle duality). Macroscopic objects have undetectably tiny wavelengths.

Example 1.8 (worked): electron at 1.00×10⁶ m/s → λ = h/mv = 0.728 nm.

✋ Trap: λ = h/mv uses the particle's momentum (mv). A larger, heavier ball has a much smaller wavelength than a light fast electron.
