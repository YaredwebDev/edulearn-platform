5.2 Ionic Bonding

5.2.1 Formation of Ionic Bonding

What are ions?

  • Ion: any atom or molecule with a net positive or negative electric charge.
  • Net electric charge = number of protons − number of electrons.
  • Ions are highly reactive; usually gaseous; form when salts interact with solvents.

Types of ions:

  • Cations: atoms/groups with more protons than electrons → positive charge (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺).
  • Anions: atoms/groups with more electrons than protons → negative charge (Cl⁻, I⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻).

  • Ionization: the process of a neutral atom losing or gaining valence electrons.
  • General rule of ion charges: Group IA metals = 1+, Group IIA = 2+, Group IIIA = 3+, Group VIIA nonmetals = −1. (Groups IVA–VIA usually don't form ionic bonds.)
  • Ionic bond: a bond formed by two oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic force. The process = ionic bonding.
  • Ionic bonds form between a metal and a nonmetal (large electronegativity difference).
Note: Example (NaCl): Na (2,8,1) loses 1 electron → Na⁺ (2,8); Cl gains it → Cl⁻ (2,8,8). Electrostatic attraction → NaCl (table salt).
Note: Example (CaCl₂): Ca (2,8,8,2) loses 2 → Ca²⁺; two Cl atoms each gain 1 → 2Cl⁻; CaCl₂.

5.2.2 Lewis Formulas of Ionic Compounds

  • Lewis (electron-dot) formula: proposed by Gilbert N. Lewis (1916).
  • The chemical symbol represents the atom; dots around it represent the valence electrons (max 2 per side, placed right/left/above/below).

5.2.3 General Properties of Ionic Compounds

  1. Form crystals — exist as crystalline solids; ions (not molecules) held by strong electrostatic forces; cation/anion alternate in a regular 3-D structure (e.g., NaCl crystal).
  2. High melting and boiling points — much heat needed to break the strong attractions (NaCl melts ~800 °C, boils ~1465 °C).
  3. Hard and brittle — striking shifts ions so like charges meet → repulsion → crystal shatters.
  4. Soluble in polar solvents — "like dissolves like"; water is a polar solvent. (Many carbonate, oxalate, phosphate, sulphide, hydroxide salts are insoluble.)
  5. Conduct electricity when dissolved or molten — free ions move to electrodes (cations → cathode, anions → anode). Solid ionic compounds do NOT conduct (ions not free). NaCl(molten) → Na⁺ + Cl⁻.
  6. High density — ions packed closely by electrostatic attraction.
Note: Experiment 5.1 (Conductivity): Distilled water and solid table salt do NOT light the bulb; a water solution of table salt DOES (free ions conduct).
