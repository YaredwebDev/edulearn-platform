6.4 Carboxylic Acids

◌ Not started

6.4.1 The carboxyl group & classification

  • Carboxyl group (–COOH or –CO₂H) = a carbonyl (C=O) plus a hydroxyl (–OH) on the same carbon.
  • Saturated monocarboxylic acids: R–COOH (R = H or alkyl). General formula: CnH2n+1COOH.
  • Aromatic carboxylic acids: R = aryl group (e.g. benzoic acid, benzenecarboxylic acid).
  • Di/tricarboxylic acids: two/three –COOH groups. Dicarboxylic examples: ethanedioic (oxalic), propanedioic (malonic), butanedioic (succinic), pentanedioic (glutaric), hexanedioic (adipic) acid. Tricarboxylic: citric acid (typical; in citrus fruits).
  • First three saturated monocarboxylic acids: methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic acid; benzoic acid = simplest aromatic.

💡 In nature (Activity 6.8): citric acid (citrus), ethanoic/acetic (vinegar), ascorbic (vitamin C, many fruits), malic (apples), tartaric (grapes/wine). "Tella"/"Tej" turning sour = ethanol oxidised to ethanoic acid by air/bacteria.

6.4.2 Naming carboxylic acids

  • Common names (from natural sources, end in "–ic acid"): HCOOH = formic acid (ants, Formica); CH₃COOH = acetic acid (vinegar, Acetum); propionic acid (basic fat, Propio); butyric acid (rancid butter, Butyrum); valeric acid (valerian herb); caproic acid (goat, Caper). Branched/substituted acids use Greek letters α, β, γ, δ (= C2, C3, C4, C5): α-chloropropionic acid, α,γ-dimethylvaleric acid.
  • Dicarboxylic common names: oxalic (ethanedioic), malonic (propanedioic), succinic (butanedioic), glutaric (pentanedioic), adipic (hexanedioic), pimelic (heptanedioic).
  • Aromatic common names use o-, m-, p- prefixes (ortho/meta/para): o- and p- positions relative to –COOH; e.g. m-chlorobenzoic acid; o,p-dimethylbenzoic acid.
  • IUPAC naming: replace terminal "e" of alkane with "–oic acid" (methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic, butanoic, pentanoic, hexanoic…). Number from the carboxyl carbon (C-1). Dicarboxylic acids = "alkanedioic acid". Aromatic = benzenecarboxylic acid derivatives, numbered 1–6 with –COOH as C-1.
  • IUPAC examples: CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–COOH = 3-methylbutanoic acid; CH₃–CH₂CH(Cl)–CH(CH₃)–COOH = 3-chloro-2-methylpentanoic acid; HOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOH = butanedioic acid.

6.4.3 Physical properties of carboxylic acids

  • State: lower aliphatic acids (up to ~9 C) are liquids; higher members are waxy solids; benzoic acid is a colourless solid.
  • Odour: sharp/irritating (methanoic, ethanoic) → unpleasant (butanoic, pentanoic, hexanoic).
  • Melting & boiling points: the highest of comparable hydrocarbons/alcohols/aldehydes/ketones because carboxylic acids form dimers held by TWO hydrogen bonds (each –OH H-donates to the other molecule's C=O O). Bp comparison: 2-methyl-1-butene 31 °C < 2-butanone 80 °C < 2-butanol 99 °C < propanoic acid 141 °C.
  • Solubility: acids with ≤ 4 carbons are miscible with water (H-bond with water); solubility falls with molecular mass. Acetic acid = glacial form when anhydrous.
  • Table highlights: methanoic bp 100.5 °C, ethanoic 118, propanoic 141, butanoic 164, pentanoic 187, hexanoic 205 °C (bp rises with chain length); solubility falls after butanoic.

✍️ Must-know bp/solubility ranking: carboxylic acids (H-bonded dimers) have the highest boiling points among similar-mass organic molecules; acids ≤4 C are water-miscible; longer-chain acids are waxy, nearly odourless solids.

6.4.4 Chemical reactions of carboxylic acids

i. Reaction as an acid (cleavage of O–H → carboxylate ion + H⁺/H₃O⁺; carboxylic acids are weak acids, only partially ionised): - With active metals (Na, K, Mg, Ca) → metal carboxylate + H₂: 2CH₃COOH + 2Na → 2CH₃COO⁻Na⁺ + H₂. - With strong bases (NaOH/KOH) → metal carboxylate + water (neutralisation). - With weak bases (carbonates/bicarbonates) → salt + water + CO₂ (fizzing): with Na₂CO₃ or NaHCO₃. - With ammonia → ammonium carboxylate. - Salt naming: metal name + acid ending "–ic acid"→"–ate" (ethanoic acid → sodium ethanoate).

ii. Formation of esters (esterification): carboxylic acid + alcohol heated with conc. H₂SO₄ → ester + water. The –OH comes from the acid and the –H from the alcohol. RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O.

✋ Trap: in esterification the oxygen in the water comes from the acid's –OH (and H from the alcohol) — write the product ester keeping the alcohol's alkoxy group (–OR′) on the carbonyl carbon.

iii. Preparation by oxidation: - Primary alcohols → carboxylic acids with K₂Cr₂O₇ or KMnO₄ (ethanol → ethanoic acid). - Alkylbenzenes → aromatic acids (toluene → benzoic acid with KMnO₄/OH⁻, heat).

6.4.5 Acetic (ethanoic) acid & industrial vinegar

  • Acetic acid is a food preservative and key industrial chemical. Laboratory prep: oxidation of ethanol with KMnO₄, or refluxing ethanol over Na₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄ (Experiment 6.3: the orange dichromate turns green as Cr is reduced; product turns blue litmus red and fizzes with Na₂CO₃ → it is an acid).
  • Industrial (Quick Vinegar Fermentation Process): ethanol trickled through wood-shavings (in old vinegar) with air pumped in; bacteria oxidise ethanol → acetic acid. Max ~10% acetic acid, concentrated by fractionation to glacial acetic acid (anhydrous).
  • Uses: solvent and starting material for acetates/acetic anhydride/vinyl acetate (paints, adhesives); vinegar is ~8–10% acetic acid; long-chain carboxylic acids → soaps, detergents, shampoos.

6.4.6 Fatty acids

Fatty acids = carboxylic acids with long hydrocarbon chains. Most natural ones have an even number of carbons and are unbranched. They are saturated (no C=C) or unsaturated (have C=C). In natural unsaturated fatty acids the double bonds are never conjugated — they are separated by a methylene (–CH₂–) group.

Common naturally occurring fatty acids (memorise C numbers / key mps): | #C | Saturated | mp °C | |---|---|---| | 12 | Lauric acid | 44 | | 14 | Myristic acid | 58 | | 16 | Palmitic acid | 63 | | 18 | Stearic acid | 69 | | 20 | Arachidic acid | 77 | | #C | Unsaturated | mp °C | |---|---|---| | 16 | Palmitoleic acid (1 C=C) | 0 | | 18 | Oleic acid (1 C=C) | 13 | | 18 | Linoleic acid (2 C=C) | −5 | | 18 | Linolenic acid (3 C=C) | −11 |

Trends: saturated-acid melting point rises with molecular weight (more van der Waals). Unsaturated acids melt much lower than saturated acids of similar mass (the cis double bond kinks the chain, less packing). Among unsaturated, mp falls as the number of double bonds increases (oleic 13 → linoleic −5 → linolenic −11 °C).

✍️ Must-know: an 18-C chain melts at 69 °C if saturated (stearic), 13 °C with one double bond (oleic), −5 °C with two (linoleic), −11 °C with three (linolenic). Unsaturation lowers melting point.
