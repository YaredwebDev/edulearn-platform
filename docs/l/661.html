7.2 Radioactivity

◌ Not started

Radioactivity = the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei to form more energetically stable nuclei. Radioactive decay is a highly exoergic, statistically random process in which a small amount of mass is converted to energy.

(History: Marie & Pierre Curie won the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on radioactivity; Marie later discovered polonium and radium and won a second Nobel (Chemistry, 1911) — first person with two Nobel Prizes. She died of aplastic anaemia from radiation exposure.)

The three types of nuclear radiation: 1. Alpha (α): a helium nucleus (²He⁴ = 2 protons + 2 neutrons), charge +2e. Low penetration (stopped by paper/skin). Mass number decreases by 4, atomic number by 2. High ionising power. 2. Beta (β): a fast electron (β⁻) (or positron β⁺) emitted when a neutron becomes a proton (β⁻). Charge ∓e. Moderate penetration (stopped by a few mm of aluminium). Atomic number changes by ±1, mass number unchanged. Moderate ionising power. 3. Gamma (γ): high-energy electromagnetic radiation (photons). No charge, no rest mass. Very penetrating (needs thick lead/concrete). Low ionising power but dangerous from long-term exposure.

✋ Trap (ionisation vs penetration are opposite): α is most ionising but least penetrating; γ is least ionising but most penetrating. β is in between. This is a favourite exam question.

Alpha decay equation (X→Y): ᴬzX → ᴬ⁻⁴ z₋₂Y + ⁴₂He (α). Beta⁻ decay: ᴬzX → ᴬ z₊₁Y + ⁰₋₁e (β⁻) (+ antineutrino). Gamma: γ accompanies α or β decay; no change in A or Z.

Dangers of ionising radiation depend on: the type of radiation; sensitivity of tissues/organs; manner and length of exposure; isotopes involved; and characteristics of the person (age, sex, health). - Inside the body: α is highly ionising and very dangerous (radiation poisoning & cancer possible); β moderate; γ minimal but long-term cancer danger. - Outside the body: α is absorbed by dead skin cells (little danger from outside); β moderate; γ most penetrating (shielding needed).

Doses & units: - Absorbed dose: energy deposited per unit mass. Unit gray (Gy): 1 Gy = 1 J/kg. - Effective/equivalent dose (biological effect): unit sievert (Sv) = 1 J/kg of biological effect. - Doses above ~3 Gy can be fatal; above ~6 Gy almost certainly fatal.

Safety precautions with radioactive sources (schools): handle with tongs/forceps never bare hands; keep at arm's length pointing away from the body; keep as far as possible from the eyes; wash hands after experiments and before eating.

Radiation detectors: devices measuring ionisation. Examples: scintillator (emits light when struck), gaseous ionisation detectors, Geiger(-Müller) counter/tube (detects all three types; a thin-window tube can detect α, metal cover can separate β from γ; aluminium stops β, lead stops γ).

Half-life and decay: - Decay constant λ = the probability of decay per unit time (unit s⁻¹); also called disintegration/exponential-decay constant. - Activity A = decay rate = −dN/dt = λN; SI unit Becquerel (Bq) = 1 decay/s; also curie (Ci). - Exponential decay: N = N₀ e^(−λt). - Half-life (T½): the time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay; related to the decay constant by T½ = ln2/λ = 0.693/λ.

✍️ Must-know: N = N₀e^(−λt); A = λN; T½ = 0.693/λ. Activity falls to half each half-life. Example 7.3: activity of 1 g of Ra-226 (half-life 1590 yr) and strontium-90 remaining after a given number of half-lives.
