3.2 Fundamental Laws of Chemical Reactions

  • Law (scientific law): a generalized statement, based on repeated experiments/observations, that describes or predicts natural phenomena. It does not explain why (that's a theory).

3.2.1 The Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Discovered by Antoine Lavoisier (1789). Also called the "Law of Indestructibility of Matter."
  • Statement: In any closed system, mass is neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions. The total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
Note: Example: Reactant 1 + Reactant 2 → Product 1 + Product 2 (mass reactants = mass products).
Note: Problem: 224.4 g KOH + 88.0 g CO₂ → K₂CO₃ + 36.0 g H₂O. Mass of K₂CO₃ = (224.4 + 88.0) − 36.0 = 276.4 g.

Experiments: AgNO₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + HNO₃(l); Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq).

3.2.2 The Law of Definite Proportions (Proust's Law / Law of Constant Composition)

  • Proposed by Joseph Louis Proust.
  • Statement: every chemical compound contains fixed and constant proportions (by mass) of its constituent elements.
Note: Example: H₂O always has H:O by mass = 1:8 (2 g H : 16 g O), whatever the amount of water.
Note: CO₂ has C:O by mass = 12:32 = 3:8.

Molecular weight = sum of atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule (H=1, C=12, N=14, O=16).

  • e.g., NH₄⁺ = 14 + (1×4) = 18.

Mole: amount of substance containing as many elementary entities as atoms in 0.012 kg of carbon-12. Symbol "mol."

  • Number of moles = given mass / molecular mass. (2 g NH₄⁺ = 2/18 = 0.11 mol)

3.2.3 The Law of Multiple Proportions

  • Statement: when two different compounds form from the same two elements, the masses of one element reacting with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers.
Note: Example (CO & CO₂): 1.33 g O in CO, 2.66 g O in CO₂ per 1.00 g C. Ratio 1.33 : 2.66 = 1 : 2.
Note: Example (nitrogen oxides): 2.62 g N and 0.656 g N each combine with 1.50 g O → ratio 2.62 : 0.656 = 4 : 1 (small whole numbers).
