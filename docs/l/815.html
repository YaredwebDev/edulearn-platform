6.4 Biomes

◌ Not started
  • Biome: a major terrestrial or aquatic life zone characterized by vegetation type (terrestrial) or the physical environment (aquatic).

Terrestrial Biomes:

  1. Tropical rain forests — between latitudes 10° N and S (equatorial Africa, East Indies, SE Asia, South America, Central America). Annual rainfall 130–200 cm; average temperature 25 °C; photosynthesis year-round. Plants are vertically layered; broad-leaf evergreen trees dominant. Highest animal diversity of any terrestrial biome. Threat: deforestation.

  2. Deserts — average < 10 cm rain/year; cover about one-fifth of Earth's land surface; many at about 30° N/S latitude (Atacama, Gobi). Hot deserts > 50 °C; cold deserts < −30 °C. Plants: succulents (cacti/euphorbs), deep-rooted shrubs, herbs. Plant adaptations: tolerance of heat/desiccation, water storage, reduced leaf surface area. Fauna: snakes, lizards, scorpions, ants, beetles, birds, seed-eating rodents (many nocturnal). Water conservation is a common adaptation.

  3. Savannas — occur in equatorial/sub-equatorial regions, between tropical forests and hot deserts (Africa, India, Australia). Precipitation 30–50 cm/year; dry season up to 8–9 months; temperature 24–29 °C year-round. Scattered trees; fire-adapted plants; grasses common; tolerate grazing. Herbivores: giraffes, zebras, elephants, antelopes, wildebeest herds; carnivores: lions, hyenas. Humans may have lived in savannas; fires and cattle ranching affect them.

  4. Temperate grasslands — warm summers, cold winters; annual rainfall 25–100 cm. Grass roots hold topsoil in place. North American grasslands = shortgrass and tallgrass prairies. Once supported elk, pronghorn, bison (prey to wolves).

  5. Boreal forests (taiga / coniferous forest) — south of the Arctic Circle (Canada, Alaska, Russia, N Europe). Cold, dry winters; short, cool, wet summers; precipitation 40–100 cm (mostly snow). Cold-tolerant coniferous plants (pines, spruce) with needle-shaped leaves. Soils acidic with little nitrogen; lower species diversity.

  6. Temperate broad-leaf forest — at mid-latitudes in the Northern Hemisphere (also Chile, S Africa, Australia, NZ). Precipitation 70–200+ cm/year; winters ~0 °C, summers up to 35 °C. Dominant plants = deciduous trees (drop leaves before winter). Many mammals hibernate; birds migrate. Heavily settled/logged but recovering.

  7. Tundras — cover about 20% of Earth's land surface (Arctic; alpine tundra on high mountains). Precipitation 20–60 cm/year (arctic). Winters below −30 °C; summer < 10 °C. Vegetation: herbaceous (mosses, grasses, forbs), dwarf shrubs, lichens. Permafrost (permanently frozen soil) restricts root growth. Grazers: musk oxen (resident), caribou/reindeer (migratory); predators: bears, wolves, foxes. Major human impact: mineral and oil extraction.

Aquatic Biomes: - Occupy roughly 75% of Earth's surface; determined by salinity and other physical factors. - Freshwater biomes (lakes, streams/rivers, wetlands): salt concentration typically less than 1%. - Marine biomes (oceans, intertidal zones, coral reefs): salt concentrations generally around 3%.

  1. Lakes — standing bodies of water (ponds to lakes covering thousands of km²). Rooted/floating aquatic plants near shore; phytoplankton (cyanobacteria) and zooplankton in open water; fishes in zones with oxygen. Nutrient enrichment (runoff, waste) → algal "bloom," oxygen depletion, fish kills.

  2. Wetlands — soil permanently or periodically saturated with water; shallow bodies of water. Types: marshes, swamps, bogs, mudflats, salt marshes. High organic production; water and soils periodic low in dissolved oxygen. High capacity to filter dissolved nutrients and chemical pollutants; among the most productive biomes. Swamps dominated by woody plants; bogs by sphagnum mosses. Draining/filling has destroyed up to 90% of wetlands in Europe.

  3. Streams and rivers — speed and volume of flow are key. Headwater streams: cold, clear, swift, turbulent; downstream rivers: warmer, more turbid (suspended sediment). Salt/nutrient content increases from headwaters to mouth; headwaters rich in oxygen. Pollution, damming, and flood control degrade them (threaten migratory species like salmon).

  4. Estuaries — a transition area between river and sea; seawater flows up/down with tides; salinity varies. Nutrients from the river make estuaries among the most productive biomes. Producers: saltmarsh grasses, algae, phytoplankton. Support worms, oysters, crabs, many fish; breeding grounds for marine life; crucial feeding areas for waterfowl. Filling, dredging, and pollution disrupt them.

Ocean zone terms (note box): Photic zone = zone of sufficient light for photosynthesis; Aphotic zone = little light penetration; Pelagic zone = photic + aphotic together; Abyssal zone = the part of the ocean 2000–6000 m below the surface.
