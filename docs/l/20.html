4.1 Farm Animal Species

◌ Not started
  • Domestication = conversion of wild animals to domestic use. Domesticated animals are selectively bred to live alongside humans. Most were domesticated between about 8000 and 2500 BC.
  • Species = the largest group of animals capable of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring. Breed = a specific group within a species that is visibly similar in most characteristics, distinguishing it from other breeds of the same species. A species is composed of several breeds.

4.1.1 Farm animals in Ethiopia and their description

  • Ethiopia's farm animals comprise mammalian, avian and honeybee species. Mammals: cattle, sheep, goats, camels, donkeys, horses, mules. Avian: chicken, ostrich, turkey (ostrich & turkey not widely used). Stinging honeybees are the most important bee species. Nearly all livestock are local breeds, some hybrid, some exotic.
  • Key breed terms: Local breed = native to a territory of origin, well-adapted to local conditions (e.g. Horro cattle). Exotic breed = mainly foreign, used to improve productivity of locals through crossbreeding (e.g. Holstein Friesian). Hybrid/cross-breed = offspring of two genetically different parents (e.g. Horro cow × Holstein bull).
  • Ethiopian livestock population (CSA 2021): cattle ~70.3 M (97.4% local), sheep ~42.9 M (99.5% local), goats ~52.5 M (99.9% local), camel ~8.1 M, chicken ~57.0 M, ~7.0 M beehives.

Species (taxonomy + traits)

  • Cattle — family Bovidae, genus Bos; humped Bos indicus (tropical) vs usually non-humped Bos taurus (temperate). Reared for meat, milk, draft; hides→leather; manure a by-product. Ruminant herbivores; feed on pasture (+ manufactured feeds in modern farming).
  • Sheep (Ovis aries) — family Bovidae, genus Ovis (>200 breeds). Raised for meat, wool, skin; some milk. Ruminant herbivores on short fine grass & brushy weeds.
  • Goat (Capra hircus) — family Bovidae, genus Capra (>300 breeds). Milk, meat, skins. Angora breed → mohair hair/yarn. Ruminant herbivores; browse shoots/twigs/leaves of brush plants; also hay & complete feeds.
  • Camel (Camelus dromedary/bactrianus) — even-toed ungulates, family Camelidae, genus Camelus. Dromedary = one hump; Bactrian = two humps. "Ships of the desert"; pack/saddle animals; provide milk, meat, wool, hides. Pseudo-ruminant (no rumen; three-chambered stomach: omasum, abomasum, reticulum; do not chew the cud). Herbivores eating tree leaves/branches/thorny bushes (thick tough lips).
  • Poultry (Gallus gallus domesticus) — chicken/fowl, duck, guinea fowl, turkey, geese. Two domestic chicken types: layers (eggs) and broilers (meat, slaughtered 6–8 weeks). Omnivorous. Monogastric (single stomach), dependent on less fibrous feeds (ground grains, mixed rations).
  • Honey bee (Apis) — pollination & honey. Colony = worker, queen, drone. Workers & queen are female; queen is reproductive & larger; drones are male (larger compound eyes, no stingers). Feed on pollen & nectar.
  • Fish — important worldwide food; fish farming = fastest growing area of animal food production (e.g. Tilapia).

✍️ Must-know (species classification): Cattle, sheep, goat = ruminants (four-chambered stomach). Camel = pseudo-ruminant (three chambers, no rumen). Poultry/horse/pig = monogastric (single stomach). Herbivore (cattle, sheep, goat, camel), omnivore (chicken/pig), etc. Local/exotic/hybrid breed definitions with examples.

4.1.2 Ruminant and monogastric animals

  • Ruminants have a four-chambered stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum); mainly herbivores (cows, sheep, goats); eat large amounts of roughage/fibre; more efficient at breaking down food & absorbing nutrients than monogastrics.
  • Rumen = first & largest chamber; a big fermentation vat where microorganisms (bacteria) digest cellulose; rumination/regurgitation lets them chew cud repeatedly.
  • Reticulum = second chamber ("honeycomb"); involved in rumination & passing food from rumen to omasum; rumen+reticulum work together = reticulorumen.
  • Omasum = third chamber ("many plies", many folds); squeezes water from feed.
  • Abomasum = fourth chamber = the true stomach; produces digestive juices; passes feed to small → large intestine → excreted.
  • Pseudo-ruminants have a three-chambered stomach (omasum, abomasum, reticulum) but no rumen; do not regurgitate/chew the cud. Example: camel.
  • Monogastrics have a single stomach; rely on easily digestible feedstuffs; all food habits; generally cannot digest much cellulose (grass). Examples: poultry, pigs, horses.
  • Horse digestive system: mouth (teeth break feed) → esophagus → stomach (short-term storage/digestion) → small intestine (absorption) → cecum (contains microorganisms for fermenting/breaking down plant material — allows roughage digestion) → large intestine → excreted via rectum/anus.
  • Poultry digestive system: beak → esophagus → crop (expandable storage at base of neck, up to 12 h) → stomach (proventriculus = glandular stomach producing acid/enzymes; gizzard = muscular stomach/pacemaker of gut motility) → small intestine (absorption) → ceca (blind sacks where bacteria break down undigested food) → large intestine (absorbs water) → cloaca (urine mixes with waste) → exits at the vent.

✋ Trap (exam favourite): monogastric digestive system does NOT include a rumen. The chicken's gizzard is the muscular stomach; proventriculus secretes acid/enzymes; crop stores food. The horse digests roughage mainly in the cecum.
