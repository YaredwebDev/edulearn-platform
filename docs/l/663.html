7.4 Nuclear Reaction and Energy Production

◌ Not started

Two fundamental types of nuclear reactions: 1. Nuclear fission: the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two (or more) smaller nuclei plus neutrons and energy. (e.g. ²³⁵U fission releases ~200 MeV.) 2. Nuclear fusion: combining two or more light nuclei into a heavier nucleus (+ subatomic particles) with a large energy release. (The energy source of the Sun and stars.)

Nuclear fission and its applications

  • Fission is used in nuclear power reactors and in nuclear weapons. A fission event releases neutrons that can cause further fission.
  • Chain reaction: neutrons released in fission produce additional fission in at least one further nucleus → repeats. Occurs only above a certain critical mass. The process may be controlled (nuclear power) or uncontrolled (nuclear weapons).
  • Balancing nuclear equations: total mass number A and total charge Z must balance on both sides. (Example 7.4: complete a fission equation, find X and the element Y.)

Components of a nuclear (fission) power reactor (know these parts): - Fuel: uranium (uranium-235), usually as pellets of uranium oxide (UO₂) in fuel rods. - Moderator: slows down the neutrons so they cause more fission (usually water, heavy water, or graphite). - Control rods/blades: made of neutron-absorbing material (cadmium, hafnium, boron); inserted/withdrawn to control or halt the reaction rate. - Coolant: fluid circulating through the core to remove heat (in light-water reactors the water moderator also acts as coolant). - Pressure vessel / pressure tubes: the robust steel vessel containing the core. - Steam generator: uses reactor heat to make steam to drive a turbine (in pressurized-water reactors). - Containment: a thick concrete-and-steel structure protecting against radiation leakage/outside intrusion.

Nuclear waste problem: reactor by-products (e.g. caesium-137, half-life 30 years) remain radioactive for a long time, posing long-term storage/safety challenges.

7.4.1 Nuclear fusion and its uses (the Sun)

  • Heavy hydrogen isotopes deuterium (²H) and tritium (³H) fuse at extremely high temperatures to form a helium nucleus and a neutron: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n, releasing energy (mass loss ~0.0188 u).
  • The extremely high temperature gives nuclei enough kinetic energy to overcome the electrical repulsion between the positive nuclei.
  • The Sun runs on fusion (a chain/cyclic fusion of hydrogen isotopes) — this is the energy source of stars.
  • Fusion generally produces less hazardous long-lived radioactive waste than fission and cannot easily run away like an uncontrolled fission chain.

✍️ Must-know (fission vs fusion): fission splits a heavy nucleus (needs neutrons, produces radioactive waste, used in power reactors & bombs); fusion joins light nuclei (needs extreme temperature, powers the Sun/stars, less waste). Fusion requires very high temperature to overcome Coulomb repulsion.
