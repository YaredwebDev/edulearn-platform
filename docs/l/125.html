3.5 Enzyme Regulation

◌ Not started

Enzyme regulation = a control system turning enzymes "on"/"off" as needed. A regulatory enzyme responds to other biomolecules and regulates a pathway. Two types: allosteric enzymes and covalently modulated enzymes.

  1. Allosteric enzymes — have extra binding sites for effector molecules besides the active site. Two sites: the active (catalytic) site and the allosteric (regulatory) site. Effectors are small molecules (inhibitor or activator) that bind reversibly (non-covalent) at the allosteric site, causing conformational changes that modify enzyme activity.

  2. Genetic and covalent modification — alters the enzyme protein surface. Covalent modulated enzymes switch between active and inactive forms due to enzyme-catalysed addition/removal of chemical groups. Most frequent example: phosphorylation (addition of phosphate groups to proteins).

  3. Enzyme inhibition — decrease in enzyme activity by inhibitors (molecules that bind and block the enzyme). Two types: - Irreversible inhibitor — permanently blocks enzyme action. - Reversible inhibitor — inactivates the enzyme via easily reversed (non-covalent) interactions; types:

    • Competitive inhibitor — blocks the substrate from binding the active site (resembles substrate).
    • Non-competitive inhibitor — binds the enzyme (often already bound to substrate) and decreases enzyme efficacy (does not compete for the active site).
    • Uncompetitive inhibitor — binds only to the enzyme–substrate complex (not the free enzyme); slows reactions in reactions with two or more substrates/products.

✋ Trap: competitive inhibitors bind at the active site (blocking substrate); non-competitive/uncompetitive bind elsewhere (allosteric), not at the active site. Example: the HIV drug Tipranavir is a reversible inhibitor that blocks a viral enzyme (protease) from making more copies.
