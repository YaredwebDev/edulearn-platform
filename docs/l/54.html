9.4 Milk Production and Processing

◌ Not started

9.4.1 Milk production

  • Milk contains calcium, iodine, potassium, phosphorus, fats, vitamins B2 & B12 — important for food security, income and nutrition.
  • Two milking systems: hand milking & machine milking. Choice depends on level of operation, economic efficiency, number of cows. Milk in a calm, quiet place.
  • Considerations in milking:
  • Frequency: 2–3×/day but at regular intervals; sudden time changes affect total yield.
  • Interval: affects quantity & composition; ideal = 12 hours.
  • Duration: ideal 5–6 min (machine), 7 min (hand).
  • Order (to prevent cross-infection): ① first-calf heifers free of mastitis → ② older cows free of mastitis → ③ cows with mastitis history (no symptoms) → ④ cows with quarters producing abnormal milk. Newly introduced animals milked separately until status known.
  • Lactation cycle = period between one calving and the next (cows usually calve every 12 months). Split into four phases: early, mid, late lactation and the dry period.
  • Normal lactation length ≈ 10 months (305 days); remaining ~2 months = "drying off" period.
  • Colostrum = first milk after calving.
  • Milk yield rises in the first months, then declines continuously.
  • Peak yield = highest milk production in the lactation; normally reached 4–10 weeks after calving; then gradually declines.
  • Dry period = most important phase; normally 55–60 days; prepares cow for next calving; abnormalities harm health & future production.
  • Encourage maximum intake in early lactation; cows should reach max DM intake within 10 weeks after calving (eating ≥4% of body weight) and be fed to maintain peak as long as possible.

9.4.2 Milk processing

  • Milk spoils easily (excellent microbial medium). Extend shelf life by cooling, fermentation, or processing.
  • Cooling = most common to keep original quality; cool immediately after milking, keep as cold as possible — best temperature 4 °C or below (mechanical refrigeration or cooling tanks).
  • Reasons to process milk: increase shelf life & palatability, reduce bulkiness (reach distant markets), satisfy consumer demand, create employment.
  • Pasteurised milk: heated to destroy harmful bacteria & extend shelf life. Heat to 63–65 °C for 20–30 min OR 72–75 °C for 15–30 s. Shelf life 2–3 days, up to 12 days if kept at 4 °C.
  • Cream: higher-butterfat top layer that rises; remaining milk = skim milk. Single/light cream ≈ 18% milk fat; double/heavy cream ≈ 48% milk fat. Fermented cream → butter & buttermilk.
  • Fermented milk / yoghurt: milk fermented with bacteria (sometimes yeast); acidity stops microbes and preserves. Yoghurt: add mixed culture of Lactobacillus bulgaricus & Streptococcus thermophilus (1:1) to convert lactose → lactic acid. Heat milk 70 °C 15–20 min, cool to 30–40 °C, add culture, ferment at 32–47 °C ~5 h, cool to 4 °C.
  • Cheese: concentrated form of milk-fat & protein; made from whole/sour/skim milk or buttermilk (cheapest from buttermilk/skim). Purpose: prolong shelf life, reduce spoilage & load, increase palatability. Made by coagulating milk via direct acidification, bacterial lactic acid, added rennet, or combination. Coagulant heated ~50 °C ≥20 min. Solid = cheese; fluid = whey. Hard cheese drains whey & is pressed; soft cheese keeps some whey. Store at 5–10 °C.
  • Cottage cheese (practical): boil whole milk 83–85 °C 20 min; add ~30 mL lemon juice (diluted with equal water); stir 3 min; settle 15 min; filter; salt ~40 g/kg; press overnight.
  • Butter: semi-solid, from churning fresh or fermented cream; ~80–85% milk fat, 15–16% water, 2% SNF. Churn at 14–17 °C; salt ~16 g/kg.
  • Ghee = "clarified butter oil"; from butter by direct heating to 110–120 °C; water evaporated at constant boiling. Stores more easily than butter (no refrigeration). Almost entirely butterfat; low/no moisture & SNF inhibits bacterial growth.
  • Industrial flow: whole milk → separate cream & skim (separator) → churn cream → butter (85% fat) → heat → ghee (89% fat); heat skim milk 50 °C 20 min → whey & cottage cheese.
