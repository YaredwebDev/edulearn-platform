7.1 The Nucleus

◌ Not started

Structure of the atom: every atom has electrons, protons and neutrons — except the smallest isotope of hydrogen (¹H) which has no neutron. - Electron: charge −e (≈ −1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C), mass ≈ 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg (0.000549 u). - Proton: charge +e, mass ≈ 1.6726×10⁻²⁷ kg (≈1.007 u). - Neutron: charge 0, mass ≈ 1.6749×10⁻²⁷ kg (≈1.009 u).

Atomic nucleus = positively charged protons + neutral neutrons, held together by the strong nuclear force (the strongest known fundamental force).

Nuclear notation: ᴬzX, where A = mass number (protons + neutrons), Z = atomic number (protons). N = A − Z neutrons.

Radius of a nucleus: R ≈ r₀ A^(1/3), with r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m). The atom is mostly empty space — the nucleus occupies only a tiny fraction (e.g. ~0.06% of the atom's volume). Example 7.1: compare radii/volumes of ¹H and ²³⁸U.

Isotopes: members of a family of an element that all have the same number of protons (Z) but different numbers of neutrons (N). - Hydrogen's isotopes: protium (¹H, 1p 0n), deuterium (²H or D, 1p 1n), tritium (³H, 1p 2n). - Other examples: different oxygen/carbon isotopes with the same Z.

✋ Trap: isotopes of an element differ in neutron number (and thus mass number), never in proton number (which defines the element). Same N but different Z ⇒ isotones; same A ⇒ isobars.

7.1.1 Historical origins of the nucleus & its particles

Discovery of the nucleus — Rutherford's gold-foil (α-scattering) experiment: | Observation | Conclusion | |---|---| | Most α-particles passed straight through (0–10° deflection) | Most of the atom is empty space | | Some deflected by small angles (10–90°) | Positive charge is concentrated, not spread uniformly | | Very few deflected back (90–180°, rarely 180°) | Most of the mass and all positive charge are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus |

  • Discovery of the proton: associated with hydrogen nuclei being emitted from nitrogen when bombarded (protons are hydrogen nuclei; Rutherford identified the proton).
  • Discovery of the neutron: James Chadwick (1932) discovered the neutron — uncharged particles of mass ≈ proton, produced when beryllium was bombarded by α-particles.
  • Why can't electrons be inside the nucleus? Because of the very small size/heisenberg/localization constraints — electrons are far too large energetically to be confined to the nucleus (their de Broglie wavelength/size would be too big).

7.1.2 What keeps the nucleus together — nuclear forces

  • Strong nuclear force: a very short-range attractive force acting between nucleons (protons & neutrons); it holds the nucleus together, overcoming the electrical repulsion of the protons. Range ~10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Weak nuclear force: acts inside individual nucleons; responsible for radioactive decay (especially beta decay). Even shorter-ranged than the strong force.
  • Strength comparison at ~10⁻¹⁵ m: strong force ≈ 100× electromagnetism ≈ 10⁶× weak ≈ 10³⁸× gravitation.

✍️ Must-know: the strong force binds protons+neutrons in the nucleus and is ~100× the electromagnetic force; the weak force causes beta decay.

Nuclear binding energy (BE): the energy required to completely separate a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons (equivalently, the energy liberated when nucleons combine). Because mass is converted to energy (Einstein, E = Δm c²), the mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of its separate nucleons — this "missing mass" is the mass defect.

Mass defect: Δm = (mass of Z protons + mass of (A−Z) neutrons) − mass of nucleus. - Binding energy from mass defect: BE = Δm c². - Using units: nuclear mass in u (atomic mass unit), energy in MeV. Conversion: 1 u ≈ 931 MeV (used via 1 u c² ≈ 931.5 MeV). BE(MeV) = Δm(u) × 931. - Binding energy per nucleon (BEN) = BE / A — a measure of nuclear stability; iron (⁵⁶Fe) is near the peak (most stable).

✍️ Must-know: BE = Δm c²; Δm = [Z m_p + (A−Z)m_n] − m_nucleus; 1 u ≈ 931.5 MeV; BEN = BE/A. Example 7.2 computes mass defect and BEN for ¹⁶O.

Nuclear stability: very light and very heavy nuclei are less stable (lower BEN); the most tightly bound nucleus is around iron. For stability, the neutron–proton ratio increases with mass number (heavier stable nuclei need more neutrons to overcome proton repulsion). Plot of BEN vs A peaks near A ≈ 56.
