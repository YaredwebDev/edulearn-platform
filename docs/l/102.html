16.3 Nutrient Enrichment Strategies

◌ Not started
  • Dietary modifications = changes to increase the bioavailability of micronutrients in food (at commercial or household level), during food preparation, processing, consumption — mostly to reduce micronutrient deficiencies. Example: eating iron-rich foods with ascorbic acid (vitamin C).
  • Reasons for diet modification: provide a change in diet constituents; maintain, restore or correct nutritional status; include all nutrients; increase/decrease the energy value; provide bland foods; modify the intervals of feeding.
  • Four nutrient-enrichment techniques:

16.3.1 Fortification

  • Food fortification = deliberately increasing the content of an essential micronutrient (vitamins, minerals, trace elements) in a food. Aims to improve nutritional quality of the food supply, give public-health benefit with minimal risk, and reduce the number of people with dietary deficiencies. Adding micronutrients to staples & condiments prevents large-scale deficiencies.
  • Most commonly fortified foods (FAO): cereals & cereal products, milk/milk products, fats & oils, tea & other beverages, infant formulas. It is cost-effective, reaches large at-risk populations through existing delivery systems, and needs no major change in consumption patterns.

16.3.2 Germination

  • Sprouting pulses & cereals — a traditional food-processing method, extensively used for weaning babies & elderly people. A pulse/cereal sprouts small shoots when kept with a small amount of water. Grains/pulses are soaked in just enough water (all absorbed — don't throw away soaking water or nutrients are lost), then sprouted.
  • Grains like wheat, barley, sorghum can be sprouted, dried in shade, roasted lightly, ground & used in dishes. Soaking ~8–16 hours; sprouting ~12–24 hours; the cloth holding soaked grain must be kept moist. Sprouting followed by fermentation improves vitamin content much more.
  • Advantages: increases digestibility (some CHO & proteins broken into smaller, digestible forms); grains/pulses soften → less cooking time; increases nutritive value with no additional cost; some vitamins & minerals increase — vitamin B almost doubles; vitamin C increases almost 10×.

16.3.3 Fermentation

  • Adding micro-organisms (e.g. yeast for bread, bacteria for yoghurt) that chemically break down food (with effervescence & heat), changing existing nutrients into simpler, more digestible forms and making new nutrients. It makes dough rise (almost double). Fermented foods in Ethiopia: milk curd, bread, Injera.
  • Advantages: improves digestibility (micro-organisms break proteins & carbohydrates into smaller parts); during fermentation of cereals/pulses, calcium, phosphorus & iron change into better-quality, easily absorbed forms; fermented foods become spongy & soft — liked by children & adults.

16.3.4 Roasting

  • A cooking method using dry heat (hot air envelops food); uses indirect, diffused heat (as in an oven); suitable for slower cooking of meat in a large whole piece. Meats & most root/bulb vegetables can be roasted. Heat seals the outside of the food, trapping juices inside — so the food cooks in its own juices. Mainly used for fleshy foods (fish, meat, chicken). Enhances flavour.
