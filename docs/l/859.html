3.1 Biochemical Molecules

The body is made of two types of biochemical molecules:

  • Inorganic biochemical molecules: do not contain both carbon and hydrogen. Examples: water (H₂O), hydrochloric acid (HCl). Some contain carbon (e.g., CO₂, carbonate ions).
  • Organic biochemical molecules: contain both carbon and hydrogen, joined by covalent bonds. Examples: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids. Carbon and hydrogen are the 2nd and 3rd most abundant elements in the body.

3.1.1 Inorganic Molecule: Water

Water = H₂O, composed of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

  • A key component of cells, making up 70–95% of a cell's mass; the human body is ~60% water.

Structure/polarity: oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen → unequal electron sharing → water is a polar molecule (O has partial negative charge δ⁻, H has partial positive charge δ⁺). This creates hydrogen bonds between water molecules, giving water its unique properties.

Properties of water (and their importance)

  1. Water as a solvent: dissolves ions and polar molecules (sugars, glycerol). Non-polar molecules (lipids) are insoluble and are pushed together.
  2. High specific heat capacity: the heat needed to raise 1 g of water by 1 °C is high; hydrogen bonds make water resistant to temperature changes.
  3. High latent heat of vaporization: much energy is needed to vaporize water (break hydrogen bonds) → evaporation has a strong cooling effect (e.g., sweating).
  4. Density and freezing: ice is less dense than liquid water → ice floats and insulates water below (life survives cold). Water density decreases below 4 °C.
  5. High surface tension and cohesion: water molecules stick together (cohesion) and to other molecules (adhesion); capillary action allows water to climb tubes (important for transport in plants). Surface tension allows small organisms (raft spiders) to walk on water.
  6. Boiling and freezing points: high boiling point (100 °C) and freezing point (0 °C) because hydrogen bonds must be broken; important for aquatic ecosystems.
✍️ Must-know: Water's polarity and hydrogen bonding give it its special properties. It is the "universal solvent."

3.1.2 Inorganic Ions

Inorganic ions are charged entities (atoms with unshared outer-shell electrons). When discussed in the human body they are often called minerals.

Classification by daily requirement:

  • Macronutrients: required in larger amounts — Sodium, Magnesium, Potassium, Chloride, Phosphorus, Sulfur.
  • Micronutrients: required in trace amounts — Iron, Copper, Iodine, Manganese.

Key inorganic ions

a. Hydrogen ions (H⁺)

  • Maintain balance and equilibrium; found in cytoplasm, intercellular fluid, blood, CSF.
  • Concentration expressed as pH (scale 1–14); the higher the pH, the lower the H⁺ concentration (inverse relationship).

b. Sodium ions (Na⁺)

  • Most important extracellular cation; higher concentration in extracellular fluid than in cells.
  • Requirement: 3–6 g/day (as common salt); excess is removed in urine; lower salt intake needed for high blood pressure.

c. Potassium ions (K⁺)

  • Major intracellular cation (higher inside cells).
  • Requirement: 3–4 g/day; sources: bananas, oranges, potatoes, chicken, liver.
  • An electrolyte (carries electric charge when dissolved); necessary for normal cell, nerve, and muscle function.

d. Calcium ions (Ca²⁺)

  • Most abundant inorganic ion; mainly in cytoplasm, bones, teeth.
  • Requirement: ~800 mg/day; sources: milk and milk products, leafy vegetables, egg yolk, fish, beans.
  • Calcium = 1–1.5 kg of body weight; 99% in bones and teeth, 1% in cells/extracellular fluid.

e. Phosphate ions (PO₄³⁻)

  • Negatively charged; ~1 kg in the body, mostly with calcium in bones and teeth.
  • Requirement: ~800 mg/day (like calcium); sources: milk, cereals, meat, eggs.

f. Chloride ions (Cl⁻)

  • Negatively charged, in extracellular fluids; works with sodium.
  • Requirement: 5–10 g/day.
  • Maintains osmotic pressure, fluid balance, acid–base balance; makes HCl for gastric juice (digests protein, kills pathogens); activates salivary amylase.

g. Iron ions (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺)

  • ~70% in haemoglobin in red blood cells; sources: beans, red meat, liver, spinach, pumpkin seeds, fish.
  • Needed for O₂ and CO₂ transport (haemoglobin, myoglobin), cytochromes (electron transport chain), peroxidase enzyme.
  • Deficiency diseases: iron-deficiency anemia (too little iron), hemosiderosis (excess iron), hemochromatosis (abnormal iron deposits in liver, spleen, skin, pancreas).

h. Copper ions (Cu²⁺)

  • Sources: shellfish, seeds/nuts, organ meats, wheat, chocolate.
  • Needed for synthesis of haemoglobin, collagen, elastin, and normal nervous-system development.
  • Wilson disease: abnormal copper metabolism → copper deposits in liver (cirrhosis) and brain (damage); kidney deposits → renal failure.

i. Iodine ions (I⁻)

  • Sources: iodized salt, dairy, seafood, plants grown in iodine-rich soil.
  • Vital for thyroid health; used to make thyroid hormones.
  • Deficiency → hypothyroidism, underactive thyroid → goiter (enlarged thyroid). Hyperthyroidism = overactive thyroid.
  • Requirement: 150 µg/day for adolescents and adults.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know diseases: Goiter = iodine deficiency; Anemia = iron deficiency; Wilson disease = copper metabolism.

3.1.3 Organic Molecules

An organic molecule is a compound that contains carbon and is found in living things. Carbon forms large, complex molecules via covalent bonding.

Four principal groups: Carbohydrates, Lipids, Proteins, Nucleic acids.

Monomers and polymers (Table 3.3):

MacromoleculeElementsMonomer
ProteinsC, H, O, NAmino acids
LipidsC, H, OGlycerol and fatty acids
CarbohydratesC, H, OMonosaccharides (glucose, galactose, fructose)
Nucleic acidsC, H, O, N, PNucleotides

Monomers = small single molecular units; polymers = long chains of many monomers joined by strong covalent bonds (polymerization).

Carbohydrates

  • Made of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen; ratio of H:O is 2:1.
  • Functions: energy source, energy storage, cell-membrane components, structural components.
  • Three groups: monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides.

a. Monosaccharides (one sugar unit)

  • General formula (CH₂O)ₙ (or CₙH₂ₙOₙ); C:H:O = 1:2:1.
  • By number of carbons: trioses (3C), pentoses (5C), hexoses (6C).
  • Hexose formula: C₆H₁₂O₆.
  • Glucose, galactose, and fructose are isomers (same formula, different structure).
  • Roles: energy source in respiration (C–H bonds release energy to make ATP from ADP); building blocks for starch, glycogen, cellulose; ribose/deoxyribose used in RNA/DNA/ATP.

b. Disaccharides (two monosaccharides)

  • Maltose = glucose + glucose (product of starch breakdown by amylase)
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose (milk sugar)
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose (transport sugar in plants, table sugar)
  • General formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.

c. Polysaccharides (many glucose units) — storage & structural

  • Starch: plant's stored sugar (in roots, seeds); broken down to glucose.
  • Glycogen: animal storage form; highly branched; stored in liver and muscle; animal analogue of starch.
  • Cellulose: plant cell walls; provides structural support; every other glucose monomer is densely packed → rigidity/strength; dietary fibre (humans can't digest; herbivores digest with help of cellulase from rumen bacteria).

Food tests:

  • Benedict's test (reducing sugars): blue copper(II) sulphate → brick-red copper(I) oxide precipitate when reducing sugar present (all monosaccharides, maltose). Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar.
  • Iodine test (starch/glycogen): iodine turns starch blue, dextrin reddish-purple.

Functions of carbohydrates: primary energy source; storage (starch/glycogen); cell-membrane components for intercellular communication; structural components (cell walls, chitin in fungi/exoskeleton of insects).

Lipids

  • Insoluble in water but soluble in non-polar solvents (ether, chloroform, acetone, benzene).
  • Elements: C, H, O.
  • Fats = solid at room temperature; oils = liquid at room temperature.
  • True lipids = organic compounds formed by fatty acids + alcohol (glycerol).
  • Fatty acid general formula: CH₃(CH₂)ₙCOOH, containing the acidic carboxyl group (-COOH); long hydrocarbon tail (often 15 or 17 carbons).

Types of fatty acids:

  • Unsaturated fatty acids: contain one or more double bonds (C=C) → not maximum hydrogen; melt easily; mostly oils (plants); mono-unsaturated (one double bond) vs polyunsaturated (several).
  • Saturated fatty acids: no double bonds; solid at room temperature; animal fats.

Phospholipids: one fatty acid replaced by a phosphate group; the phosphate head is hydrophilic (water-loving), the two fatty-acid tails are hydrophobic (water-hating) → form a bilayer (cell membrane).

Functions of lipids: stored energy / major fuel store; insulation (adipose cells); structural components of cell membranes; precursors for vitamin D, bile acids, hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, progesterone, estrogen, testosterone); protective coatings (wax on skin, fur, feathers, fruit, leaves).

Proteins

  • Elements: C, H, O, N (sometimes P and S).
  • General amino-acid formula: RCH(NH₂)COOH — central carbon bonded to an amine group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • More than 50% of the dry mass of most cells is protein.
  • 20 different amino acids occur in proteins, differing only in their R group.
  • Peptide bond: covalent bond between amino acids formed by condensation (loss of water: one loses -OH, the other loses -H). Many amino acids → polypeptide.
  • Hydrolysis (adding water) breaks peptide bonds during digestion.
  • Sites of protein synthesis: ribosomes.
  • By shape:
  • Globular proteins: curl into a ball; soluble; roles in metabolic reactions (enzymes, haemoglobin, antibodies).
  • Fibrous proteins: long strands; insoluble; structural (keratin → hair/nails/skin; collagen → skin, tendons, cartilage, bones).

Functions of proteins: structural (collagen, elastin, keratin); enzymes (biological catalysts lowering activation energy — DNA polymerase, lysozyme, nitrogenase, lipase); hormones (GH, FSH); respiratory pigments (myoglobin, haemoglobin); transport (serum albumin, channel/carrier proteins); motor (actin, myosin); storage (ferritin, ovalbumin, casein); toxins (diphtheria toxin).

Food tests:

  • Biuret test: protein in alkaline solution reacts with copper ions → mauve/purple colour.
  • Xanthoproteic test: concentrated nitric acid + NaOH → identifies aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan).

Nucleic Acids

  • Made of phosphoric acid, sugars, and organic bases; the cell's principal information-carrying molecules.
  • Two types: DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid).
  • Both are polynucleotides made of nucleotides.
  • Genes = stretches of DNA that code for RNA and amino acids (proteins).

Nucleotides = three components:

  1. A nitrogen-containing base
  2. A pentose (5-carbon) sugar
  3. A phosphate group

Nitrogenous bases: In DNA — Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C). In RNA — A, G, C, Uracil (U) instead of thymine.

Pentose sugars: Ribose (C₅H₁₀O₅) in RNA; Deoxyribose (C₅H₁₀O₄) in DNA (no oxygen on the 2nd carbon).

Difference between DNA and RNA

FeatureDNARNA

Functions:

  • DNA: genetic material storing information for the next generation; replicates (copies) during cell reproduction; carries the genetic code; transcribed to mRNA, then translated into proteins.
  • RNA: genetic material in some viruses; enzymatic activity in others (ribozyme).
  • mRNA: moves genetic code from DNA to ribosomes.
  • tRNA: supplies amino acids for protein synthesis.
  • rRNA: forms the ribosome with proteins.
✍️ Must-know: DNA = double helix, deoxyribose, A-T-G-C; RNA = single strand, ribose, A-U-G-C.
