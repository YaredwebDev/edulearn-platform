2.3 Covalent Bonding

◌ Not started

A covalent bond forms when two non-metal atoms share one or more pairs of electrons so each achieves an octet. A molecule is the smallest unit of a covalently bonded substance.

2.3.1 Lewis structures — step-by-step

  1. Count the total valence electrons (add electrons for negative charge, subtract for positive).
  2. Arrange atoms (usually the least electronegative central atom).
  3. Put single bonds (lines) between bonded atoms.
  4. Complete octets with lone pairs, placing remaining electrons on the central atom (if any).
  5. If the central atom is short of an octet, form double/triple bonds by moving lone pairs in.

Worked valence-electron counts: NF₃ = 5 + 3×7 = 26; CO₃²⁻ = 4 + 3×6 + 2 = 24; NO₂⁻ = 5 + 2×6 + 1 = 18 valence electrons.

H₂ bond length: the H–H distance in H₂ is about 74 pm (the shared electron pair holds the two H together).

Coordinate (dative) covalent bond: a bond in which both shared electrons come from one atom (the donor), e.g. in NH₄⁺ the N shares its lone pair with H⁺; also found in O₃, CO and many oxyacids. Once formed, a coordinate bond is indistinguishable from a normal covalent bond.

Resonance: when one Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule, two or more contributing structures are used, differing only in the position of π electrons (not atom positions). E.g. O₃ and NO₂⁻ are resonance hybrids.

✍️ Must-know: O₃ is written with two resonance structures; the N–O bond lengths in NO₂⁻ are equal (resonance), not one single + one double.

2.3.2 Octet-rule exceptions (three groups)

  1. Electron-deficient molecules — central atom has fewer than 8 e⁻: BeCl₂, BF₃, AlCl₃.
  2. Expanded octet (hypervalent) — central atom has more than 8 e⁻ (period 3+ uses d orbitals): PF₅, SF₆, XeF₄. (AsF₅ likewise does not obey the octet.)
  3. Odd-electron molecules — cannot give every atom an octet: NO, NO₂, ClO₂.

✋ Trap: SF₆ and PF₅ legitimately exceed the octet; AsF₅ does not "comply" with the octet rule. Odd-electron species like NO₂ are free radicals with an unpaired electron.

2.3.3 Bond polarity and dipole moment

  • A covalent bond is non-polar when the two atoms have equal electronegativity (electron pair shared equally); polar when there is a difference in electronegativity (unequal sharing → partial charges δ+ and δ−).
  • Electronegativity difference scale: 0 (non-polar covalent) → small (polar covalent) → large (ionic). Ionic character grows with the electronegativity difference; a bond with ΔEN ≳ ~1.7 is treated as largely ionic.
  • Bond dipole: a vector pointing from the positive (δ+) to the negative (δ−) end of a polar bond.
  • Dipole moment μ = δ × d (charge × separation distance). Unit Debye (D); 1 D = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m.
  • A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel by symmetry.

Key numbers/examples: - CO₂ μ = 0 (linear, two equal C=O dipoles cancel) — non-polar even though each C=O bond is polar. - H₂O μ = 1.87 D (= 6.23 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m) — the fact that water has a net dipole proves it is bent, not linear. - cis-1,2-dichloroethene μ = 1.89 D (dipoles add) vs trans-1,2-dichloroethene μ = 0 (dipoles cancel). - CCl₄ (tetrahedral, symmetrical) μ = 0; CHCl₃ is polar.

✍️ Must-know: μ = δ × d, 1 D = 3.33564×10⁻³⁰ C·m; a zero dipole despite polar bonds means the geometry is symmetrical (CO₂, CCl₄, trans-C₂H₂Cl₂).

2.3.4 Covalent compounds: general properties (experiment)

  • Usually gases, liquids, or low-melting solids.
  • Poor conductors of electricity in all states (no free ions/electrons).
  • Often insoluble in water but soluble in non-polar solvents (except when they can hydrogen-bond, e.g. sugar).
