4.5 Mendelian Inheritance

◌ Not started
  • Gregor Mendel — Austrian monk, "father of genetics"; studied pea plants (Pisum sativum) for ~8 years and discovered the principles of heredity in the mid-1800s. He coined terms: factors (later genes/alleles), dominant, recessive, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous.
  • Mendelian inheritance = inheritance of traits controlled by a single gene with two alternative alleles.
  • Two foundational laws: law of segregation and law of independent assortment.
  • Mendel's 7 traits (dominant shown first): flower colour (purple>white), flower position (axial>terminal), seed colour (yellow>green), seed shape (round>wrinkled), pod shape (inflated/smooth>constricted), pod colour (green>yellow), stem length (tall>short).

✍️ Why peas? They show easily observed variation, mating can be controlled, many traits are single-gene — but NOT an unusually long generation time (peas have short generation time; that exception was the distractor in the review).

4.5.2 Monohybrid cross

  • A cross to study the inheritance of one character.
  • Mendel self-crossed pure lines to confirm purity/true-breeding, then crossed pure contrasting parents → F1 generation (all show the dominant trait, e.g. all Tt tall), then selfed F1 → F2 generation (both traits appear).
  • Punnett square = a chart predicting the expected genotype/phenotype ratios of offspring.
  • Example TT × tt: all F1 = Tt (tall). Selfing Tt × Tt: F2 genotypes TT, Tt, Tt, tt → genotypic ratio 1:2:1, phenotypic ratio 3:1 (tall:short).
  • Seven crosses each gave ≈ 3:1 in F2 (e.g., flower colour 705:224 ≈ 3.15:1).
  • Mendel's conclusions: parents each carry two "factors"; one dominant, one recessive; the factors separate so each parent contributes only one factor to an offspring; each offspring inherits one factor from each parent; a dominant factor is expressed even if a recessive is present; a recessive is expressed only when two recessives are present.

✍️ Ratios: F2 monohybrid = genotypic 1:2:1, phenotypic 3:1.

4.5.3 Dihybrid cross

  • A cross studying two traits at once. Example RRYY (round-yellow) × rryy (wrinkled-green) → all F1 RrYy. Selfing RrYy × RrYy (gametes RY, Ry, rY, ry) → F2:
  • Round-yellow = 9, round-green = 3, wrinkled-yellow = 3, wrinkled-green = 1 → 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio.

The Law of Segregation (1st law, from monohybrid): copies of genes randomly separate (segregate) during meiosis so each gamete receives only one allele; offspring receive one allele from each parent. Physical basis = the first division of meiosis (homologous chromosomes segregate).

The Law of Independent Assortment (2nd law, from dihybrid): genes do not influence each other in sorting alleles into gametes; separate genes for separate traits pass independently to offspring; every combination of alleles is equally likely. Physical basis = Metaphase I when homologous pairs align randomly. A YyRr heterozygote forms 4 equally likely gametes: YR, Yr, yR, yr (50:50 for R/r and 50:50 for Y/y).

4.5.4 Test cross & back cross

  • Test cross = crossing an organism of unknown genotype (but dominant phenotype) with a known homozygous recessive to determine the unknown genotype.
  • If all progeny show the dominant trait → the unknown parent was homozygous dominant.
  • If progeny are ~1:1 dominant:recessive → the unknown parent was heterozygous.
  • Example: purple (P? ) × white (pp): all purple → PP; 1:1 → Pp.
  • Back cross = crossing the F1 hybrid back to one of its parents (dominant or recessive).

✍️ Must-know (review answers): homozygous black BB × bb → 100% black. AaBb selfed → probability of AABB offspring = 1/16. Test cross for yellow-seeded pea plants uses a homozygous-recessive (green, yy) plant. In dihybrid F2, red/axial etc. — expect red-terminal = 3/16 of offspring (e.g. of 1000 → ≈ 190).
