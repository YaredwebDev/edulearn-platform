4.3 Factors Affecting the Rate of a Chemical Reaction

◌ Not started

The rate depends on: nature of reactants, surface area, concentration, temperature, and presence of a catalyst.

i. Nature of the reactants

  • Reactions differ in intrinsic speed depending on the substances. Oppositely charged ions react extremely fast because there are no bonds to break (e.g. H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O, the HCl–NaOH acid–base reaction) — far faster than reorganising reactions like 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
  • Metal reactivity matters: sodium reacts with water almost explosively, calcium moderately, iron barely at room temperature. Mg + HCl bubbles faster than Cu + HCl (Cu barely reacts).

💡 Experiment 4.2: equal masses of Cu and Mg in equal 1 M HCl → Mg reacts far faster because Mg is the more reactive metal.

ii. Surface area of reactants

  • Reactions occur at the interface/boundary between phases (heterogeneous reactions involve ≥2 phases, e.g. solid + gas). A homogeneous reaction occurs in a single phase (e.g. NO → ½N₂ + ½O₂).
  • Finely divided (powdered) solids react faster than the same mass as a large piece because surface area is greater (more contact for collisions). Logs chopped small burn faster; powdered Zn dissolves faster than large Zn pieces in acid.

iii. Concentration of reactants

  • At fixed temperature, increasing the concentration of a reactant increases the rate (more particles → more collisions per second).
  • For gaseous reactants, increasing pressure increases concentration and thus rate. Pressure has no effect if the reactants are solids or liquids.
  • Wood burns more rapidly in pure O₂ than in air; marble dissolves faster in more concentrated acid.

iv. Temperature

  • Raising the temperature increases the rate dramatically. Particles gain kinetic energy → collide more frequently and with greater energy (more collisions exceed E_a).
  • In homogeneous systems the rate often roughly doubles for every ~10 °C rise.
  • Everyday evidence: foods cook faster when hot; refrigerators slow food spoilage by lowering temperature; wood/coal oxidise far faster when heated.

v. Presence of a catalyst

  • Catalyst = a substance that changes (usually increases) the reaction rate by providing a different reaction mechanism with a lower activation energy (E_a). Catalysts are not used up — they are recovered unchanged at the end. A catalyst does not alter the position of equilibrium (Unit 5).
  • Positive catalyst: lowers E_a → increases rate. E.g. V₂O₅ in 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃.
  • Negative catalyst (inhibitor): raises E_a → decreases rate. E.g. OH⁻ inhibits OCl⁻ + I⁻ → OI⁻ + Cl⁻.
  • Heterogeneous catalyst: different phase from reactants (V₂O₅ solid catalysing gas reaction). Homogeneous catalyst: same phase as reactants (OH⁻ in aqueous reaction).
  • Catalysts can be specific — one that catalyses a reaction may have no effect on a similar one. Biological catalysts are enzymes.

✋ Trap: a catalyst speeds up a reaction by lowering E_a and is recovered unchanged — it is NOT consumed, does NOT change the equilibrium constant or the equilibrium composition, and can only affect reactions it specifically catalyses.
