🧬 MASTER KEY-FACTS SHEET (keep for revision)

Unit 1 (Sub-fields of Biology):

  • Biology = study of life. Major branches: botany (plants), zoology (animals), microbiology (microbes).
  • Structural subfields: cytology (cells), histology (tissues), anatomy, morphology, genetics, taxonomy.
  • Process subfields: physiology, ecology, embryology, evolution, ethology (behaviour).
  • Pure vs applied: discovery vs practical use (e.g., medicine, agriculture, biotechnology).
  • Key discoveries: microscope (cells), germ theory (Pasteur/Koch), inheritance (Mendel), DNA (Watson/Crick).

Unit 2 (Plants):

  • Root → anchor & absorb; stem → support & transport; leaf → photosynthesis.
  • Gymnosperms: naked seeds, cones; Angiosperms: flowers, seeds in fruits.
  • Seed = embryo + stored food + protective coat; germination needs water, O₂, warmth.
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O →(light, chlorophyll)→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
  • Transport: xylem (water/minerals up), phloem (sugars up & down); ringing cuts phloem.
  • Tropisms: growth toward/away from stimulus; auxin controls phototropism & gravitropism.

Unit 3 (Biochemical Molecules):

  • Inorganic = no C+H (water, salts); Organic = C+H (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids).
  • Water: polar, hydrogen bonds → solvent, high heat capacity, surface tension, ice floats.
  • Ions: Na⁺/K⁺ electrolytes, Ca²⁺ bones/teeth, Fe haemoglobin, I thyroid/goiter, Cu Wilson disease.
  • Carbohydrates (CH₂O)ₙ: monosaccharides → disaccharides → polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose).
  • Lipids: glycerol + fatty acids; saturated vs unsaturated; phospholipid bilayer.
  • Proteins: amino acids + peptide bonds; globular vs fibrous.
  • Nucleic acids: DNA (double helix, deoxyribose, A-T-G-C), RNA (single, ribose, A-U-G-C).

Unit 4 (Cell Reproduction):

  • Cell cycle = Interphase (G₁, S, G₂) + cell division.
  • Mitosis: 1 division, 2 identical diploid (2n) cells; growth/repair; PMAT.
  • Meiosis: 2 divisions, 4 haploid (n) cells; gametes; Meiosis I separates homologous, II separates sister chromatids.
  • Human somatic = 46 (23 pairs); gametes = 23.

Unit 5 (Human Biology):

  • Adult teeth 32; milk teeth 20. Stomach pH 1.5–2.5 (HCl + pepsin).
  • Villi: sugars/amino acids → capillaries; fats → lacteals.
  • Heart: 4 chambers; universal donor O, recipient AB; BP 120/80.
  • Breathing: inspiration (diaphragm contracts) vs expiration; gas exchange in alveoli.
  • Excretion: nephron filtration → reabsorption → secretion; kidney makes EPO (85%) + vitamin D.
  • Immunity: innate (non-specific) vs adaptive (specific, memory, vaccination).

Unit 6 (Ecological Interactions):

  • Trophic levels: producers → primary → secondary → tertiary consumers; 10% energy rule.
  • Energy pyramid always upright; number/biomass can invert.
  • Water cycle, carbon cycle (photosynthesis ↔ respiration), nitrogen cycle (fixation→nitrification→assimilation→ammonification→denitrification), phosphorus cycle.
  • Earthworms = "friends of farmers"; biofertilizers, vermicompost, crop rotation (legumes fix N).

🔑 TOP EXAM-TRAP POINTS TO ALWAYS REMEMBER

  1. Organic vs inorganic: both must contain C AND H to be organic (CO₂ is inorganic despite having C).
  2. Water's properties all trace to hydrogen bonds — polarity, high specific heat, surface tension, ice floating.
  3. Goiter = iodine deficiency; anemia = iron deficiency; Wilson disease = copper.
  4. DNA: A-T-G-C, deoxyribose, double helix. RNA: A-U-G-C, ribose, single strand. (U replaces T.)
  5. Mitosis = 2 identical diploid cells (growth/repair); Meiosis = 4 haploid cells (gametes).
  6. Meiosis I separates HOMOLOGOUS chromosomes; Meiosis II separates SISTER chromatids (like mitosis).
  7. Human somatic = 46 chromosomes (2n); gametes = 23 (n).
  8. Universal donor = O (no antigens); universal recipient = AB (no antibodies).
  9. Tachycardia >100 bpm; bradycardia <60 bpm; normal BP = 120/80 mmHg.
  10. Expired air: O₂ 16%, CO₂ 4%; inspired: O₂ 21%, CO₂ 0.04%.
  11. Only 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level; 90% lost as heat, work, egestion.
  12. Energy pyramid always upright; number & biomass pyramids can be inverted.
  13. N-cycle microbes: Fixation = Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Klebsiella; Nitrification = Nitrosomonas (→NO₂⁻), Nitrobacter (→NO₃⁻); Denitrification = Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus, Micrococcus.
  14. Plants prefer ammonium (NH₄⁺) over nitrate (NO₃⁻) for assimilation.
  15. N₂O (nitrous oxide) has ~300× the global-warming potential of CO₂.
  16. Eutrophication = excess phosphate/nitrate run-off → algal bloom → O₂ depletion.

1.1 Major Branches of Biology

Biology is the study of living organisms and life processes. It is divided into three major branches:

Major branchWhat it studiesExamples of sub-disciplines
Zoology (Animal biology)The study of animalsHerpetology (reptiles), Ichthyology (fish), Mammalogy (mammals), Ornithology (birds), Entomology (insects)
BotanyThe study of plantsPlant structure, properties, biochemical processes, classification, plant diseases, plant–environment interactions
MicrobiologyThe study of microscopic organisms (microbes) that cannot be seen with the unaided eyeBacteriology (bacteria), Mycology (fungi); includes bacteria, archaea, protists, viruses, microscopic algae and fungi

Botany facts to remember:

  • Plants provide food, shelter, clothing, medicine, ornament, and tools.
  • Through photosynthesis, plants convert solar energy into chemical energy of food and release oxygen as a by-product — both essential for all life.

1.2 Pure and Applied Fields of Biology

  • Pure biology — the study of how life functions in nature (behaviour, structure, reproduction, etc.).
  • Applied biology — the use of biological knowledge to manage life (gardening, nursery work, agriculture, plant disease control, forestry, poultry, etc.). Relationship is similar to anatomy and surgery.

Examples (Table 1.4):

  • Pure: Morphology, Taxonomy, Evolution, Cytology, Embryology, Physiology, Histology, Paleontology, Bacteriology, Entomology.
  • Applied: Agriculture, Biotechnology, Horticulture, Pathology, Forestry, Pharmacognosy, Poultry, Pisciculture, Apiculture, Agroforestry, Animal Husbandry, Sericulture.

Importance of biology in other branches of science

  1. Biotechnology — use of living organisms or their products for human welfare; involves genetic manipulation (Genetic Engineering); produced genetically modified (GM) crops and animals.
  2. Bioinformatics — acquisition, storage, analysis, and dissemination of biological data (DNA and amino-acid sequences) using computer programs.
  3. Genetic Engineering — extracting or synthesizing selected genes and inserting them into another organism → organism with a new combination of genes.
  4. Biomedical Engineering — application of engineering principles to biology and medicine to improve health.
  5. Environmental Management — observation of the environment and finding solutions to maintain the balance of nature.
  6. Forensic Science — application of biological knowledge (DNA fingerprints, blood typing) to criminal and civil law.

2.3 Structure and Function of Plant Parts

A typical angiosperm has two major external systems:

  • Shoot system (above ground): stem, branches, leaves, buds, flowers, fruits.
  • Root system (below ground): primary/tap root, lateral/branch roots, root hairs, root cap.
  • Roots vs underground stems: roots do not bear leaves or buds.

External structure of a leaf

  • Petiole (leaf stalk), lamina/blade (broadest flat part), midrib (contains the vein/transport vessels), margin, base, tip/apex.
  • Large, thin lamina → large surface area for light collection and short diffusion distance for gas exchange through stomata.

Types of roots

  • Tap root: one large primary vertical root with few lateral roots; penetrates deep for anchorage and deep water; feature of dicots.
  • Fibrous root: thin, moderately branching roots of similar size from the stem base; efficient at absorbing surface water; holds soil (prevents erosion); feature of monocots.

2.3.1 Internal structure of a leaf

  • Outer layer (epidermis): single layer of tightly packed cells.
  • Upper epidermis covered with a waxy cuticle → transmits light but restricts water loss.
  • Lower epidermis contains bean-shaped guard cells forming stomata (regulate O₂ release, CO₂ intake, water loss); stomata are more abundant on the lower epidermis.
  • Middle layer (mesophyll):
  • Palisade layer: columnar cells just below the upper epidermis, packed with the largest number of chloroplasts → does most of the photosynthesis.
  • Spongy layer: irregular, loosely arranged cells with fewer chloroplasts and numerous air spaces → allow diffusion of O₂, CO₂, water vapour.

2.3.2 Internal structure of a stem (dicot)

From outside in: Epidermis (cuticle, compact cells) → Hypodermis (collenchyma, thickened corners → mechanical strength) → Cortex (thin-walled cells for food storage) → Endodermis (innermost cortex layer, starch sheath) → Vascular bundles (xylem + phloem + cambium, arranged in a ring around the pith) → Pith (central, stores food, helps internal water translocation).

Monocot vs dicot stem: dicot has few (4–8) vascular bundles in one or two rings with a well-developed pith; monocot has scattered vascular bundles.

2.3.3 Internal structure of a root (dicot)

From outside in: Piliferous layer (outermost, single-celled root hairs, no cuticle) → Cortex (multi-layered, stores food and water) → Endodermis (barrel-shaped, controls water movement into xylem; Casparian strip) → Pericycle (single layer, site of origin of lateral roots) → Vascular bundles (xylem + phloem + cambium) → Pith.

✍️ Must-know: Root hair = single cell; best suited for absorption (large surface area). Endodermis may be called the starch sheath. Palisade layer has the most chloroplasts.

2.4 Reproduction in Plants

2.4.1 Reproductive structure & life cycle of non-flowering plants (Gymnosperms — pine tree)

  • Pine has male and female cones on one plant.
  • Pollination (by wind) transfers pollen from male cone to female cone.
  • Pollen germinates forming a pollen tube; the generative cell divides into two sperm cells.
  • Each female-cone scale has two ovules; each ovule undergoes meiosis producing 4 cells, of which only one survives → a gametophyte with an archegonium (contains a single large egg).
  • Fertilization: one sperm fuses with the egg → zygote → seed embryo.
  • Mature seed is released when the cone dries and opens; dispersed and germinates into a seedling.

Key cycle: Sporophyte (2n, mature tree) → cones → gametophytes → egg + sperm → zygote (2n) → seed → seedling → mature tree.

Note: 📌 Ethiopian gymnosperms: Podocarpus falcatus ("Zigba") and Juniperus procera ("Tid").

2.4.2 Reproductive structure & life cycle of flowering plants (Angiosperms)

Four floral parts:

  • Sepals (calyx): green leaf-like, protect the flower.
  • Petals (corolla): brightly coloured, attract pollinators.
  • Stamen (androecium): the male part = filament + anther (bilobed, produces pollen).
  • Pistil (gynoecium/carpel): the female part = stigma + style + ovary (with ovules and egg).

Complete vs incomplete flower: complete has all four parts; incomplete lacks one or more.

Perfect vs imperfect flower: perfect has both stamen and pistil; imperfect has only one (staminate or pistillate).

Life cycle stages (angiosperm):

  1. Pollination → 2. Pollen tube formation → 3. Fertilization (union of male and female gametes in the ovule → zygote; angiosperms show double fertilization forming the endosperm) → 4. Seed and fruit formation (ovule → seed; ovary → fruit) → 5. Seed dispersal → 6. Seed dormancy / germination.
✍️ Must-know: Seed = matured ovule; Fruit = matured ovary. Angiosperms show double fertilization (zygote + endosperm).

2.4.3 Pollination

  • Pollination = transfer of pollen from anther to stigma.
  • Self-pollination = between anther and pistil on the same flower or between flowers on one plant.
  • Cross-pollination = between flowers on two different plants.
  • Agents of pollination: insects, wind, animals, water. Flower structure (colour, scent) strongly relates to the pollinating agent.

2.5 Seeds

A seed (fertilized ovule) has three parts: seed embryo, cotyledon/endosperm (reserve food), and seed coat. In angiosperms the seed is also covered by the fruit.

Seed embryo parts: radicle (future root), epicotyl, hypocotyl, and plumule (future shoot).

Difference between dicot and monocot seeds (Table 2.1)

Dicot seed (e.g., bean)Monocot seed (e.g., corn/maize)
Two cotyledonsOne cotyledon
Cotyledons are fleshy, store foodCotyledon thin, lacks food
Endosperm absentEndosperm large and well developed
Primary root from radicle bears many lateral rootsPrimary root replaced by adventitious fibrous roots

2.6 Seed Dispersal and Germination

2.6.1 Seed dispersal

  • Plants are sessile, but seeds show long-distance mobility via dispersal agents.
  • Dispersal prevents overcrowding and ensures germination and survival.
  • Agents: animals (fleshy fruits eaten; seeds escape digestion due to hard coats), wind (hairy/winged seeds, e.g., kites/propellers), water (floating seeds), and clinging seeds (stick to fur/clothes).

2.6.2 Germination of seed

  • Germination = resumption of active growth of the embryo after dormancy.
  • Three essential conditions:
  1. Water (moisture): hydrates seed coat, increases permeability to O₂, enables enzymatic hydrolysis of food, transports soluble substances.
  2. Oxygen (aeration): needed for aerobic respiration → energy for embryo growth.
  3. Temperature (warmth): optimum temperature required.

Process: radicle emerges first (from hypocotyl) → grows down as the primary root (anchorage, water/nutrients); plumule shoot grows upward toward light → young plant (seedling) with photosynthetic leaves.

Epigeal vs hypogeal germination:

  • Epigeal: cotyledon is pushed up above soil and becomes the photosynthetic surface (elongation of hypocotyl).
  • Hypogeal: cotyledon remains under the soil.

2.7 Photosynthesis

  • The leaves of green plants are the largest "food factories" on Earth.
  • Photosynthesis = the process by which chlorophyll-bearing organisms trap solar energy and convert it into chemical energy (food).
  • Plants are producers/autotrophs; animals are consumers/heterotrophs.

2.7.1 The photosynthetic apparatus (Chloroplast)

Two distinct parts:

  • Granum: stacks of flattened sacs called thylakoids; contains chlorophyll, enzymes, co-factors → site of the light reaction.
  • Stroma: gel-like colourless matrix; site of sugar (carbohydrate) synthesis via carbon fixation → the dark reaction.

2.7.2 Light-absorbing system

  • Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll a and b and accessory pigments that absorb light in the visible range (400–700 nm).
  • Maximum absorption at 400–500 nm (blue) and 600–700 nm (red).
  • Light of 500–600 nm (green) is reflected, not absorbed → this is why leaves look green.

2.7.3 Mechanism of photosynthesis

1. Light reaction (light-dependent stage) — occurs in the granum:

  • Photolysis: splits water (H₂O → H⁺ + O₂); O₂ is released to the atmosphere through stomata.
  • Excites electrons → they pass down the electron transport system (ETS) generating high-energy ATP.
  • The ATP and H⁺ are inputs for the dark reaction.

2. Dark reaction (light-independent stage) — occurs in the stroma:

  • Can occur without light as long as H⁺ and ATP are supplied.
  • Carbon fixation: H⁺ combines with CO₂ → glucose (carbohydrate) is the immediate result.

2.7.4 Testing a leaf for starch

  • Iodine turns starch blue-black.
  • Two problems before testing: leaf cuticle is impermeable to iodine, and chlorophyll interferes with the colour.
  • Procedure: (1) boil leaf in water (kills cells, stops reactions); (2) boil in alcohol (removes chlorophyll), turning off the Bunsen burner first (alcohol is flammable); (3) dip in cold water; (4) add iodine → blue-black confirms starch.

2.8 Transport in Plants

Vascular bundles = xylem + phloem.

  • Xylem: transports water and dissolved minerals from roots to leaves; made of elongated dead cells joined end-to-end.
  • Phloem: transports food (organic matter, sucrose, amino acids) from leaves to the rest of the plant (translocation); made of living cells; transport up and down (source → sink).

2.8.1 Uptake of water and minerals

  • Water enters the root through root hairs (elongated single cells with large surface area) — this is absorption.
  • Water passes passively (by osmosis across the membrane/cytoplasm and diffusion along porous cell walls) to the root xylem.
  • Water is pulled upward by transpiration pull (the main force); cohesion (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) holds the water column together; adhesion (water to cell walls) resists gravity.

2.8.2 Translocation of organic matter (food)

  • Translocation = transport of food from source (leaf) to sink (stem/root).
  • Occurs through phloem; it is active transport (living cells use energy).
  • Ringing/girdling experiment: removing a ring of bark (with phloem) causes a swelling above the girdle due to accumulation of food. Ringing ultimately kills the plant because food can't reach the root; the root dies first.
✍️ Must-know: Xylem = water & minerals (dead cells, one-way up); Phloem = food (living cells, active, two-way).

2.9 Response in Plants

Tropism = bending toward (positive) or away from (negative) the direction of a unilateral (one-sided) stimulus, caused by unequal distribution of the growth hormone auxin (e.g., Indole Acetic Acid, IAA), resulting in unequal growth.

Auxin effects: produced at shoot and root tips; promotes shoot growth at moderate concentrations but inhibits root growth at the same concentrations; roots need very low auxin.

3.1.1 Inorganic Molecule: Water

Water = H₂O, composed of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

  • A key component of cells, making up 70–95% of a cell's mass; the human body is ~60% water.

Structure/polarity: oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen → unequal electron sharing → water is a polar molecule (O has partial negative charge δ⁻, H has partial positive charge δ⁺). This creates hydrogen bonds between water molecules, giving water its unique properties.

Properties of water (and their importance)

  1. Water as a solvent: dissolves ions and polar molecules (sugars, glycerol). Non-polar molecules (lipids) are insoluble and are pushed together.
  2. High specific heat capacity: the heat needed to raise 1 g of water by 1 °C is high; hydrogen bonds make water resistant to temperature changes.
  3. High latent heat of vaporization: much energy is needed to vaporize water (break hydrogen bonds) → evaporation has a strong cooling effect (e.g., sweating).
  4. Density and freezing: ice is less dense than liquid water → ice floats and insulates water below (life survives cold). Water density decreases below 4 °C.
  5. High surface tension and cohesion: water molecules stick together (cohesion) and to other molecules (adhesion); capillary action allows water to climb tubes (important for transport in plants). Surface tension allows small organisms (raft spiders) to walk on water.
  6. Boiling and freezing points: high boiling point (100 °C) and freezing point (0 °C) because hydrogen bonds must be broken; important for aquatic ecosystems.
✍️ Must-know: Water's polarity and hydrogen bonding give it its special properties. It is the "universal solvent."

3.1.2 Inorganic Ions

Inorganic ions are charged entities (atoms with unshared outer-shell electrons). When discussed in the human body they are often called minerals.

Classification by daily requirement:

  • Macronutrients: required in larger amounts — Sodium, Magnesium, Potassium, Chloride, Phosphorus, Sulfur.
  • Micronutrients: required in trace amounts — Iron, Copper, Iodine, Manganese.

Key inorganic ions

a. Hydrogen ions (H⁺)

  • Maintain balance and equilibrium; found in cytoplasm, intercellular fluid, blood, CSF.
  • Concentration expressed as pH (scale 1–14); the higher the pH, the lower the H⁺ concentration (inverse relationship).

b. Sodium ions (Na⁺)

  • Most important extracellular cation; higher concentration in extracellular fluid than in cells.
  • Requirement: 3–6 g/day (as common salt); excess is removed in urine; lower salt intake needed for high blood pressure.

c. Potassium ions (K⁺)

  • Major intracellular cation (higher inside cells).
  • Requirement: 3–4 g/day; sources: bananas, oranges, potatoes, chicken, liver.
  • An electrolyte (carries electric charge when dissolved); necessary for normal cell, nerve, and muscle function.

d. Calcium ions (Ca²⁺)

  • Most abundant inorganic ion; mainly in cytoplasm, bones, teeth.
  • Requirement: ~800 mg/day; sources: milk and milk products, leafy vegetables, egg yolk, fish, beans.
  • Calcium = 1–1.5 kg of body weight; 99% in bones and teeth, 1% in cells/extracellular fluid.

e. Phosphate ions (PO₄³⁻)

  • Negatively charged; ~1 kg in the body, mostly with calcium in bones and teeth.
  • Requirement: ~800 mg/day (like calcium); sources: milk, cereals, meat, eggs.

f. Chloride ions (Cl⁻)

  • Negatively charged, in extracellular fluids; works with sodium.
  • Requirement: 5–10 g/day.
  • Maintains osmotic pressure, fluid balance, acid–base balance; makes HCl for gastric juice (digests protein, kills pathogens); activates salivary amylase.

g. Iron ions (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺)

  • ~70% in haemoglobin in red blood cells; sources: beans, red meat, liver, spinach, pumpkin seeds, fish.
  • Needed for O₂ and CO₂ transport (haemoglobin, myoglobin), cytochromes (electron transport chain), peroxidase enzyme.
  • Deficiency diseases: iron-deficiency anemia (too little iron), hemosiderosis (excess iron), hemochromatosis (abnormal iron deposits in liver, spleen, skin, pancreas).

h. Copper ions (Cu²⁺)

  • Sources: shellfish, seeds/nuts, organ meats, wheat, chocolate.
  • Needed for synthesis of haemoglobin, collagen, elastin, and normal nervous-system development.
  • Wilson disease: abnormal copper metabolism → copper deposits in liver (cirrhosis) and brain (damage); kidney deposits → renal failure.

i. Iodine ions (I⁻)

  • Sources: iodized salt, dairy, seafood, plants grown in iodine-rich soil.
  • Vital for thyroid health; used to make thyroid hormones.
  • Deficiency → hypothyroidism, underactive thyroid → goiter (enlarged thyroid). Hyperthyroidism = overactive thyroid.
  • Requirement: 150 µg/day for adolescents and adults.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know diseases: Goiter = iodine deficiency; Anemia = iron deficiency; Wilson disease = copper metabolism.

An organic molecule is a compound that contains carbon and is found in living things. Carbon forms large, complex molecules via covalent bonding.

Four principal groups: Carbohydrates, Lipids, Proteins, Nucleic acids.

Carbohydrates

  • Made of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen; ratio of H:O is 2:1.
  • Functions: energy source, energy storage, cell-membrane components, structural components.
  • Three groups: monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides.

a. Monosaccharides (one sugar unit)

  • General formula (CH₂O)ₙ (or CₙH₂ₙOₙ); C:H:O = 1:2:1.
  • By number of carbons: trioses (3C), pentoses (5C), hexoses (6C).
  • Hexose formula: C₆H₁₂O₆.
  • Glucose, galactose, and fructose are isomers (same formula, different structure).
  • Roles: energy source in respiration (C–H bonds release energy to make ATP from ADP); building blocks for starch, glycogen, cellulose; ribose/deoxyribose used in RNA/DNA/ATP.

b. Disaccharides (two monosaccharides)

  • Maltose = glucose + glucose (product of starch breakdown by amylase)
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose (milk sugar)
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose (transport sugar in plants, table sugar)
  • General formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.

c. Polysaccharides (many glucose units) — storage & structural

  • Starch: plant's stored sugar (in roots, seeds); broken down to glucose.
  • Glycogen: animal storage form; highly branched; stored in liver and muscle; animal analogue of starch.
  • Cellulose: plant cell walls; provides structural support; every other glucose monomer is densely packed → rigidity/strength; dietary fibre (humans can't digest; herbivores digest with help of cellulase from rumen bacteria).

Food tests:

  • Benedict's test (reducing sugars): blue copper(II) sulphate → brick-red copper(I) oxide precipitate when reducing sugar present (all monosaccharides, maltose). Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar.
  • Iodine test (starch/glycogen): iodine turns starch blue, dextrin reddish-purple.

Functions of carbohydrates: primary energy source; storage (starch/glycogen); cell-membrane components for intercellular communication; structural components (cell walls, chitin in fungi/exoskeleton of insects).

Lipids

  • Insoluble in water but soluble in non-polar solvents (ether, chloroform, acetone, benzene).
  • Elements: C, H, O.
  • Fats = solid at room temperature; oils = liquid at room temperature.
  • True lipids = organic compounds formed by fatty acids + alcohol (glycerol).
  • Fatty acid general formula: CH₃(CH₂)ₙCOOH, containing the acidic carboxyl group (-COOH); long hydrocarbon tail (often 15 or 17 carbons).

Types of fatty acids:

  • Unsaturated fatty acids: contain one or more double bonds (C=C) → not maximum hydrogen; melt easily; mostly oils (plants); mono-unsaturated (one double bond) vs polyunsaturated (several).
  • Saturated fatty acids: no double bonds; solid at room temperature; animal fats.

Phospholipids: one fatty acid replaced by a phosphate group; the phosphate head is hydrophilic (water-loving), the two fatty-acid tails are hydrophobic (water-hating) → form a bilayer (cell membrane).

Functions of lipids: stored energy / major fuel store; insulation (adipose cells); structural components of cell membranes; precursors for vitamin D, bile acids, hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, progesterone, estrogen, testosterone); protective coatings (wax on skin, fur, feathers, fruit, leaves).

Proteins

  • Elements: C, H, O, N (sometimes P and S).
  • General amino-acid formula: RCH(NH₂)COOH — central carbon bonded to an amine group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • More than 50% of the dry mass of most cells is protein.
  • 20 different amino acids occur in proteins, differing only in their R group.
  • Peptide bond: covalent bond between amino acids formed by condensation (loss of water: one loses -OH, the other loses -H). Many amino acids → polypeptide.
  • Hydrolysis (adding water) breaks peptide bonds during digestion.
  • Sites of protein synthesis: ribosomes.
  • By shape:
  • Globular proteins: curl into a ball; soluble; roles in metabolic reactions (enzymes, haemoglobin, antibodies).
  • Fibrous proteins: long strands; insoluble; structural (keratin → hair/nails/skin; collagen → skin, tendons, cartilage, bones).

Functions of proteins: structural (collagen, elastin, keratin); enzymes (biological catalysts lowering activation energy — DNA polymerase, lysozyme, nitrogenase, lipase); hormones (GH, FSH); respiratory pigments (myoglobin, haemoglobin); transport (serum albumin, channel/carrier proteins); motor (actin, myosin); storage (ferritin, ovalbumin, casein); toxins (diphtheria toxin).

Food tests:

  • Biuret test: protein in alkaline solution reacts with copper ions → mauve/purple colour.
  • Xanthoproteic test: concentrated nitric acid + NaOH → identifies aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan).

Nucleic Acids

  • Made of phosphoric acid, sugars, and organic bases; the cell's principal information-carrying molecules.
  • Two types: DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid).
  • Both are polynucleotides made of nucleotides.
  • Genes = stretches of DNA that code for RNA and amino acids (proteins).

Nucleotides = three components:

  1. A nitrogen-containing base
  2. A pentose (5-carbon) sugar
  3. A phosphate group

Nitrogenous bases: In DNA — Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C). In RNA — A, G, C, Uracil (U) instead of thymine.

Pentose sugars: Ribose (C₅H₁₀O₅) in RNA; Deoxyribose (C₅H₁₀O₄) in DNA (no oxygen on the 2nd carbon).

4.1 Cell Cycle

A cell cycle is a sequence of events that takes place in the parent cell as a means of distributing genetic material, thereby forming daughter cells.

Two main divisions of the cell cycle:

  1. Interphase (preparation)
  2. Cell division (nuclear division + cytoplasmic division)

Interphase (divided into three phases)

  • G₁ phase (First Gap): cell is metabolically active; accumulates building blocks of DNA and associated proteins; stores energy for chromosome replication.
  • S phase (Synthesis of DNA): DNA replication occurs.
  • G₂ phase (Second Gap): cell replenishes energy stores, synthesizes proteins for chromosome movement; some organelles are duplicated; final preparations before mitosis.

Cell division

  • Karyokinesis (nuclear division) — mitosis or meiosis.
  • Cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division) — separation of cytoplasm into daughter cells.
✍️ Must-know: A cell spends most of its time in interphase (growth + DNA synthesis). Cancer occurs when the cell-cycle controlling system fails and cells divide in an unregulated manner ("cell madness").

Cancer: occurs when the controlling factors over cell division and cell growth fail; cancer cells divide continuously at high density, may not communicate with healthy cells, may not carry out normal functions, and may not anchor securely → they can travel and spread.

4.2 Cell Division

  • Cell doctrine: new cells arise from pre-existing cells by cell division (Rudolf Virchow, 1858) — the basis of continuity of life.
  • Cause of cell division: as a cell grows, its surface-area-to-volume ratio falls, so diffusion across the membrane becomes inadequate; the nucleus's controlling power is minimized. Cell division solves this.

Importance of cell division:

  • Unicellular organisms (e.g., Amoeba): a means of reproduction (increasing population).
  • Multicellular organisms: growth, replacement of damaged/dead cells, and repair.
  • Sexually reproducing organisms: producing gametes (sex cells) in reproductive organs.

4.2.1 Mitosis

Mitosis = a type of nuclear division where duplicated chromosomes of a single mother cell are distributed between two identical daughter cells, having the same number and kind of chromosomes as the parent nucleus.

  • A diploid (2n) mother cell gives rise to two diploid (2n) identical daughter cells.
  • Term coined by Walther Flemming (1882).
  • Occurs in somatic (body) cells.

Stages of mitosis (Karyokinesis)

  1. Prophase (first phase): each duplicated chromosome (two sister chromatids) pairs up; nuclear membrane breaks down, nucleolus disappears; chromosomes shorten/thicken and become visible; centrosomes move to opposite poles and spindle fibres emerge.
  2. Metaphase (arrangement phase): spindle fully developed; chromosomes line up along the metaphase plate (equatorial plane); sister chromatids attach to spindle fibres from opposite poles.
  3. Anaphase (migrating phase): cohesion proteins (centromere) break down; sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles by the mitotic spindle.
  4. Telophase (reverse of prophase): nuclear membrane reappears around each set of chromosomes (two new nuclei); mitotic spindle breaks down.

Cytokinesis

  • Animal cells: cytoplasm pinches/constricts in the middle → two daughter cells separate.
  • Plant cells: hard cell wall prevents pinching; a new wall (middle lamella) is laid down between the two daughter cells.

Importance of mitosis: growth, repair and replacement of dead/damaged cells; unicellular organisms increase population; after fertilization the zygote (2n) develops into a multicellular organism by repeated mitotic division (cleavage: 2-cell → 4-cell → 8-cell → 16-cell stages).

Key terms:

  • Spindle fibre: long protein fibre from the centrioles; pulls separated chromosomes toward the poles during anaphase.
  • Homologous chromosomes: paired maternal and paternal chromosomes (represent a parent for a unit character); humans have 23 pairs (46 total) in diploid body cells.
  • Sister chromatids: two copies of one chromosome linked at the centromere.
✍️ Must-know: Mitosis = 2 identical diploid daughter cells; used for growth and repair; PMAT (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase).

4.2.2 Meiosis

Meiosis = a characteristic of sexually reproducing organisms; occurs in reproductive organs (ovaries, testes, anther and ovules of flowering plants).

  • Involves two fissions of the nucleus giving rise to four gametes (sex cells), each with half the number of chromosomes (n) — called reduction division.
  • A diploid (2n) mother cell → four haploid (n) daughter cells.

Why meiosis is important: if human gametes had 46 chromosomes, the zygote would have 92 (not normal). During gametogenesis in ovaries/testes, the 46 chromosomes are reduced to 23; sperm + egg (n + n) → zygote with the normal 46 (2n).

Meiosis I:

  • Prophase I: chromosomes appear shortened/thickened (two chromatids); homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis); crossing over occurs.
  • Metaphase I: homologous pairs line up on the metaphase plate; centromeres do NOT separate.
  • Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes (each with paired chromatids) separate and move to opposite poles — reduction to haploid (n) occurs.
  • Telophase I: nuclear membrane reforms; cells begin to divide; may be a prolonged interphase but no further DNA duplication.

Meiosis II (similar to mitosis):

  • Prophase II: new spindles form; chromosomes move to the middle.
  • Metaphase II: chromosomes line up; spindle attached to sister chromatids from opposite poles.
  • Anaphase II: centromeres divide; sister chromatids separate and move to opposite ends.
  • Telophase II: nuclear membrane reappears; cytokinesis → four haploid daughter cells.

5.1 Digestive System

5.1.1 Anatomy of the Digestive System

The digestive system is a tube running from the mouth to the anus, together with associated organs. Its functions: break down food (physical and chemical), absorb nutrients, and eliminate waste.

Main parts (alimentary canal): Mouth → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) → Large intestine (colon, rectum) → Anus.

Associated organs: Salivary glands, Liver, Gall bladder, Pancreas.

Teeth

  • Human teeth: incisors (cutting/biting), canines (tearing), premolars (crushing/grinding), molars (grinding/chewing).
  • Adult (permanent) dental formula: (2:1:2:3 / 2:1:2:3) × 2 = 32 teeth.
  • Milk (deciduous) teeth: (2:1:0:2 / 2:1:0:2) × 2 = 20 teeth.

5.1.2 The Mouth

  • Food is chewed (mastication) and mixed with saliva.
  • Saliva is produced by 3 pairs of salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual).
  • Saliva contains salivary amylase (ptyalin) which begins breaking starch into maltose, and mucus (lubrication).
  • Bolus = ball of chewed food formed by the tongue.
  • Deglutition (swallowing): the epiglottis closes the windpipe (trachea) so food goes to the oesophagus.

5.1.3 The Stomach

  • A J-shaped muscular bag.
  • Gastric juice contains:
  • Hydrochloric acid (HCl): pH 1.5–2.5; kills bacteria, activates pepsinogen, denatures proteins.
  • Pepsinogen → activated to pepsin (protein-digesting enzyme) by HCl.
  • Mucus: protects the stomach lining from HCl and pepsin.
  • Churns food into chyme (semi-liquid).
  • Absorbs limited amounts of water, alcohol, some drugs (aspirin).

5.1.4 Small Intestine

  • ~6–7 m long; main site of digestion and absorption.
  • Duodenum: first part; receives bile (from gall bladder) and pancreatic juice (from pancreas).
  • Jejunum and ileum: continue digestion and absorption.
  • Intestinal juice from intestinal glands contains enzymes (maltase, sucrase, lactase, peptidase, lipase).
  • The inner wall has villi and microvilli — finger-like projections that enormously increase the surface area for absorption.
  • Inside each villus: capillaries (absorb monosaccharides and amino acids) and a lacteal (absorbs fatty acids and glycerol).

  • Absorption: digested products pass through the intestinal wall into blood/lymph.
  • Assimilation: absorbed nutrients are used by cells to build new materials (e.g., amino acids → body proteins).
  • Egestion (defecation): removal of undigested waste (faeces) via the anus.

5.1.7 Disorders of the Digestive System

  • Constipation: infrequent, difficult defecation; caused by low fibre/water.
  • Diarrhoea: frequent loose, watery stools; caused by infection (e.g., cholera, rotavirus); leads to dehydration.
  • Peptic ulcer: erosion of stomach/duodenum lining; caused by Helicobacter pylori bacteria, excess acid, stress, some drugs.
  • Appendicitis: inflammation of the appendix.
  • Malnutrition: deficiency or excess of nutrients (e.g., marasmus, kwashiorkor, vitamin deficiencies, obesity).
✍️ Must-know: Saliva starts starch; HCl (pH 1.5–2.5) + pepsin digest protein in the stomach; villi in the small intestine absorb nutrients (capillaries for sugars/amino acids, lacteals for fats).

5.2 Circulatory System

5.2.1 The Heart

  • A muscular organ located in the chest; about the size of a fist; pumps blood.
  • Four chambers: two upper atria (right and left) and two lower ventricles (right and left).
  • Septum: muscular wall separating right and left halves.
  • Valves (prevent backflow):
  • Tricuspid valve (right atrium ↔ right ventricle)
  • Bicuspid (mitral) valve (left atrium ↔ left ventricle)
  • Semilunar valves (base of pulmonary artery and aorta)
  • Pericardium: protective membrane around the heart.

Blood circulation

  • Pulmonary circulation: right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium (deoxygenated blood goes to lungs to pick up O₂).
  • Systemic circulation: left ventricle → aorta → body → vena cavae → right atrium (oxygenated blood to body).

Cardiac cycle

  • Systole: heart muscle contracts (pumps blood out).
  • Diastole: heart muscle relaxes (fills with blood).
  • Heart sounds: "Lub" (AV valves closing) and "Dup" (semilunar valves closing).
  • Pulse: the expansion and recoil of arteries with each heartbeat.
  • Blood pressure: force of blood against artery walls; normal ~120/80 mmHg (systolic/diastolic).

5.2.3 Blood

  • Plasma: liquid part (~55%), contains water, proteins, nutrients, wastes, hormones.
  • Red blood cells (erythrocytes): carry O₂/CO₂ via haemoglobin; no nucleus; biconcave discs; made in bone marrow.
  • White blood cells (leucocytes): defend against infection (phagocytes: neutrophils, macrophages; lymphocytes: T and B cells).
  • Platelets (thrombocytes): help blood clotting.

Blood groups (ABO + Rh):

  • Antigens on red cells: A, B, AB, O. Antibodies in plasma: anti-A, anti-B.
  • Universal donor: O (no antigens). Universal recipient: AB (no antibodies).
  • Rh factor (D antigen): Rh⁺ or Rh⁻. If an Rh⁻ mother carries an Rh⁺ baby, her antibodies may attack the baby's red cells → erythroblastosis fetalis (haemolytic disease of the newborn).

5.2.4 Lymphatic System

  • Lymph: fluid from tissue spaces collected into lymph vessels.
  • Lymph nodes, tonsils, spleen, thymus are part of it.
  • Functions: returns tissue fluid to blood; transports fats (via lacteals); filters pathogens; produces lymphocytes; immunity.
  • Elephantiasis: caused by filarial worms (e.g., Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi) blocking lymphatic vessels → severe swelling of limbs.

5.2.5 Circulatory System Diseases

  • Leukemia: cancer of blood-forming tissues (bone marrow) → abnormal white cells.
  • Varicose veins: swollen, twisted veins due to faulty valves.
  • Anaemia: low haemoglobin/red cells → fatigue (iron deficiency common).
  • Cardiovascular diseases: hypertension (high BP), coronary heart disease (blocked heart arteries → angina, heart attack), stroke (blocked/ruptured brain blood vessel).
  • Bradycardia: heart rate below 60 beats/min.
  • Tachycardia: heart rate above 100 beats/min.
  • Arrhythmia: irregular heart rhythm.
  • Atherosclerosis: hardening/narrowing of arteries by fatty plaques.
  • Heart failure: heart can't pump enough blood.
✍️ Must-know: Universal donor = O; universal recipient = AB. 120/80 mmHg normal BP. Tachycardia >100, bradycardia <60 bpm.

5.3 Breathing System

5.3.1 Anatomy

Air passage: Nose (nasal cavity) → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli.

  • Nose: warms, moistens, filters air (cilia and mucus).
  • Pharynx: common passage for air and food.
  • Larynx (voice box): contains vocal cords (sound production); the glottis is the opening to it; the epiglottis covers it during swallowing.
  • Trachea (windpipe): tube with C-shaped cartilage rings; lined with ciliated mucus membrane.
  • Bronchi: two branches into the lungs; divide into bronchioles.
  • Alveoli: tiny air sacs where gas exchange occurs; surrounded by capillaries; enormous surface area.
  • Lungs: right (3 lobes) and left (2 lobes); enclosed by the pleural membranes.
  • Diaphragm: sheet of muscle below the lungs.

5.3.2 Mechanism of Breathing

Inspiration (inhalation):

  • Diaphragm contracts and flattens; rib cage moves up and out (external intercostal muscles contract).
  • Volume of the thorax increases, pressure decreases → air rushes in.

Expiration (exhalation):

  • Diaphragm relaxes and moves up; rib cage moves down and in.
  • Volume decreases, pressure increases → air forced out.

Lung volumes: Tidal volume (~500 mL), vital capacity (~4.5–5 L in adults), residual volume (air left after maximal expiration).

5.3.4 Respiratory Disorders

  • Bronchitis: inflammation of the bronchi (often from smoking/infection).
  • Emphysema: destruction of alveolar walls → reduced gas exchange (COPD).
  • Asthma: narrowing of airways due to inflammation/bronchospasm.
  • COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease): umbrella term (chronic bronchitis + emphysema).

  • Tuberculosis (TB): bacterial infection of the lungs (Mycobacterium tuberculosis).
  • Lung cancer: often linked to smoking.

5.4 Excretory System

5.4.1 Structure

Organs: Kidneys (2) → Ureters → Urinary bladder → Urethra.

  • Kidney: bean-shaped; has cortex (outer), medulla (inner), and pelvis.
  • Functional unit of the kidney = the nephron (~1 million per kidney).

Nephron parts

Renal corpuscle (Bowman's capsule + glomerulus) → Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) → Loop of Henle → Distal convoluted tubule (DCT) → Collecting duct.

5.4.2 Function of the Kidney (Urine Formation)

  1. Glomerular filtration: blood pressure forces water, salts, glucose, urea, amino acids (filterable) out of the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule (filtrate). Blood cells and proteins stay in blood.
  2. Tubular reabsorption: PCT reabsorbs useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most water, salts) back into blood.
  3. Loop of Henle: concentrates urine (reabsorbs water, maintains salt gradient).
  4. Tubular secretion: DCT actively secretes wastes (H⁺, K⁺, creatinine, drugs) into the filtrate.
  5. Collecting duct: final water reabsorption under ADH (antidiuretic hormone) control.

Other kidney functions: produces erythropoietin (EPO) (85% of the body's) which stimulates red-cell production; completes vitamin D activation; regulates blood pH, blood pressure, and blood volume.

Excretion is the removal of metabolic wastes (urea, CO₂, excess water/salts); vs egestion (undigested food) and defecation.

5.4.3 Excretory Disorders

  • Kidney stones: crystallized minerals in the kidney/ureter causing pain.
  • Nephritis: inflammation of the kidney (infection).
  • Kidney failure: kidneys stop filtering → dialysis or kidney transplant needed.
  • Uraemia: accumulation of urea in blood due to kidney failure.
  • Diabetes insipidus: ADH deficiency → excessive dilute urine.
  • Glycosuria: glucose in urine (diabetes mellitus, when blood glucose high).
✍️ Must-know: Urine formation = filtration (glomerulus) → reabsorption (PCT) → secretion (DCT). Kidney makes EPO (85%) and completes vitamin D.

5.5 Immune System

5.5.1 Innate (Non-specific) Immunity

  • Present from birth, immediate, non-specific.
  • Defenses: skin (physical barrier), mucous membranes, cilia, stomach acid (HCl), lysozyme in tears/saliva, phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages) engulfing pathogens, inflammatory response, fever.

5.5.2 Adaptive (Acquired/Specific) Immunity

  • Develops after exposure; specific and has memory (basis of vaccination).
  • Two components:
  • Humoral immunity: mediated by B-lymphocytes (B cells) → produce antibodies (Y-shaped immunoglobulins) that neutralize antigens.
  • Cell-mediated immunity: mediated by T-lymphocytes (T cells) — helper T cells, cytotoxic (killer) T cells.
  • Antigens: foreign molecules that trigger an immune response.
  • Antibodies: proteins produced by B cells that bind specific antigens.

5.5.3 Cells of the Immune System

  • Lymphocytes (T and B cells): recognize and respond to antigens; memory cells provide long-term immunity.
  • Macrophages: large phagocytes that engulf pathogens and present antigens.
  • Dendritic cells: antigen-presenting cells.
  • Natural killer (NK) cells: kill infected/cancer cells.
  • Neutrophils: most abundant white cells; phagocytic.
  • Mast cells: release histamine (allergy/inflammation).

5.5.4 Immunity Types

  • Active immunity: body produces its own antibodies (natural infection or vaccination).
  • Passive immunity: antibodies transferred (maternal to baby via placenta/breast milk; or antiserum).

Vaccination: introducing a weakened/inactive pathogen (antigen) so the body produces antibodies and memory cells → protection later.

5.5.5 Immunological Disorders

  • Allergy: overreaction to harmless antigens (allergens) → histamine release (hay fever, asthma, hives).
  • Autoimmune diseases: immune system attacks self tissues (Type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, lupus).
  • Immunodeficiency: weakened immune system (HIV/AIDS) → opportunistic infections.
✍️ Must-know: Innate = non-specific, immediate, from birth. Adaptive = specific, memory-based (vaccination). Antibodies = Y-shaped immunoglobulins from B cells.

6.1 Food Chain, Food Web, and Ecological Pyramids

6.1.1 Ecological Terms

  • Ecology: the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment.
  • Population: all the organisms of the same species living in a particular area.
  • Community: all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
  • Ecosystem: a community of organisms together with their non-living (abiotic) environment.
  • Habitat: the place where an organism lives.
  • Biotic factors: living components (producers, consumers, decomposers).
  • Abiotic factors: non-living components (temperature, light, water, soil, air).

6.1.2 Trophic Levels and Food Chains

  • Producers (autotrophs): make their own food by photosynthesis (green plants, algae). = Trophic level 1.
  • Primary consumers (herbivores): eat producers. = Trophic level 2.
  • Secondary consumers (carnivores): eat primary consumers. = Trophic level 3.
  • Tertiary consumers (top carnivores): eat secondary consumers. = Trophic level 4.
  • Decomposers/saprophytes (bacteria, fungi): break down dead organic matter, recycling nutrients.

Food chain: a linear sequence of organisms in which each organism is eaten by the next.

Note: Example: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk.

Food web: an interconnected network of many food chains; more realistic than a food chain (which is unidirectional; a food web is multi-directional/interwoven).

6.1.3 Energy Transfer (10% Rule)

  • Only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is transferred to the next.
  • About 90% is lost as heat, work, and undigested/egested material (faeces).
  • This limits food chains to usually 3–4 trophic levels (too little energy remains for a 5th level).

6.1.4 Ecological Pyramids

Pyramid of energy: always upright (energy always decreases up the levels); never inverted.

Pyramid of numbers: can be inverted (e.g., one tree → many birds → many fleas).

Pyramid of biomass: can be inverted (e.g., in aquatic ecosystems: small biomass of phytoplankton supports a larger biomass of zooplankton/tilapia: Chlorella → Daphnia → Tilapia).

Biomass: the total mass of living organisms at a trophic level at a given time (dry weight often used).

✍️ Must-know: Only ~10% energy passes to the next level (90% lost as heat/work/egestion). Pyramid of energy is always upright.

6.2 Biogeochemical Cycles

Biogeochemical cycles = the circulation of chemical substances (water, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus) between organisms and the environment.

6.2.1 The Water (Hydrologic) Cycle

Stores of water: oceans (97.5% of Earth's water — saline), ice caps/glaciers, groundwater, rivers, lakes, atmosphere. Only <1% of fresh water is readily accessible for use.

Processes:

  1. Evaporation: water → water vapour (from oceans, lakes, rivers).
  2. Transpiration: water loss from plant leaves.
  3. Condensation: water vapour cools → clouds.
  4. Precipitation: rain, snow, hail falls to the ground.
  5. Runoff and infiltration: water flows over land (rivers) and seeps into the ground (groundwater).

6.2.2 The Carbon Cycle

  • Carbon moves between the atmosphere, organisms, oceans, and rocks.
  • Photosynthesis removes CO₂ (fixed into organic matter).
  • Respiration, decomposition, and combustion release CO₂ back to the atmosphere.
  • Overall equation:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O ⇌ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

(Photosynthesis →; Respiration ←)

  • CO₂ as a greenhouse gas: traps heat in the atmosphere; increased CO₂ (from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, industry) → enhanced greenhouse effect → global warming/climate change.

6.2.3 The Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen (N₂) makes up ~78% of air but is mostly unavailable to organisms in this form — it must be fixed.

  1. Nitrogen fixation: converting N₂ into ammonia (NH₃) / nitrates.
  • Symbiotic: Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules of legumes (using the enzyme nitrogenase).
  • Non-symbiotic (free-living): Azotobacter, Klebsiella.
  • Industrial/atmospheric: Haber process, lightning.
  1. Nitrification: Nitrosomonas oxidizes NH₃ → nitrite (NO₂⁻); Nitrobacter oxidizes nitrite → nitrate (NO₃⁻). Nitrates are the form plants absorb.
  2. Ammonification: decomposers (aerobic/anaerobic bacteria, fungi) break down dead organisms and waste, releasing ammonia/NH₄⁺.
  3. Assimilation: plants take up nitrogen (they prefer NH₄⁺ over NO₃⁻) to build proteins and nucleic acids.
  4. Denitrification: anaerobic bacteria (Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus, Micrococcus) convert nitrates back to N₂ gas, returning it to the atmosphere.
  • Also produces N₂O (nitrous oxide), a greenhouse gas with a global-warming potential ~300× that of CO₂.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know (N-cycle microbes): Fixation = Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Klebsiella. Nitrification = Nitrosomonas (→NO₂⁻), Nitrobacter (→NO₃⁻). Denitrification = Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus, Micrococcus.

6.2.4 The Phosphorus Cycle

  • Phosphorus is essential for DNA, RNA, ATP, cell membranes (phospholipids) and bones.
  • Occurs as phosphate ions (PO₄³⁻).
  • Weathering of rocks releases phosphate into soil and water; plants take it up; it moves through food chains; decomposers return it to soil.
  • No significant gaseous phase (unlike C and N cycles).
  • Excess phosphate (and nitrate) run-off into water → eutrophication → algal blooms → O₂ depletion → death of aquatic life.

6.3 Conservation of Natural Resources

6.3.1 Soil Fertility and Earthworms

  • Earthworms = "friends of farmers": they aerate the soil (burrowing), mix and improve soil structure, and their casts (waste) add humus and nutrients, increasing fertility and water-holding capacity.

6.3.2 Biofertilizers and Vermicompost

  • Biofertilizers: living organisms (e.g., Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Azospirillum, blue-green algae) that enhance soil fertility naturally.
  • Vermicompost: compost produced by earthworms digesting organic waste — a rich, eco-friendly fertilizer.
  • These are sustainable, eco-friendly alternatives to chemical fertilizers (which can cause pollution and soil degradation).

6.3.3 Crop Rotation and Legumes

  • Crop rotation: growing different crops in sequence on the same land.
  • Growing legumes (peas, beans, clover) in rotation fixes nitrogen (via Rhizobium), naturally replenishing soil fertility — reducing the need for synthetic nitrogen fertilizers.

6.3.4 Conservation of Natural Resources

  • Natural resources = water, soil, forests, minerals, biodiversity, air.
  • Conservation: careful, sustainable use and protection of natural resources for present and future generations.
  • Methods: reforestation, controlled grazing, terracing, water conservation, sustainable agriculture, recycling, protecting endangered species, establishing national parks.

Haploid (n) – cell with one set of chromosomes (23 in human gametes).

Homeostasis – maintenance of a stable internal environment.

Hydrogen bond – weak bond between water molecules (gives water its properties).

Hydrolysis – breaking molecules by adding water (during digestion).

Innate immunity – non-specific, from-birth defenses (skin, phagocytes).

Inspiration – breathing in (diaphragm contracts, volume increases).

Interphase – growth + DNA synthesis phase of the cell cycle (G₁, S, G₂).

Karyokinesis – nuclear division (mitosis or meiosis).

Lacteal – lymphatic capillary in a villus absorbing fats.

Lipid – water-insoluble organic molecule (fats, oils, phospholipids).

Lymph – tissue fluid collected into lymph vessels.

Lymphocyte – white blood cell (T or B cell) responsible for specific immunity.

Macronutrient – mineral needed in larger amounts (Na, Mg, K, Cl, P, S).

Meiosis – reduction division producing 4 haploid gametes.

Micronutrient – mineral needed in trace amounts (Fe, Cu, I, Mn).

Mitosis – division producing 2 identical diploid somatic cells.

Monomer – single molecular unit that forms polymers.

Monosaccharide – single sugar unit (glucose, galactose, fructose).

Nephron – functional unit of the kidney.

Nitrification – conversion of ammonia to nitrite then nitrate.

Nitrogen fixation – conversion of N₂ to ammonia by bacteria.

Nucleic acid – DNA or RNA (polynucleotides of nucleotides).

Nucleotide – base + pentose sugar + phosphate.

Organic molecule – compound containing both carbon and hydrogen.

Peptide bond – covalent bond joining amino acids (formed by condensation).

Phagocyte – white cell that engulfs pathogens (neutrophil, macrophage).

Phospholipid – lipid with hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tails (membrane bilayer).

Plasma – liquid part of blood (~55%).

Platelet – blood cell fragment helping clotting.

Polymer – long chain of many monomers joined by covalent bonds.

Polypeptide – chain of many amino acids.

Precipitation – water returning to earth as rain/snow/hail.

Producer – organism making its own food (autotroph, green plant).

Pulmonary circulation – heart → lungs → heart (O₂ pickup).

Pyramid of energy – always-upright diagram of energy through trophic levels.

Reduction division – meiosis (halving chromosome number to n).

Ribose – 5-carbon sugar in RNA.

Saliva – fluid with salivary amylase starting starch digestion.

Sister chromatids – two identical copies of one chromosome.

Spindle fibre – protein fibre pulling chromosomes apart in anaphase.

Starch – plant storage polysaccharide.

Systole – contraction phase of the heart (pumping out blood).

Tachycardia – heart rate above 100 beats/min.

Trophic level – feeding position in a food chain.

Tubular reabsorption – return of useful substances to blood in the nephron.

Vaccination – introducing weakened antigen to build immunity/memory.

Villus – finger-like projection of small-intestine wall increasing absorption area.

Water potential – tendency of water to move (drives plant transport).
