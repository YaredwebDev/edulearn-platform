UNIT 1: CHEMICAL REACTIONS AND STOICHIOMETRY

Unit outcome: By the end of this unit you must be able to define chemical reactions, write & balance equations, identify the four types of reactions, redox reactions, oxidation numbers, mole concept, molecular/formula/empirical formulas, and solve all stoichiometry problems.

1.1 Introduction

Physical change – a change that does NOT form a new substance with a new chemical composition.

  • Examples: evaporation of liquids, powdering sugar, melting ice.

Chemical change – a change that forms one or more new substances with a new chemical composition.

  • Examples: milk → curd, photosynthesis, rotting of egg, rusting of iron, burning wood.

A chemical reaction is a process in which reacting substances (called reactants) are converted into new substances (called products).

Characteristics of a chemical reaction (evidences):

  1. 1. Formation of new substances
  2. 2. Production of heat and/or light
  3. 3. Change in colour
  4. 4. Change in temperature

Chemical equation – shorthand notation showing what happens during a reaction: `Reactants → Products`.

Stoichiometry – the quantitative study of reactants and products in a chemical reaction.

> Exam tip: Be able to classify a given change as physical or chemical, and give evidence for a chemical change.

1.2 Chemical Equations

A chemical equation uses chemical symbols/formulas. Reactants on the LEFT, products on the RIGHT, arrow (→) means "to produce/to form/to yield". '+' means "reacts with".

State symbols:

  • (s) = solid, (l) = liquid, (g) = gas, (aq) = aqueous (dissolved in water)

Diatomic molecules (7 elements that exist as pairs): H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. Must be written as molecules in equations.

Steps to write a chemical equation:

  1. 1. Write the word equation.
  2. 2. Write symbols/formulas (unbalanced).
  3. 3. Balance the equation.
Example: Calcium carbonate + sulphuric acid → calcium sulphate + water + carbon dioxide
  • `CaCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(l) → CaSO₄(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)`

Qualitative vs quantitative meaning:

  • Qualitatively – shows types (identity) of reactants and products.
  • Quantitatively – shows the relative amounts (moles, molecules, masses).
  • e.g. `2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O` means 2 units hydrogen + 1 unit oxygen → 2 units water.

1.2.1 Balancing Chemical Equations

Based on the Law of Conservation of Mass: atoms are neither created nor destroyed, so the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides.

To balance = place appropriate coefficients (numbers) in front of formulas. NEVER change subscripts.

A) Inspection (trial & error) method – best for simple equations.

Example: `Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂`

  1. 1. Tabulate atoms on each side.
  2. 2. Balance Fe: 3Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  3. 3. Balance O: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  4. 4. Balance H: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ ✅

B) LCM method – find LCM of total valencies.

Example: `Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃`

  • Al valency 3, O₂ total valency 4; in Al₂O₃: Al total 6, O total 6. LCM = 12.
  • Coefficients: 12/3=4 for Al, 12/4=3 for O₂, 12/6=2 for Al₂O₃
  • 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ ✅

C) Algebraic method – assign variables (a,b,c...) to each species, set up atom-balance equations, solve.

Example: `N₂ + H₂ → NH₃`

  • aN₂ + bH₂ → cNH₃
  • N: 2a = c; H: 2b = 3c
  • Let a = 1 → c = 2 → 2b = 6 → b = 3
  • N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ ✅
  • If fractions arise (e.g. Al + O₂), multiply all by the lowest common denominator.

> Exam tip: Practice balancing: Na+H₂O→NaOH+H₂; KClO₃→KCl+O₂; H₂O₂→H₂O+O₂; Al+H₃PO₄→AlPO₄+H₂; C₆H₁₂O₆+O₂→CO₂+H₂O; FeCl₃+MgO→Fe₂O₃+MgCl₂; P₄O₁₀+H₂O→H₃PO₄.

1.3 Types of Chemical Reactions (the 4 basic types)

  1. 1. Combination (synthesis): `A + B → AB` (two or more → single product)
  2. `2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl` (element+element)
  3. `CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃` (compound+compound)
  4. Only ONE product.
  1. 2. Decomposition (analysis): `AB → A + B` (single compound → two or more)
  2. Carried out by heat (Δ), light, electricity, or catalyst.
  3. `2HgO(s) →Δ 2Hg(l) + O₂(g)`
  4. `2KClO₃(s) →Δ 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g)`
  1. 3. Single displacement (replacement): `A + BC → B + AC` (a more reactive element displaces a less reactive one)
  2. Metal + acid → salt + H₂: `Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂` (but `Cu + HCl → no reaction`)
  3. Metal + water: `2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂`
  4. More active metal displaces less active: `Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu`; `Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu`
  1. 4. Double displacement (metathesis): `AB + CD → AD + CB` (exchange of positive & negative ions)
  2. `AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃`
  3. `BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NaCl`
  4. `Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂`

> Some reactions fit more than one type. A complete understanding helps predict products of unknown reactions.

1.4 Oxidation and Reduction Reactions (Redox)

Oxidation = loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number).

Reduction = gain of electrons (decrease in oxidation number).

These occur simultaneously → a redox reaction involves transfer of electrons from one atom to another.

Example: `Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu` (Zn is oxidized, Cu²⁺ is reduced).

1.4.1 Oxidation Number / Oxidation State

The number of electrons an atom appears to have gained or lost when combined with other atoms.

Rules for assigning oxidation numbers:

  1. 1. Uncombined element = 0 (also for S₈, P₄, O₂, O₃).
  2. 2. Monatomic ion = its charge (Na⁺=+1, Mg²⁺=+2, S²⁻=−2).
  3. 3. Oxygen in compounds = −2, EXCEPT peroxides (−1, e.g. Na₂O₂), superoxides (−½, e.g. KO₂), and OF₂ (+2).
  4. 4. Hydrogen in compounds = +1, EXCEPT metal hydrides (−1, e.g. NaH, CaH₂, AlH₃).
  5. 5. Sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound = 0.
  6. 6. Sum in a polyatomic ion = charge on the ion.
  7. 7. Group IA = +1, Group IIA = +2 in all their compounds.
  8. 8. The more electronegative element gets the negative oxidation number.
Worked examples:
  • In HNO₃: H=+1, O=each −2. N: x + 1 + 3(−2) = 0 → x = +5.
  • Cr₂O₇²⁻: 2y + 7(−2) = −2 → y = +6.
  • MnO₄⁻: x + 4(−2) = −1 → x = +7.
  • Ca(H₂PO₄)₂: +2 + 4(+1) + 2x + 8(−2) = 0 → x = +5.

1.4.2 Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

  • Reducing agent – the substance that loses electrons (is oxidized), causes reduction. (Becomes more positive.)
  • Oxidizing agent – the substance that gains electrons (is reduced), causes oxidation. (Becomes more negative.)

| Reducing agent | Oxidizing agent |

|---|---|

| Loses electrons | Gains electrons |

| Causes reduction | Causes oxidation |

| Undergoes oxidation | Undergoes reduction |

| Becomes more positive | Becomes more negative |

Common oxidizing agents: KMnO₄ (purple→colourless), K₂Cr₂O₇ (orange→green), Cl₂, potassium chromate, sodium chlorate, MnO₂.

Common reducing agents: C, CO, Na₂S₂O₃, Na₂SO₃, iron(II) salts, KI, H₂S.

Factors affecting oxidizing/reducing ability:

  • Electronegativity: high EN (F₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂) = good oxidizing agents; low EN (Na, K, Mg, Al) = good reducing agents.
  • Oxidation state: high oxidation state → oxidizing agent (KMn⁺⁷O₄, NaCl⁺⁷O₄, K₂Cr⁺⁶₂O₇); low oxidation state → reducing agent (Fe⁺²S, C⁺²O, Na₂S⁺⁴O₃).

Analyzing a redox reaction:

Example: `2Fe + 3O₂ → Fe₂O₃` (Fe 0→+3 oxidized = reducing agent; O 0→−2 reduced = oxidizing agent).

Example: `Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂` (Mg 0→+2 = reducing agent; H +1→0, so HCl is oxidizing agent).

1.4.4 Balancing Redox by Oxidation-Number-Change Method

  1. 1. Assign oxidation numbers to all atoms.
  2. 2. Identify atoms oxidized & reduced and their change.
  3. 3. Make total increase = total decrease (use LCM).

Example: `Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂`

  • Fe +3→0 (decrease 3, ×2 = 6); C +2→+4 (increase 2, ×3 = 6)
  • Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ ✅

Example: `HNO₃ + H₃AsO₃ → NO + H₃AsO₄ + H₂O`

  • N +5→+2 (decrease 3); As +3→+5 (increase 2). Use 2 N for every 3 As.
  • 2HNO₃ + 3H₃AsO₃ → 2NO + 3H₃AsO₄ + H₂O ✅

Non-redox reactions: oxidation states do NOT change. Examples: neutralization and double displacement.

  • `Na₂SO₄ + CaCl₂ → CaSO₄ + 2NaCl`; `CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂`; `KOH + HNO₃ → KNO₃ + H₂O`

1.5 Molecular & Formula Masses, Mole Concept, Chemical Formulas

1.5.1 Molecular Mass (MM) & Formula Mass (FM)

Molecular mass (MM) = sum of masses of all atoms in a molecule.

  • MM of H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = 18 amu. MM of CO₂ = 12 + 2(16) = 44 amu.

Formula mass (FM) = sum of atomic masses in the formula unit (used mostly for ionic compounds).

  • FM of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 amu.
  • FM of Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2(16+2) = 40 + 36 = 76 amu.

1.5.2 The Mole Concept

Mole = the amount of substance containing the same number of entities as atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12 = 6.022 × 10²³ units. This number is Avogadro's number (Nₐ).

  • 1 mole H₂O = 6.02×10²³ molecules H₂O.
  • 1 mole NaCl = 6.02×10²³ formula units NaCl.

Molar mass = molecular/formula mass expressed in grams = mass of 1 mole of a substance.

  • Molar mass H₂O = 18 g/mol; NaCl = 58.5 g/mol.

1.5.3 Empirical and Molecular Formulas

Empirical (simplest) formula – smallest whole-number ratio of atoms (e.g. benzene CH, glucose CH₂O).

Molecular formula – actual exact number of atoms (benzene C₆H₆, glucose C₆H₁₂O₆).

Percent composition by mass:

```

% element = (n × molar mass of element) / (molar mass of compound) × 100

```

where n = number of moles of that element in 1 mole of compound.

  • In H₂O₂ (34.02 g/mol): %H = (2×1.008/34.02)×100 = 5.93%; %O = (2×16/34.02)×100 = 94.06%.

Steps to find empirical formula:

  1. 1. Convert each element's mass (or % given → treat as grams) to moles: moles = mass/molar mass.
  2. 2. Divide each by the smallest number of moles.
  3. 3. Multiply by an integer to get whole numbers.
Example: 43.6% P, 56.4% O.
  • mol P = 43.6/31 = 1.41; mol O = 56.4/16 = 3.53.
  • P: 1.41/1.41=1; O: 3.53/1.41=2.5 → ×2 → P₂O₅. ✅

Molecular formula = empirical formula × n, where n = molar mass of molecular formula ÷ molar mass of empirical formula.

  • P₂O₅ molar mass = 142; molecular mass 284 → n = 284/142 = 2 → P₄O₁₀.
  • Ethylene glycol: 38.7% C, 9.75% H, rest O (51.55% O), MM = 62.07. Empirical = CH₃O (mass 31.03), n = 62.07/31.03 = 2 → C₂H₆O₂.

1.6 Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry = study of the quantitative composition of substances and relationships between reactants & products (moles, mass, volume).

Stoichiometric calculations are based on:

  • Definite formula of each substance.
  • Law of Conservation of Mass.

Calculations are done ONLY for balanced equations.

Molar ratio from balanced equation:

`N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃` means:

  • 1 molecule N₂ + 3 molecules H₂ → 2 molecules NH₃ (molecular)
  • 1 mol N₂ + 3 mol H₂ → 2 mol NH₃ (molar)
  • 28 g N₂ + 6.06 g H₂ → 34 g NH₃ (mass)

1.6.2 Mass–Mass Relationships

Conversion factor method (general):

```

gram A × (1 mol A/molar mass A) × (mole ratio) × (molar mass B/1 mol B) = gram B

```

For mole–mole: `mole A × mole ratio = mole B`.

Example: How many g Al to produce 20.4 g Al₂O₃? `4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃`
  • Via proportion: x/108 = 20.4/204 → x = 10.8 g Al.
  • Via conversion: 20.4 g × (1/102) × (4/2) × 27 = 10.8 g Al.
Example: 22 g CO₂ reacts with CaO. `CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃`
  • mol CO₂ = 22/44 = 0.5 mol → mol CaO = 0.5 mol → mass = 0.5 × 56 = 28 g CaO.
Example: 28 g Fe produced, `Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂`
  • mol Fe = 28/56 = 0.5 → mol CO = 0.75 → mass CO = 0.75 × 28 = 21 g.

Mole–mass: mol₁ → mol₂ → mass₂. Mass–mole: mass₁ → mol₂.

1.6.3 Volume–Volume Relationships

Molar volume at STP (0°C, 1 atm): 1 mole of any gas = 22.4 L.

Avogadro's Law: equal volumes of different gases at same T & P contain equal numbers of molecules. So V ∝ n (moles).

Example: `CH₄ + 2O₂ → 2H₂O + CO₂`. What volume O₂ reacts to produce 44.8 L CO₂ at STP?
  • x/44.8 = 44.8/22.4 → x = 89.6 L O₂.

1.6.4 Mass–Volume Relationships

Put masses on one side, volumes on the other.

Example: `Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂`. Grams Mg to produce 5.6 L H₂ at STP?
  • x/24 = 5.6/22.4 → x = 6 g Mg.

1.6.5 Limiting and Excess Reactants

Limiting reactant – the reactant completely consumed first; it LIMITS/determines the amount of product formed.

Excess reactant – present in greater quantity than needed to react with the limiting reactant.

To find limiting reactant: calculate the product yield using EACH reactant; the reactant giving the smaller yield is the limiting reactant.

Example: `Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂`, 0.30 mol Mg + 0.52 mol HCl.
  • Using Mg: 0.30 mol → 0.30 mol H₂.
  • Using HCl: 0.52/2 = 0.26 mol H₂.
  • HCl is limiting (gives less), Mg is excess. H₂ produced = 0.26 mol.
Example: `2CH₃CHO + O₂ → 2HC₂H₃O₂`, 20 g CH₃CHO (44 g/mol) + 10 g O₂ (32 g/mol).
  • mol CH₃CHO = 0.454; mol O₂ = 0.312.
  • CH₃CHO gives 0.454 mol acid; O₂ gives 0.624 mol acid.
  • Limiting = acetaldehyde. Acid produced = 0.454 mol × 60 g/mol = 27.2 g.
  • O₂ consumed = 0.454 × (1/2) = 0.227 mol; excess O₂ = 0.312 − 0.227 = 0.085 mol (2.7 g).

1.6.6 Theoretical, Actual and Percentage Yields

  • Theoretical yield = calculated amount of product if the reaction goes to completion.
  • Actual yield = measured amount actually obtained (usually less than theoretical).
  • Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100.
Example: 24.8 g CaCO₃ heated gives 13.1 g CaO. `CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂`
  • Theoretical: x/56 = 24.8/100 → x = 13.9 g CaO.
  • % yield = (13.1/13.9) × 100 = 94.2%.

UNIT 2: SOLUTIONS

Unit outcome: types of solutions, solution process, rate of dissolution, heat of solution, solubility, concentration units, preparing solutions, solution stoichiometry, and reactions in solution.

Mixtures vs Substances

  • Mixture: each component keeps its own properties; components separable physically; variable proportions.
  • Substance (element/compound): definite physical & chemical properties; separable only by chemical process.

2.1 Heterogeneous and Homogeneous Mixtures

  • Homogeneous mixture: uniform composition throughout, no visible boundaries, evenly distributed. Often confused with a pure substance (but composition of a solution varies).
  • Heterogeneous mixture: physically distinct parts, each with different properties; at least two visible phases. E.g. oil+water, sand+water.

Classification of mixtures: suspensions, solutions, colloids.

A. Suspension – heterogeneous mixture where solid particles are spread through liquid WITHOUT dissolving. Examples: chalk+water, sand+water, slaked lime+water, muddy water. Particles settle (removable by filtration).

B. Solution – homogeneous mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent; particles are individual atoms/molecules/ions.

  • Solvent = major component; Solute = minor component (if both 50%, either can be solute).
  • All solutions are homogeneous mixtures, but NOT all homogeneous mixtures are solutions.
  • Properties: single uniform phase, no visible boundaries, does not settle on standing, passes through filter paper, stable.

Types of solutions (by physical state):

  1. 1. Gaseous solutions (gas-gas): atmosphere (N₂ solvent), natural gas.
  2. 2. Liquid solutions (solute solid/liquid/gas in liquid solvent): soda (gas in liquid), alcohol (liquid-liquid), seawater (solid in liquid).
  3. 3. Solid solutions: alloys (solid-solid), dental filling (Hg-Ag), H₂ in Pd (gas in solid), wax.

C. Colloid – heterogeneous mixture of insoluble particles (1–100 nm) suspended uniformly in another substance.

  • Properties: appears homogeneous but is heterogeneous; particles 1–100 nm (between solution & suspension); pass through filter paper; two phases (dispersed phase = smaller amount, dispersion medium = larger amount); stable; shows Tyndall effect (scattering of a light beam, making its path visible). True solutions do NOT show Tyndall effect.

Types of colloids (8): aerosol (liquid/solid in gas: fog, smoke), foam (gas in liquid: shaving cream), emulsion (liquid-liquid: milk, face cream), sol (solid-liquid: milk of magnesia, mud), foam (gas in solid: sponge, pumice), gel (liquid-solid: jelly, cheese, butter), solid sol (solid-solid: gemstones).

Coagulation – process by which dispersed phase of a colloid aggregates & separates (e.g. curdling of milk).

Association colloid – colloid whose dispersed phase is made of micelles (e.g. soap in water). Soap = sodium stearate; the hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail points inward, hydrophilic carboxyl head faces water → cleansing action.

Micelle – colloidal-sized aggregate formed when molecules having hydrophobic + hydrophilic ends are dispersed in water.

2.2 The Solution Process

Solution formation = physical process (NOT chemical change). Involves three steps:

  1. 1. Break solute–solute attractions (endothermic).
  2. 2. Break solvent–solvent attractions (endothermic).
  3. 3. Form solute–solvent interactions (mixing) (exothermic).

"Like dissolves like" – substances with similar intermolecular forces dissolve in each other. Ionic/polar compounds dissolve in polar solvents (NaCl, ethanol in water); nonpolar compounds dissolve in nonpolar solvents (I₂, oil, hexane in CCl₄, benzene).

Polar vs nonpolar: polarity depends on electronegativity difference, shape, and net dipole moment. H₂, O₂, N₂, CH₄, CCl₄, CO₂ are nonpolar; H₂O, NH₃, HCl, H₂S, CH₃OH are polar. (CO₂ is nonpolar because its two dipoles cancel; H₂O is polar because dipoles add up.)

Intermolecular forces (order weakest → strongest):

```

Dispersion (London) < dipole-induced dipole < dipole-dipole < H-bonding < ion-dipole

```

  • Dipole–dipole: between polar molecules (e.g. HBr in H₂O).
  • Dipole–induced dipole: polar molecule distorts nonpolar molecule's electron cloud (O₂, N₂, noble gases in water).
  • Dispersion (London) forces: between ALL molecules; due to constantly changing electron distribution; important for nonpolar.
  • Ion–dipole: principal force when ionic compound dissolves in water (NaCl in H₂O).
  • Ion–induced dipole: ion distorts a nearby nonpolar molecule (Fe²⁺ in hemoglobin binding O₂).
  • Hydrogen bond: attractive interaction between H bonded to electronegative O, N, or F in one molecule and lone pair on O, N, F of another. (In water, acetic acid, ammonia.)

Dipole moment (µ) – measure of molecular polarity, unit debye (D). 1 D = 3.34×10⁻³⁰ C·m.

2.2.2 / 2.2.3 Solutions of Ionic Solids & Solubility Rules

  • Soluble: ≥1 g dissolves in 100 mL water. Ionic compounds that dissolve exist as hydrated ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻ surrounded by water).
  • Solubility = max amount of substance that can dissolve in a given volume (usually 100 mL) of solvent at a given temperature.

Solubility rules (MEMORIZE):

  1. 1. All salts of Group 1A (Na⁺, K⁺, Li⁺) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) are soluble.
  2. 2. All acetates (CH₃COO⁻), nitrates (NO₃⁻), most perchlorates (ClO₄⁻) are soluble.
  3. 3. All chlorides, bromides, iodides are soluble EXCEPT those of Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Hg₂²⁺. All fluorides soluble except Pb²⁺ and Group 2A.
  4. 4. Most hydroxides are only slightly soluble. Group 1A hydroxides soluble; Ca, Sr, Ba slightly soluble; transition metals & Al³⁺ insoluble (Fe(OH)₃, Al(OH)₃ insoluble).
  5. 5. All sulfates (SO₄²⁻) soluble except Ba, Sr, Pb, Ca, Ag, Hg.
  6. 6. Except rule 1, carbonates (CO₃²⁻), sulfides (S²⁻), oxides (O²⁻), phosphates (PO₄³⁻) are insoluble.

2.2.4 Solutions of Liquids in Liquids

  • Miscible – two liquids that mix into a solution (similar polarity). e.g. ethanol + water.
  • Immiscible – liquids that cannot mix (different polarity). e.g. oil + water.

2.2.5 Rate of Dissolution

Dissolution = process by which a solute dissolves in a solvent.

Rate of dissolution = speed at which solute dissolves. Faster when solute–solvent interactions are stronger than solute–solute & solvent–solvent. Increasing surface area, stirring/agitation, and temperature increase rate of dissolution.

2.2.6 Energy Changes in Solution Process

  • Solvation energy = energy released when solute is solvated by solvent.
  • Hydration energy = energy released when solute is solvated by WATER.
  • Heat of solution (enthalpy of solution, ΔH_soln) = heat released or absorbed during dissolution.
  • `q = m × c × ΔT` (q in J, m = mass of solution, ΔT = T_final − T_initial, c = specific heat ~4.184 J/g°C for dilute aqueous)
  • `ΔH_soln = q ÷ moles of solute` (in kJ/mol)

Endothermic dissolution: mixing energy < energy to break forces → absorbs heat → solution gets cold (e.g. NH₄NO₃, KNO₃). Used in instant cold packs.

Exothermic dissolution: mixing energy > breaking energy → releases heat → solution gets hot (e.g. NaOH, CaCl₂, MgSO₄). Used in hot packs.

Ideal solution: ΔH_soln = 0 (solute-solute & solvent-solvent strengths similar to solute-solvent). e.g. ideal gases, methanol+ethanol, pentane+hexane.

Lattice energy (ΔH_lat): energy needed to break ions apart from crystal lattice (endothermic → works against dissolving). Lattice energy increases with higher ion charge, decreases with larger ion distance. Ionic solids with large lattice energy are usually less soluble.

ΔH_soln = ΔH_lat + ΔH_hyd.

Calorimeter – device used to measure heat involved in a chemical/physical process.

2.3 Solubility as an Equilibrium Process

Solution equilibrium – when rate of dissolution = rate of recrystallization (dynamic equilibrium). Recrystallization = dissolved solute returning to solid state.

Types of solutions:

  • Saturated: at equilibrium, contains maximum dissolved solute at a given temperature (with undissolved solute present).
  • Unsaturated: has not reached maximum solubility; more solute can still dissolve.
  • Supersaturated: contains more dissolved solute than the normal saturation point (made by increased temperature/pressure; meta-stable). Adding a seed crystal causes rapid crystallization (used in hand warmers).

Factors affecting solubility:

  1. 1. Intermolecular forces (predominant).
  2. 2. Temperature:
  3. Solids: solubility usually INCREASES with temperature (more sugar in hot tea). e.g. Na₂SO₄ rises >10-fold from 0°C→32.4°C; NaCl 36 g/100 mL at 25°C → 39 g at 100°C.
  4. Gases: solubility DECREASES with increasing temperature.
  5. 3. Pressure: affects gases only (Henry's Law). Solids/liquids incompressible → pressure has little effect.

Henry's Law: the solubility (concentration) of a gas is directly proportional to its partial pressure above the solution.

```

C = kP (C = concentration of dissolved gas, P = partial pressure, k = Henry's constant in mol/L·atm)

C₁/P₁ = C₂/P₂

```

Gases that react with water or form H-bonds (HCl, H₂S, NH₃) do NOT obey Henry's Law (much more soluble).

Worked example: CO₂ in a cola bottle at P = 4 atm, k = 3.3×10⁻² mol/L·atm.
  • C = kP = 3.3×10⁻² × 4 = 0.132 ≈ 0.1 mol/L.
Worked example: N₂ solubility at 25°C & 1 atm is 6.8×10⁻⁴ mol/L. Partial pressure of N₂ in atmosphere = 0.78 atm. Find concentration.
  • C₂ = P₂C₁/P₁ = (0.78 × 6.8×10⁻⁴)/1 = 5.3×10⁻⁴ mol/L.

2.4 Ways of Expressing Concentration of Solutions

Concentration = relative quantity of solute against total quantity of solution or solvent.

2.4.1 Percent by Mass/Volume

```

% (w/w) solute = (mass solute / mass solution) × 100 [mass solution = solute + solvent]

% (v/v) = (volume solute / volume solution) × 100

% (w/v) = (mass solute / volume solution) × 100

```

Example: 13.5 g glucose in 0.100 kg water → 13.5/(13.5+100) ×100 = 11.9%.
Example: 0.9 g NaCl in 100 mL → %(w/v) = 0.9%.

Parts per million / billion:

```

ppm = (mass solute / mass solution) × 10⁶

ppb = (mass solute / mass solution) × 10⁹

```

  • 1 ppm = 1 mg/L for dilute aqueous solutions.
  • Example: 2.5 g groundwater contains 5.4 µg Pb²⁺. ppm = (5.4×10⁻⁶/2.5)×10⁶ = 2.16 ppm.

2.4.2 Mole Fraction

```

X_A = moles of A / total moles of solution (solute + solvent)

X_solute + X_solvent = 1

Mole % = mole fraction × 100

```

Example: 30 g I₂ in 400 g CCl₄.
  • mol I₂ = 30/254 = 0.12; mol CCl₄ = 400/154 = 2.6.
  • X_I₂ = 0.12/(0.12+2.6) = 0.044; X_CCl₄ = 1 − 0.044 = 0.96.

2.4.3 Molarity (M)

```

Molarity = moles of solute / volume of solution in L

moles = mass / molar mass; moles = M × V

```

Most common & most useful for reaction stoichiometry. Units: mol/L (M).

Drawbacks: temperature affects volume (and thus M); volumes may not be additive.

Example: 16.0 g CH₃OH (32 g/mol) in 200 mL.
  • M = (16/32)/0.2 = 2.50 M.
Example: moles NaOH in 2.50 L of 0.100 M → n = 0.100 × 2.50 = 0.25 mol.

2.4.4 Molality (m)

```

Molality = moles of solute / mass of solvent in kg

```

Based on mass (not volume). Advantages: independent of temperature; masses are additive. For dilute aqueous solutions, molality ≈ molarity (since 1 L water ≈ 1 kg).

Example: 32.0 g CaCl₂ (111 g/mol) in 271 g water.
  • m = (32/111)/0.271 = 1.06 m.

2.4.5 Normality (N)

```

Normality = number of gram equivalents of solute / volume of solution in L

Number of gram equivalents = (mass / molar mass) × z

Equivalent weight = molar mass / z

N = (mass / (molar mass × V_L)) × z

N = M × z

```

z = number of transferable H⁺/OH⁻ ions (acid-base), charges (precipitation), or electrons (redox).

  • z = 1 for HCl, 1 for NaOH; 2 for H₂SO₄, Ca(OH)₂, Na₂CO₃; 3 for H₃PO₄; 6 for Al₂(SO₄)₃.
  • Normality is the ONLY concentration unit that is reaction-dependent (z can vary for polyprotic acids).
Example: 18.25 g HCl (36.5) → eq = (18.25/36.5)×1 = 0.5 eq. 98 g H₂SO₄ (98) → eq = (98/98)×2 = 2 eq.
Example: Normality of 0.5 M H₂SO₄ = M×z = 0.5×2 = 1 N.

2.4.6 Conversion of Concentration Units

These are "intrinsic" units (any portion of a solution has the same ratio).

  • Mole fraction → molality: use moles of solute & convert moles of solvent to kg.

Example: X_glucose = 0.15, X_water = 0.85. m = 0.15/(0.85×18/1000) = 9.8 m.

  • Molality → molarity: need density. Example: 0.273 m KCl, density 1.011×10³ g/L, molar mass 74.6. V_solution = [(0.273×74.6)+1000]/(1.011×10³) = 1.01 L; M = 0.273/1.01 = 0.270 M.
  • (w/w)% → molarity: 36.5% HCl, density 1.20 g/mL. 36.5 g HCl in 100 g solution → 1 mol HCl; V = 100/1.2 = 83.3 mL = 0.0833 L; M = 1/0.0833 = 12.0 M.

2.5 Preparation of Solutions

Method 1 — By dissolving a known mass of solute to a given final volume:

  • Weigh the required mass of solute (mass = moles × molar mass), dissolve in solvent in a volumetric flask, add solvent to the mark.

Method 2 — By dilution (from a concentrated stock solution):

Number of moles of solute does NOT change when you add solvent:

```

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ (dilution formula)

V₁ = M₂V₂ / M₁

```

Example: Prepare 250 mL of 2.25 M H₂SO₄ from 7.41 M stock.
  • V₁ = (2.25 × 250)/7.41 = 75.9 mL. Withdraw 75.9 mL stock, add water to 250 mL.

> SAFETY: When diluting concentrated acids, ALWAYS add acid slowly to water (never water to acid) — otherwise it heats & splatters.

2.6 Solution Stoichiometry

Additional step: convert solution volume to moles using n = M × V(L). General steps:

  1. 1. Balance equation.
  2. 2. Find moles of one substance from volume & molarity.
  3. 3. Relate to stoichiometrically equivalent amount of another.
  4. 4. Convert to desired units.

Mole–Mole: What M NaOH reacts with equal volume of 0.104 M H₂SO₄? `H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O`

  • n_NaOH/n_H₂SO₄ = 2/1 → M_NaOH = 2 × 0.104 = 0.208 M.

Mole–Mass: 45 mL of 0.63 M Mg(NO₃)₂ with excess NaOH → mass Mg(OH)₂ (58.321 g/mol).

  • 2NaOH + Mg(NO₃)₂ → Mg(OH)₂ + 2NaNO₃
  • mass = 0.63 × 0.045 × 58.321 = 1.65 g.

Mass–Volume: volume of 0.0995 M Al(NO₃)₃ reacting with 3.66 g Ag. `3Ag + Al(NO₃)₃ → 3AgNO₃ + Al`

  • V = (3.66 × 1)/(3 × 107.97 × 0.0995) = 0.114 L.

Mole–Volume: volume of 0.35 M AgNO₃ to react with 55 mL of 0.24 M NaCl.

  • `NaCl + AgNO₃ → NaNO₃ + AgCl` (1:1)
  • V_AgNO₃ = (0.24 × 0.055)/0.35 = 0.0377 L = 37.7 mL.

Mole–Particles: 0.1 mol HCl → 0.1 × 6.022×10²³ = 6.022×10²² particles.

2.7 Describing Reactions in Solution

Molecular equation – reactants/products written as if they were molecular substances, with state symbols. e.g. `AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)`.

Complete ionic equation – shows dissolved ionic compounds as separate ions.

```

Ag⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) + Na⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)

```

Spectator ions – ions that appear on both sides and do not take part in the reaction (here Na⁺ and NO₃⁻).

Net ionic equation – cancel spectator ions; shows the actual reaction:

```

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

```

Steps to write a net ionic equation: (1) balanced molecular equation; (2) balanced complete ionic equation; (3) cancel spectator ions.

3.1 Introduction

Inorganic compounds = all compounds that do NOT contain carbon, EXCEPT simpler carbon compounds like oxides (CO₂), carbonates (CaCO₃), and carbides (SiC). They come from mineral constituents of the earth / non-living things.

Inorganic compounds are classified into four groups: oxides, acids, bases, salts.

  • Found in nature as silicates, oxides, carbonates, sulphides, sulphates, chlorides, nitrates.

3.2 Oxides

Oxide = binary compound of oxygen and any other element (metal, non-metal, or metalloid). Oxygen reacts with almost all elements except noble gases and inactive metals (Au, Pt, Pd).

Oxides classified as: Acidic, Basic, Amphoteric, Neutral, Peroxides.

A. Acidic Oxides (acid anhydrides)

  • Formed by non-metals (Groups 14–17). "Acid anhydride" = acid without water.
  • Chemical properties:
  • 1. Dissolve in water to form acids: `Acid anhydride + H₂O → Acid`
  • CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid)
  • SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (sulphurous acid)
  • 2. React with basic/metallic oxides → salt: `CO₂ + Na₂O → Na₂CO₃`; `SO₃ + CaO → CaSO₄`
  • 3. React with bases → salt + water (neutralization): `SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O`; `CO₂ + 2LiOH → Li₂CO₃ + H₂O`
  • NOTE: Not all non-metal oxides are acidic. CO and N₂O are non-metal oxides but are NEUTRAL. SiO₂ is acidic (neutralizes basic oxides) but doesn't react directly with water.

B. Basic Oxides (basic anhydrides)

  • Oxides of metals. Those that dissolve in water form alkaline solutions.
  • Chemical properties:
  • 1. Dissolve in water → base (alkali): `CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂`; `Li₂O + H₂O → 2LiOH`
  • 2. React with acidic oxides → salt: `BaO + SO₃ → BaSO₄`; `Na₂O + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃`
  • 3. React with acids → salt + water: `CaO + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O`; `CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O`
  • Examples: Li₂O, Na₂O, K₂O, MgO, CaO, BaO, CuO. (Insoluble basic oxides still react with acids: `FeO + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂O`.)
  • NOT all metal oxides are basic (Al₂O₃, ZnO are amphoteric).

Indicators: substances that identify acids/bases by colour change.

  • Universal indicator: red (strong acid) → yellow-orange (weak acid) → blue/purple (basic).
  • Litmus: red in acid, blue in base. Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in base. Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base.
  • Acidic oxides react with bases; basic oxides react with acids. Acidic oxides don't react with acids; basic oxides don't react with bases.

C. Amphoteric Oxides

  • Show BOTH acidic and basic behaviour. React with strong acids AND strong bases to form salt + water.
  • Examples: Al₂O₃, ZnO (also PbO, PbO₂, SnO, SnO₂).
  • Al₂O₃ + HCl → AlCl₃ + H₂O; Al₂O₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + H₂O
  • ZnO + HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O; ZnO + NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O
  • Amphoteric hydroxides: Al(OH)₃ reacts with both acids and bases.
  • Acidic, basic, and amphoteric oxides are all salt-forming oxides.

D. Neutral Oxides

  • React with neither acids nor bases; not salt-forming. Few in number.
  • Examples: H₂O, CO, N₂O, NO.

E. Peroxides

  • Contain oxygen with oxidation number −1, with a "–O–O–" link.
  • Examples: H₂O₂, Na₂O₂, CaO₂, BaO₂, SrO₂.
  • Formed by burning metals in sufficient oxygen: `2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂`; `Ca + O₂ → CaO₂`.
  • Chemical properties:
  • 1. Powerful oxidizing agents: `PbS + 4H₂O₂ → PbSO₄ + 4H₂O`; `2KI + H₂SO₄ + H₂O₂ → I₂ + K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O`
  • 2. React with aqueous acids → hydrogen peroxide: `Na₂O₂ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O₂`; `CaO₂ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O₂`

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂): colourless liquid, used as bleach & antiseptic. Decomposes (slow, sped up by MnO₂ or Pt): `2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂`. Strong oxidizing agent. Bleaching: oxidizes PbS (black) → PbSO₄ (white).

3.3 Acids

Arrhenius acid = substance that releases hydrogen ion (proton) H⁺ or hydronium ion H₃O⁺ in aqueous solution.

```

HA → H⁺ + A⁻ or HA + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + A⁻

```

Examples: HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄, HBr, HI, HF.

Classification of Acids

A. By number of ionizable H⁺:

  • Monoprotic: one ionizable H (HCl, HNO₃).
  • Diprotic (polyprotic): two ionizable H (H₂SO₄, H₂S, H₂CO₃).
  • Triprotic: three ionizable H (H₃PO₄).
  • NOTE: number of H atoms ≠ ionizable H. CH₃CH₂COOH has 6 H but is monoprotic. NH₃ has 3 H but is a base, not an acid.

B. By number of elements:

  • Binary acids: two elements (H + non-metal). e.g. HCl, HBr, HF, HI, H₂S.
  • Ternary (oxy-acids): three elements (H, O, non-metal). e.g. H₂SO₄, H₂CO₃, HClO₄, H₃PO₄.

General Properties of Acids

  1. 1. Sour taste (lemon, orange — citric acid). Never test taste in lab (corrosive, poisonous).
  2. 2. Change colour of indicators.
  3. 3. React with active metals → salt + H₂ gas:
  4. `H₂SO₄ + Zn → ZnSO₄ + H₂`; `6HCl + 2Al → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂`
  5. Concentrated HNO₃ & hot conc. H₂SO₄ are oxidizing acids: `Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O`; `Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O` (produce NO₂ and SO₂, not H₂).
  6. 4. React with carbonates & hydrogen carbonates → salt + water + CO₂:
  7. `2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂`; `HCl + NaHCO₃ → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂` (CO₂ turns limewater milky).
  8. 5. React with sulphites → salt + SO₂ + water: `CaSO₃ + H₂SO₄ → SO₂ + CaSO₄ + H₂O`.
  9. 6. React with bases (oxides & hydroxides) → salt + water (neutralization):
  10. `2HCl + MgO → MgCl₂ + H₂O`; `H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O`; `HNO₃ + KOH → KNO₃ + H₂O`.
  11. 7. Aqueous solutions are electrolytes (conduct electricity). Strong acids ionize completely (bright light); weak acids partly (dim light).

Strong vs Weak Acids

  • Strong acid: dissociates completely (single arrow →). HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄.
  • Weak acid: dissociates only partially (double arrow ⇌). CH₃COOH, H₂C₂O₄, H₂CO₃. Organic acids with –COOH are generally weak.

Concentrated vs Dilute Acids

  • Concentrated acid: relatively large amount of acid in a given solution.
  • Dilute acid: relatively small amount of acid.
  • Both strong and weak acids can be concentrated or dilute (based on amount/moles present).

Precautions handling acids: wear goggles/gloves/coat; if spilled wash with water then 10% Na₂CO₃; to dilute pour acid INTO water (never water into acid); if swallowed give weak bases (Mg(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃); use pipette bulb; acid in eye wash with water repeatedly & consult doctor.

pH and pH scale

Water autoionizes: `H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻`.

Ionic product of water: `K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴` at 25°C. In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M.

```

pH = −log[H⁺]

pOH = −log[OH⁻]

pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C)

```

  • pH scale: 0 to 14. pH < 7 = acidic; pH = 7 = neutral; pH > 7 = basic.
  • Lower pH = more acidic; each 1 unit of pH = 10× change in [H⁺].
  • pH 4 vs pH 6 → A (pH 4) is 100× more acidic.
Worked examples:
  • [H⁺] = 1.0×10⁻⁵ → pH = 5.
  • pH = 9 → [H⁺] = 10⁻⁹ M.
  • 0.01 M HCl → [H⁺]=0.01 → pH = 2.
  • 0.05 M H₂SO₄ → [H⁺]=2×0.05=0.1 → pH = 1.

Preparation of Acids

  1. 1. Acidic oxide + water → acid: `N₂O₅ + H₂O → 2HNO₃`; `P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄`.
  2. 2. Direct combination (binary acids): `H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl`; `H₂ + S → H₂S`.
  3. 3. Non-volatile acid method (volatile acids): `NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl`; `NaNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HNO₃`.

Uses of common acids

  • H₂SO₄ (leading industrial chemical): fertilizers (sulphate & phosphate), synthetic fibers, paints, drugs, detergents, paper, dyes, petroleum refining, metals, electrolyte in car batteries.
  • HCl: in gastric juice (digestion), pickling iron & steel, aniline dyes, drugs, films, PVC, recovering Mg from seawater.
  • HNO₃: explosives (TNT, trinitroglycerine), fertilizers (KNO₃, NH₄NO₃), rubber, chemicals, plastics, dyes, drugs.

3.4 Bases

Arrhenius base = substance that produces hydroxide ion OH⁻ when dissolved in water. Soluble bases are called alkalis.

  • `NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻`; `KOH → K⁺ + OH⁻`
  • Ammonia is a base though not ionic: `NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻`.

General properties of bases:

  1. 1. Bitter taste & soapy/slippery feeling (strong bases corrosive & poisonous — never taste).
  2. 2. Change indicator colour: red litmus → blue, phenolphthalein → pink, universal indicator → blue/purple.
  3. 3. Release OH⁻ in aqueous solution.
  4. 4. Neutralize acids/acidic oxides → salt + water: `NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O`; `2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O`.
  5. 5. Aqueous solutions conduct electricity (strong bases = good conductors).

Strong vs Weak Bases

  • Strong base: almost completely ionized; lots of OH⁻. e.g. LiOH, NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)₂ (Group IA & lower Group IIA hydroxides). Strong electrolytes.
  • Weak base: only partially ionized. e.g. NH₄OH, Mg(OH)₂, Ca(OH)₂. Weak electrolytes (dim light).

Concentrated vs Dilute Bases

  • Concentration = moles of base per litre. Concentrated = large amount; dilute = small amount. Both strong & weak bases may be concentrated or dilute.

Precautions: strong bases (NaOH, KOH) are caustic alkalis (can cause burning). Wear goggles/gloves/coat; if base in eye wash with water repeatedly & seek medical advice; if swallowed drink 1–2% acetic acid or lemon juice; if on skin wash with water then dilute (1%) weak acid.

pOH and relationship with pH

`pOH = −log[OH⁻]` and `pH + pOH = 14`.

Derived from K_w: [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ → log form gives pH + pOH = 14.

Example: 0.0001 M NaOH → [OH⁻]=10⁻⁴ → pOH=4 → pH = 14−4 = 10.

Preparation of Bases

  1. 1. Active metal (Group IA & IIA below Mg) + water: `2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂`; `2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂`; `Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂`.
  2. 2. Metal oxide (Group IA/IIA) + water: `Na₂O + 2H₂O → 2NaOH`; `BaO + 2H₂O → Ba(OH)₂`.
  3. 3. Double displacement: `Ba(OH)₂ + K₂SO₄ → 2KOH + BaSO₄↓`; `Ca(OH)₂ + Na₂CO₃ → 2NaOH + CaCO₃↓`.

Uses: NaOH (caustic soda/lye) — soaps, detergents, degreasers, oven & drain cleaners. Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime) — cement, lime water, neutralize acidic soil, CO₂ test.

3.5 Salts

Salt = ionic compound generally formed by neutralization of an acid with a base. Cation from base, anion from acid.

  • NaCl from NaOH + HCl; KNO₃ from KOH + HNO₃.
  • Also: ionic compounds formed when ionizable H of acids are partly/completely replaced by metal or ammonium ions.

Classification of Salts

  1. 1. Normal (neutral) salts: all ionizable H⁺ replaced completely. Neutral to litmus.
  2. `HCl + KOH → KCl + H₂O`; `HNO₃ + NH₄OH → NH₄NO₃ + H₂O`.
  3. 2. Acidic salts: only PART of ionizable H⁺ replaced. Turn blue litmus red (still release H⁺).
  4. `H₂SO₄ + KOH → KHSO₄ + H₂O`; `H₃PO₄ + NaOH → NaH₂PO₄ + H₂O`.
  5. Acidic salts react with bases → normal salts: `KHSO₄ + KOH → K₂SO₄ + H₂O`; `NaH₂PO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₃PO₄ + H₂O`.
  6. 3. Basic salts: not all hydroxide ions of base replaced by acid anions.
  7. `Al(OH)₃ + HCl → Al(OH)₂Cl + H₂O`; `Zn(OH)₂ + HNO₃ → Zn(OH)NO₃ + H₂O`.
  8. React further with acids → normal salts.

Preparation of Salts

Soluble salts → crystallization; insoluble salts → precipitation.

  1. 1. Acid + metal (direct displacement): `Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂`; `Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂` (avoid Group IA metals — too vigorous).
  2. 2. Acid + base (neutralization): `HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O`.
  3. 3. Acid + metallic oxide: `CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O`; `ZnO + 2HNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + H₂O`.
  4. 4. Acid + carbonate/bicarbonate: `Na₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → NaNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂`; `Ca(HCO₃)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2CO₂`.
  5. 5. Insoluble salts by double decomposition (precipitation): `AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃`; `Na₂CO₃ + CuSO₄ → CuCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄`.

Some important salts & uses

  • NaCl: food, raw material for Na/Cl₂/NaOH, ORS, baking soda (NaHCO₃), Na₂CO₃, freezing mixtures.
  • NH₄NO₃: nitrogenous fertilizer, explosives.
  • CuSO₄: Bordeaux mixture (with Ca(OH)₂) fungicide, electroplating, dyeing.
  • FeCl₃: waste-water treatment, etching printed circuits.
  • KNO₃: gunpowder (with C & S), explosives, fertilizer.
  • CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum): plastering walls, supporting fractured bones.
  • BaSO₄: barium meal for X-ray, white pigment.
  • FeSO₄: iron tablets (anemia).

Properties of Salts

  1. 1. Solubility varies (soluble, slightly soluble, insoluble).
  2. 2. Water absorption/release:
  3. Hygroscopic – absorb water from atmosphere but remain solid (anhydrous CuSO₄).
  4. Deliquescent – absorb water to form a solution (CaCl₂, NaNO₃, FeCl₃). Deliquescence = absorbing water to form solution.
  5. Efflorescent – lose water of crystallization to the atmosphere (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, Na₂SO₄·10H₂O). Efflorescence = loss of water of crystallization.
  6. All deliquescent are hygroscopic, but not all hygroscopic are deliquescent.
  7. 3. Aqueous solutions of soluble salts conduct electricity (release mobile ions).
  8. 4. Thermal stability:
  9. Carbonates: Group IIA carbonates decompose to metal oxide + CO₂: `MCO₃ → MO + CO₂`. Stability increases down the group. `Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂`; transition metal carbonates decompose: `CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂`.
  10. Nitrates: Group IIA & most transition metal nitrates → metal oxide + NO₂ + O₂: `2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂`; `2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂`.
  11. Na & K nitrates → nitrites + O₂: `2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂`; `2KNO₃ → 2KNO₂ + O₂`.
  12. Lithium behaves like Group IIA: `4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂`; `Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂`.

Chemical tests of ions

  • Flame tests: Li⁺ (crimson/red), Na⁺ (yellow), K⁺ (lilac/violet), Ca²⁺ (brick red), Sr²⁺ (red), Ba²⁺ (apple green).
  • Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺: NaOH/ammonia → coloured precipitates.
  • Sulphate: add acidified BaCl₂/Ba(NO₃)₂ → white precipitate of BaSO₄.
  • Carbonate/HCO₃⁻: acid → CO₂ (turns limewater milky); carbonate + CaCl₂ → white ppt.
  • Nitrate (brown ring test): nitrate + FeSO₄ + conc. H₂SO₄ → brown ring.

Plant Nutrients, Fertilizers, Pesticides, Herbicides

  • Macronutrients (needed in large amounts): N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, C, H, O.
  • Micronutrients (trace): Fe, Co, Zn, Mn, Mo, Cu, B, Cl.
  • N, P, K = primary mineral nutrients.
  • N absorbed as NO₃⁻; for amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll (green).
  • P absorbed as H₂PO₄⁻; early growth, root formation, seed/fruit, ATP.
  • K absorbed as K⁺; enzymes for photosynthesis, controls turgidity of cells.
  • Fertilizers – added to soil to increase crop growth. Organic (from plants/animals) or synthetic (artificial). Complete/NPK fertilizer contains N, P, K. Examples: (NH₄)₂SO₄, KNO₃, NH₄NO₃, DAP (NH₄)₂HPO₄. Fertilizer grade = % of N, P, K by dry weight (e.g. 10:10:10).
  • Pesticides – chemicals to kill pests.
  • Herbicides (weed killers): NaCl, CuSO₄, NaAsO₂, NH₄SO₃NH₂, NaClO₃.

UNIT 4: ENERGY CHANGES AND ELECTROCHEMISTRY

Unit outcome: energy changes in chemical reactions, how reactions produce electricity & electricity causes reactions, metallic vs electrolytic conduction, half-reactions & cell reactions for electrolysis, types of voltaic cells, and electrolytic vs voltaic cells.

4.1 Introduction — Energy Changes

  • Chemical reactions almost always involve energy change. e.g. `C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat & light`; `CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat & light`.

System & Surroundings:

  • Universe = System + Surroundings.
  • System = the part of the universe being studied (e.g. reaction mixture in a test tube).
  • Surroundings = everything else.
  • Internal energy (E) of a system = sum of its potential energy + kinetic energy.
  • Potential energy = stored energy (stored in chemical bonds).
  • Kinetic energy = energy of motion.
  • `ΔE = E_final − E_initial = E_products − E_reactants`.
  • Total energy of universe is constant; when system loses energy, surroundings gain it.
  • Energy transferred as heat (q) and/or work (w): `ΔE = q + w`.

4.1.1 Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

  • Exothermic: releases heat to surroundings (system heats up). ΔH < 0 (negative).
  • Combustion of coke/C, dissolving NaOH & CaCl₂ in water.
  • Endothermic: absorbs heat from surroundings (system cools down). ΔH > 0 (positive).
  • Dissolving KNO₃, NH₄NO₃ in water; photosynthesis.
  • At constant temperature & pressure, heat transferred = enthalpy of reaction:
  • `ΔH = H_products − H_reactants`
  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0 (products lower energy). Endothermic: ΔH > 0 (products higher energy).

Heat capacity = quantity of heat to change temperature by 1 K. `Heat capacity = q/ΔT` (units J/K).

Specific heat capacity (c) = heat to change 1 g by 1 K. `c = q/(mass × ΔT)`; so `q = c × mass × ΔT`.

  • Metals have low c (heat & cool quickly); water has very high c (heats & cools slowly — used as coolant).

4.1.2 Importance of Chemical Changes

  • Two principal advantages of chemical changes: production of energy and production of useful substances (respiration, photosynthesis, digestion).

4.2 Energy Changes in Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry = field of chemistry dealing with the interconversion of electrical energy and chemical energy.

4.2.2 Electrical Conductivity

Electrical conductivity = ability of a substance to transmit electricity. Charge carriers: electrons (negative) and ions (cations +, anions −).

a) Metallic conductivity (electronic conductivity): conduction through metals due to movement of free (delocalized) valence electrons ("sea of mobile electrons"). Silver is the best electrical conductor. Non-metals generally don't conduct (no free electrons), EXCEPT graphite (carbon atoms in hexagonal layers; electrons move freely through layers).

b) Electrolytic conductivity (ionic conductivity): conduction through electrolytes (molten or aqueous) due to movement of ions. Depends on type & concentration of ions. Cations move to cathode, anions to anode.

Difference between metallic & electrolytic conduction:

| Metallic | Electrolytic |

|---|---|

| Due to movement of electrons | Due to movement of ions |

| No chemical change in conductor | May cause chemical decomposition |

| No transfer of matter | Transfers matter (as ions) |

| Resistance from vibrating kernels | Resistance from concentration, ion attractions, viscosity |

| Conductivity decreases with ↑temperature | Conductivity increases with ↑temperature |

| Physical process | Accompanied by chemical changes at electrodes |

Electrolytes and Nonelectrolytes

  • Electrolyte: substance whose aqueous solution conducts electricity (contains mobile ions). All ionic compounds are electrolytes (also conduct when molten). Some polar covalent compounds (CH₃COOH) are electrolytes.
  • Nonelectrolyte: does not conduct in aqueous or molten state. Most polar covalent (sugar C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) and nonpolar covalent compounds. e.g. urea, methanol, ethanol, glucose, sucrose.
  • Strong electrolyte: dissociates completely (all ionic compounds, HCl, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄, NaOH, Ba(OH)₂). Bright light.
  • Weak electrolyte: dissociates partially (CH₃COOH, HF, HNO₂, NH₃, H₂O). Dim light.

4.3 Electrochemical Cells

Electrochemical cell = a system that incorporates a redox reaction to produce or use electrical energy. Contains two or more electrodes dipped in an electrolyte.

  • Electrodes = metal strips or graphite rods allowing electrons to enter/leave the electrolyte.
  • Active electrodes – take part in reactions (Zn, Mg).
  • Inert electrodes – only transfer electrons (Pt, graphite).

Two types: Galvanic (Voltaic) cells and Electrolytic cells.

4.3.1 Galvanic (Voltaic) Cells

A galvanic/voltaic cell = electrochemical cell where chemical energy of reactants is converted into electrical energy via a spontaneous redox reaction. Examples: dry cells (flashlights, radios), car batteries.

Classified into primary cells, secondary cells, fuel cells (fuel cells in grade 11).

Primary cells (battery cells): NOT rechargeable (reactions irreversible). e.g. Daniell cell (wet), Leclanche/zinc-carbon dry cell.

  • Wet cells = electrolyte in solution (Daniell cell). Dry cells = electrolyte as paste (Leclanche cell).

Daniell cell (wet primary cell): Zn strip in ZnSO₄ (anode compartment) + Cu strip in CuSO₄ (cathode compartment), linked by a salt bridge (tube of KCl-agar gel).

  • Anode: `Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻` (oxidation; Zn electrode loses mass, is negative = sacrificial electrode).
  • Cathode: `Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)` (reduction; Cu deposits, electrode gains mass, positive).
  • Overall: `Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`.
  • Purpose of salt bridge: maintains electrical neutrality between the two half-cell solutions.
  • Electrons flow from anode to cathode in external wire.

Leclanche (zinc-carbon) dry cell: Zn can = anode; graphite rod surrounded by paste of MnO₂, NH₄Cl, ZnCl₂, carbon black = cathode. Voltage ~1.5 V (decreases with use and in cold).

  • Anode: `Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻`
  • Cathode: `2NH₄⁺ + 2MnO₂ + 2e⁻ → Mn₂O₃ + H₂O + 2NH₃`
  • Overall: `Zn + 2NH₄⁺ + 2MnO₂ → Zn²⁺ + Mn₂O₃ + H₂O + 2NH₃`
  • NH₃ buildup prevented by forming complex: `Zn²⁺ + 2NH₃ + 2Cl⁻ → Zn(NH₃)₂Cl₂`.
  • Final overall: `Zn + 2NH₄Cl + 2MnO₂ → Zn(NH₃)₂Cl₂ + Mn₂O₃ + H₂O`.

Secondary (rechargeable) cells: rechargeable (reactions reversible by passing DC current). Example: lead storage battery (car battery).

  • Electrodes: one packed with spongy lead (anode), other with lead(IV) oxide PbO₂ (cathode); electrolyte = aqueous 35% H₂SO₄. Each cell delivers 2 V; a 12 V battery has 6 cells in series.
  • Discharge:
  • Anode: `Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻`
  • Cathode: `PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O`
  • Overall: `Pb + PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2SO₄²⁻ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O`
  • Recharge (reverse): `2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O → Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄`.

4.4 Electrolysis

Electrolysis = process by which electrical energy is used to drive an otherwise non-spontaneous reaction ("breakdown via electricity"). Cell in which electrolysis occurs = electrolytic cell.

Electrolytic cell components:

  • Anode: electrode connected to positive terminal; electrons leave the cell; oxidation occurs (anions discharge).
  • Cathode: electrode connected to negative terminal; electrons enter the cell; reduction occurs (cations discharge).
  • Cations → attracted to cathode (gain electrons, reduced). Anions → attracted to anode (lose electrons, oxidized).
  • Oxidation always at anode; reduction always at cathode. Electron flow always anode → cathode (in external circuit).

4.4.1 Electrolysis of Molten (Fused) Electrolytes

When ionic solids melt (fusion), they dissociate into free-moving ions. E.g. NaCl melts at 801°C.

Electrolysis of molten NaCl:

  • Cathode: `Na⁺(l) + e⁻ → Na(l)` (reduction)
  • Anode: `Cl⁻(l) → ½Cl₂(g) + e⁻` (oxidation)
  • Cell reaction: `Na⁺(l) + Cl⁻(l) → Na(l) + ½Cl₂(g)`
  • Used commercially to obtain sodium metal. Li, Mg, Ca obtained by electrolysis of their molten chlorides.

Half-reaction = reaction at one electrode. Cell reaction = sum of the half-reactions (whole cell).

In voltaic cells: anode is NEGATIVE, cathode is POSITIVE (polarity determined by the redox reaction).

In electrolytic cells: polarity is determined by the external power source.

Uses of electrolysis: purification of metals, production of metals, electroplating.

UNIT 5: METALS AND NONMETALS

Unit outcome: general properties of metals & nonmetals, extraction/chemical properties/uses of Al, Fe, Cu, and production of N, P, O, S, Cl.

5.1 Introduction — Natural Resources

  • Natural resources = resources existing without human action (sunlight, land, fossil fuels, water, air, crops).
  • Renewable: can be replenished by natural processes (water, air, biomass).
  • Non-renewable: fixed amount, cannot be replenished (fossil fuels, nuclear energy, metallic & non-metallic minerals).
  • Metallic minerals contain metallic elements; non-metallic minerals don't.
  • Minerals occur in mineral deposits. e.g. iron haematite Fe₂O₃, bauxite Al₂O₃·2H₂O, zinc blende ZnS, malachite Cu(OH)₂·CuCO₃. Nonmetallic minerals: diamond, gravel, mica.

5.2 General Properties of Metals & Production of Some Metals

5.2.1 Properties & Extraction of Metals

About 80% of known elements are metals.

A. Physical properties of metals: lustrous (shiny, can be polished), malleable (beaten into sheets), ductile (drawn into wires), hard with tensile strength, high density, sonorous (produce metallic sound). (Exceptions: Li, K, Na are soft, low density; Hg is liquid at room temp.)

B. Chemical properties of metals: positive valency, tendency to lose electrons (`M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻`), have 1–3 valence electrons, are oxidized & act as reducing agents, form basic oxides (some amphoteric), form true salt electrovalent chlorides, form ionic unstable hydrides, replace hydrogen from dilute acids (except Cu, Ag, Au).

C. Reactivity (activity) series of metals: arrangement of metals in decreasing order of activity. Most active at top, least at bottom. Hydrogen included for comparison.

  • Metals above hydrogen (K, Na, Ca, Mg) liberate H₂ from dilute acids; those below (Cu, Ag, Au) do not.
  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution. K can displace all.
  • Top metals = strong reducing agents; bottom = weak reducing agents (K strongest, Au weakest).
  • Typical order (descending): K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Sn > Pb > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au.

D. Natural occurrence & extraction:

  • Noble metals (Ag, Au, Bi, Cu, Pd, Pt) exist free/uncombined.
  • Active metals (alkali, alkaline earth) never uncombined; exist as carbonates, halides, oxides, phosphates, silicates, sulphides, sulphates.
  • Mineral = earth's crust constituent containing metal compounds (halite NaCl, sylvite KCl, magnesite MgCO₃, limestone CaCO₃).
  • Ore = mineral from which metal can be profitably extracted. Impurities = gangue.
  • Metallurgy = science & technology of extracting metals from ores and compounding alloys.

Three principal steps of extraction:

  1. 1. Preparation (concentration) of the ore — oil flotation, magnetic separation.
  2. 2. Production of the metal — roasting, calcination, reduction.
  3. 3. Purification of the metal — chemical reduction, electrolytic reduction.
  4. Most active metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg) extracted only by electrolysis (no economic reducing agent exists).

5.2.2 Alloys

Alloy = mixture of two or more metals, or metals and nonmetals, melted together and cooled to a solid (does not separate on solidifying).

  • Alloying increases hardness & strength, modifies colour & melting point, decreases electrical conductivity, increases corrosion resistance.
  • Amalgam = alloy of mercury + another metal.
  • Gun metal: Cu 87%, Sn 10%, Zn 3%.
  • Solder (fuse metal): 67% Sn, 33% Pb; melts at 183°C (lower than Sn's 232°C); used to join wires.
  • Gold alloys: gold hardened by alloying with Cu & Ag. Gold content in carats or mass %.
  • 24 carat = pure gold (100%). 22 carat = 22/24×100 = 91.67% gold. 18 carat = 75% gold.

5.2.3 Production of Aluminum, Iron and Copper

A. Aluminum (Al)

  • Most abundant metal in earth's crust; 3rd most abundant element (after O & Si); ~8% of crust; 2nd most important metal by consumption (after iron). Doesn't occur free.
  • Main mineral: bauxite (Al₂O₃·2H₂O). Others: orthoclase KAlSi₃O₈, cryolite Na₃AlF₆, corundum Al₂O₃, beryl, china clay.
  • Hall–Héroult (Hall) process for extraction:
  • 1. Purify bauxite: treat with NaOH → `SiO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SiO₃`; `Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → NaAlO₂ + H₂O`. Impurities (Fe₂O₃, TiO₂) filtered off.
  • 2. Precipitate Al(OH)₃ with acid: `AlO₂⁻ + H₃O⁺ → Al(OH)₃`.
  • 3. Heat: `2Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂O`.
  • 4. Mix Al₂O₃ with cryolite Na₃AlF₆ to lower melting point from 2045°C to 1000°C; electrolyze.
  • Anode: `3C + 6O²⁻ → 3CO₂ + 12e⁻` (graphite anode oxidizes, must be replaced)
  • Cathode: `4Al³⁺ + 12e⁻ → 4Al`
  • Overall: `2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂`
  • Physical properties: soft silvery-white light metal, density 2.7 g/cm³, melts 660°C, good conductor of heat & electricity.
  • Chemical properties: reactive but not as vigorous as Group IA/IIA.
  • With O₂: `4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃` (thin protective oxide film inhibits further reaction).
  • With N₂: `2Al + N₂ → 2AlN`.
  • With dilute acids: `2Al + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂`; `2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂`. (Does NOT react with HNO₃ due to protective oxide — used to store HNO₃.)
  • With Cl₂: `2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃`.
  • With NaOH: `2Al + 2NaOH + 6H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄ + 3H₂`.
  • Uses: lightweight corrosion-resistant alloys (duralumin = Al+Cu+Mg), aircraft/ships/automobiles, cookware, door/window frames, packaging, power lines. Thermite welding: `2Al + Fe₂O₃ → 2Fe + Al₂O₃` (~3000°C), welds rails; `Al + Cr₂O₃ → 2Cr + Al₂O₃`. Thermite = mixture of powdered Al + iron oxide.

B. Iron (Fe)

  • 2nd most abundant metal (after Al), 4th most abundant element; ~4.7% of crust. Never found free.
  • Ores: hematite Fe₂O₃, limonite Fe₂O₃·H₂O, magnetite Fe₃O₄, siderite FeCO₃, iron pyrites FeS₂ (fool's gold).
  • Extraction in a blast furnace. Raw materials: iron ore, coke, limestone, hot air.
  • `C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat`
  • `CO₂ + C → 2CO`
  • `Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂` (reduction of iron oxide by CO)
  • `CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂` (limestone = flux)
  • `CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃` (slag — glass-like calcium silicate)
  • Slag is lighter than molten metal → floats, easily separated. Used to make cement.
  • Pig iron (crude iron) directly from blast furnace: impure, brittle, 92%+ Fe, 3.5–4.5% C.
  • Cast iron = pig iron remelted with scrap iron cast into moulds.
  • Wrought iron = purest form of commercial iron (99.5% Fe), tough, malleable, ductile. Made by heating impure iron with hematite & limestone.
  • Steel making from pig iron (purification by oxidation): three techniques:
  • 1. Bessemer converter (MgCO₃, CaCO₃ lining) — 15 min, quality variable.
  • 2. Open-hearth furnace (MgO, CaO lining) — 8–10 hours, quality controllable.
  • 3. Basic oxygen process (CaO + O₂ blown in) — 20–25 min, fast, largely replaced open hearth.
  • Impurities oxidized: `C + O₂ → CO₂`; `S + O₂ → SO₂`; `12P + 10Fe₂O₃ → 3P₄O₁₀ + 20Fe`; `3Si + 2Fe₂O₃ → 3SiO₂ + 4Fe`.
  • Oxides react with CaO → slag: `P₄O₁₀ + 6CaO → 2Ca₃(PO₄)₂`; `SiO₂ + CaO → CaSiO₃`.
  • Tempering of steel = conditioning steel to desired hardness by heating & controlled cooling; removes cementite (FeC₃).
  • Physical properties: grey lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductor, high melting point (1580°C), density 7.87 g/cm³, ferromagnetic (can be permanently magnetized).
  • Chemical properties:
  • Rusts in air + moisture: `4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O`.
  • With dilute acids: `Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂`; `Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂`.
  • Two common oxidation states: ferrous Fe²⁺ (pale green) and ferric Fe³⁺ (yellowish brown). Compounds coloured.
  • `4Fe(OH)₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃`.
  • With Cl₂: `2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃`; with S: `Fe + S → FeS`.
  • Displaces less active metals: `Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu`.
  • Uses: structural metal (buildings, bridges), pig iron (boilers, radiators, railings, pipes), wrought iron (nails, sheets, gates), steel alloys.
  • Carbon steels: mild steel (up to 0.2% C) — screws, motors, railway lines; medium steel (0.3–0.6% C) — springs, chains; high carbon steel (0.6–1.5% C) — drill bits, knives, hammers; stainless steel (Fe 73, C 1, Cr 18, Ni 8) — knife blades, kitchen pots, food machinery.

C. Copper (Cu)

  • Found as native copper and in compounds: sulphides (chalcopyrite CuFeS₂, chalcocite Cu₂S, covellite CuS, bornite Cu₅FeS₄), oxides (cuprite Cu₂O, tenorite CuO), carbonate (malachite CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂).
  • Extracted principally from chalcopyrite:
  • 1. Concentrate by froth flotation (2% → 30% Cu).
  • 2. Roast with limited air: `2CuFeS₂ + 4O₂ → Cu₂S + 2FeO + 3SO₂`.
  • 3. Smelt with limestone & sand → slag (removes FeO as FeSiO₃, SiO₂ as CaSiO₃).
  • 4. Reduce Cu₂S: `Cu₂S + O₂ → 2Cu + SO₂` → blister copper (98.5–99.5% pure).
  • 5. Electrolytic refining:
  • Anode: impure copper; Cathode: pure copper; Electrolyte: CuSO₄ + dilute H₂SO₄.
  • Anode: `Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻`; Cathode: `Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu`.
  • Product: 99.99% pure copper.
  • Physical properties: soft, ductile, malleable, reddish-brown, density 8.96 g/cm³, 2nd to silver in electrical conductivity, melts 1086°C.
  • Chemical properties: less reactive (found native).
  • `2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO` (black powder).
  • Does NOT react with dilute HCl/H₂SO₄ (can't displace H). Reacts with oxidizing acids:
  • Dilute HNO₃: `3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O`
  • Concentrated HNO₃: `Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O`
  • Hot conc. H₂SO₄: `Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O`
  • Corrodes in moist air → green verdigris (basic copper carbonate): `2Cu + H₂O + O₂ + CO₂ → CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂`.
  • Two oxidation states: cuprous Cu⁺ (unstable, disproportionates: `2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu`), cupric Cu²⁺ (stable, blue coloured compounds; CuSO₄·5H₂O blue).
  • Uses: alloys (bronze = Cu+Sn for coins/medals/bells; brass = Cu+Zn for hardware/pipes), electrical wires & cables, copper compounds as pesticides.

5.3 Production of Some Important Nonmetals

5.3.1 General properties of nonmetals

Physical: solids/liquids/gases, non-lustrous, non-malleable, non-ductile, low density, low melting/boiling points, soft, non-sonorous, poor conductors.

Chemical: react with O₂ to form oxides; don't displace H from acids; form acidic or neutral oxides; combine with H to form stable hydrides; don't react with water; electronegative (gain electrons to form negative ions); oxidizing agents.

5.3.2 Production of N, P, O, S, Cl

A. Nitrogen (N)

  • ~80% by volume of atmosphere, as N₂ (diatomic, triple bond → very inert).
  • Compounds: NaNO₃ (Chile saltpeter), KNO₃ (saltpeter); in DNA & proteins.
  • Production: fractional distillation of liquid air (air compressed, cooled, condensed; N₂ distills off first).
  • Laboratory: `NH₄Cl + NaNO₂ → NaCl + N₂ + 2H₂O`.
  • Physical: colourless, odourless, tasteless; inert due to strong N≡N triple bond.
  • Chemical: at high temp reacts with reactive metals to form nitrides: `6Li + N₂ → 2Li₃N`; `3Ca + N₂ → Ca₃N₂`; `3Mg + N₂ → Mg₃N₂`. Combines with O₂: `N₂ + O₂ → 2NO`; `N₂ + 2O₂ → 2NO₂`. NO₂ (reddish brown) dimerizes to N₂O₄ (colourless). Also N₂O (laughing gas), N₂O₃, N₂O₅.
  • Haber process: `N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃`, using finely divided Fe catalyst, 200–300 atm, 400–600°C. Ammonia used for fertilizers and HNO₃.
  • Uses: food packaging (inert), semiconductors, liquid N₂ as refrigerant (preserve semen/blood); major use = ammonia production.

B. Phosphorus (P)

  • Exists only in combined state: rock phosphate Ca₃(PO₄)₂, fluoroapatite Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆F₂. In teeth, bones, DNA.
  • Allotropes = different forms of same element, same physical state, different physical properties (due to different atom arrangement). Two common allotropes: white & red phosphorus.
  • White phosphorus: very poisonous, white waxy, P₄ molecules, unstable; melts 44.1°C; spontaneously ignites in air → stored under water.
  • Red phosphorus: denser (2.16 g/cm³), much less reactive; P₄ linked into polymer; doesn't need storage under water; soluble in CS₂.
  • Production: white P from `2Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 6SiO₂ + 10C → 6CaSiO₃ + P₄ + 10CO` (electric furnace). Red P by heating white P in sunlight.
  • Chemical:
  • `P₄ + 3O₂ → P₄O₆`; `P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀`
  • `P₄O₆ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₃` (phosphorous acid); `P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄` (phosphoric acid)
  • `P₄ + 6Cl₂ → 4PCl₃`; `P₄ + 10Cl₂ → 4PCl₅`
  • Uses: red P for matches; white P for phosphoric acid & phosphorus compounds; fireworks, smoke bombs, rat poison, tracer bullets; fertilizers.

C. Oxygen (O)

  • Most abundant element on earth; ~46.6% by weight of crust; ~20% by volume of air.
  • Production: fractional distillation of liquid air.
  • Allotropes: O₂ (dioxygen) and O₃ (ozone, triatomic). Ozone produced in stratosphere by UV: `3O₂ →UV 2O₃` (absorbs harmful UV).
  • Physical: colourless, odourless, tasteless; liquid at −182.96°C (slightly bluish); density 1.429 g/L (denser than air 1.29 g/L).
  • Chemical: relatively reactive; combines with most elements to form oxides.
  • Metals → basic oxides: `2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO`; `2Ca + O₂ → 2CaO`.
  • Non-metals → acidic oxides: `S₈ + 8O₂ → 8SO₂`; `P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀`.
  • Supports combustion: `C + O₂ → CO₂`; `CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O`.

D. Sulphur (S)

  • Found native & in compounds: galena PbS, pyrites FeS₂ (fool's gold), cinnabar HgS, sphalerite ZnS, gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O, barite BaSO₄, H₂S in natural gas & crude oil.
  • Extraction: Frasch process — superheated water (~170°C) + compressed air forced down concentric pipes; froth of molten sulphur, air & water brought to surface.
  • Nearly half the S needed comes as waste product of other industries (H₂S from natural gas, SO₂ from roasting sulphide ores) — reduces pollution & acid rain.
  • Allotropes: rhombic (α, most stable, S₈ molecules, yellow octahedral, m.p. 385.8 K) and monoclinic (β, stable only above 95.3°C).
  • Physical: tasteless, odourless, brittle, pale yellow, poor conductor, insoluble in water.
  • Chemical: relatively unreactive at room temp; reacts when heated.
  • With metals → sulphides: `8Fe + S₈ → 8FeS`.
  • Burns in O₂: `S₈ + 8O₂ → 8SO₂`.
  • Contact process for H₂SO₄:
  • 1. `S₈ + 8O₂ → 8SO₂`
  • 2. `2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃` (catalyst, high temp)
  • 3. `SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇` (oleum)
  • 4. `H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄` (98% commercial).

E. Chlorine (Cl)

  • Group VIIA (halogen); most abundant halogen. Found only in compounds (chlorides of Na, K, Ca, Mg). Chief source: NaCl (seawater or rock salt).
  • Production: electrolysis of concentrated NaCl solution (brine):
  • Anode: `2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻`
  • Cathode: `2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻`
  • Cell: `2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂`
  • NaOH & Cl₂ kept apart (they react to form NaOCl: `NaOH + Cl₂ → NaOCl + HCl`).
  • Physical: greenish-yellow gas at room temp; melts −102°C, boils −34°C; fairly soluble in water; extremely poisonous.
  • Chemical: highly reactive; powerful oxidizing agent; reacts with almost all elements except noble gases, C, N.
  • `2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃`; `2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃`.
  • `H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl` (with charcoal catalyst).
  • Displaces less reactive halogens: `Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂`; `Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂`.
  • With water (disproportionation — Cl both oxidized & reduced): `Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl`.
  • Uses: bleaching agent & disinfectant (kills germs), removes colours from wood pulp, paper, cotton.

UNIT 6: HYDROCARBONS AND THEIR NATURAL SOURCES

Unit outcome: history of organic chemistry, classification of organic compounds, general formulas & naming of alkanes/alkenes/alkynes, isomerism, properties & preparation, benzene, and natural sources (petroleum, natural gas, coal).

6.1 Introduction

Organic chemistry = study of carbon compounds (except oxides of carbon, carbonates, hydrogen carbonates, cyanides, cyanates).

Natural products = organic compounds synthesized by living organisms. Some plant chemicals are drugs of abuse (nicotine from tobacco, cathinone from khat, morphine from opium, cocaine from coca leaf). Doping = misuse of substances to enhance sports performance.

6.1.1 History of Organic Chemistry

  • Vitalism theory: believed organic compounds only came from living organisms via a "vital force", and only living things could synthesize them.
  • Friedrich Wöhler (1828) disproved vitalism by synthesizing urea from ammonium cyanate (an inorganic compound) by heating/evaporating. This began organic chemistry as a science.
  • Today over 50 million organic compounds known; all contain carbon.
  • Carbon is special due to catenation — the ability of atoms of the same element to join into chains and rings (carbon forms strong C–C bonds and bonds to H, N, O, S).

6.1.2 Structural Representations of Organic Molecules

  • Lewis structure: shared electron pairs shown as dots or lines, lone pairs as dots.
  • Complete structural formula: two-electron covalent bond shown as a single dash (— single bond, = double, ≡ triple).
  • Condensed structural formula: omits some/all dashes. e.g. CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ = CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃.
  • Bond-line (skeletal) structure: C and H not shown, understood from valency (C forms 4 bonds); zig-zag lines; only heteroatoms shown. Terminals = CH₃, line junctions = carbon with appropriate H.
  • Polygon formula: cyclic compounds drawn as polygons; corners = carbon, sides = C–C bonds. e.g. cyclopentane, cyclohexane.

6.1.3 Classes of Organic Compounds & Functional Groups

  • Functional group = a group of atoms and/or bonds within a molecule that has a characteristic chemical behaviour. Members of a family have similar chemical behaviour.
  • Table: Alkane (−ane, e.g. ethane), Alkene (C=C, −ene, e.g. ethene), Alkyne (C≡C, −yne), Aromatic (benzene ring).
  • Alkanes have NO functional group (only C–C and C–H single bonds).

6.2 Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂)

Hydrocarbon = compound containing only carbon & hydrogen.

  • Aliphatic ("fat-like"): alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, derivatives.
  • Aromatic: contain benzene ring, low H:C ratio (e.g. C₆H₆, H:C = 1).
  • Saturated = only single bonds, max H per carbon (alkanes, e.g. CH₄).
  • Unsaturated = multiple bonds, fewer H per carbon (alkenes, alkynes, aromatic).

Alkane = saturated hydrocarbon; also called aliphatic ("fat"). Can be unbranched (normal) or branched.

General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

First 10 unbranched alkanes: Methane CH₄(1), Ethane C₂H₆(2), Propane C₃H₈(3), Butane C₄H₁₀(4), Pentane C₅H₁₂(5), Hexane C₆H₁₄(6), Heptane C₇H₁₆(7), Octane C₈H₁₈(8), Nonane C₉H₂₀(9), Decane C₁₀H₂₂(10). (then undecane 11, dodecane 12, tridecane 13... eicosane 20).

6.2.1 Alkane Homologous Series

Homologous series = a series of compounds where each member differs from the next by a constant unit (−CH₂−).

Characteristics: constant unit between consecutive members; general formula; trends in physical properties; same chemical reactivity. Members called homologues. Consecutive members differ by molecular mass 14 (CH₂ = 12+2).

6.2.2 Physical Properties of Alkanes

  • C₁–C₄: gases at room temp. C₅–C₁₇: liquids. C₁₈+: solids.
  • Nonpolar (C & H have similar electronegativity); held by dispersion (London) forces; insoluble in water (like dissolves like).
  • Boiling/melting point increases with molecular weight/mass. Branching decreases boiling point.
  • Alkanes are the least dense of all organic compounds; all less dense than water (petroleum floats on water).
  • As C number increases: higher m.p., b.p., density.

6.2.3 Nomenclature of Alkanes

Common names: prefixes n- (normal/continuous chain), iso- (continuous chain except one next to last carbon), neo- (central carbon bonded to 4 other carbons). e.g. n-pentane, isobutane, neopentane.

IUPAC nomenclature (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry):

  • Parent name = longest continuous carbon chain.
  • Suffix −ane for alkanes. Stems: meth-1, eth-2, prop-3, but-4, pent-5, hex-6, hept-7, oct-8, non-9, dec-10.
  • Alkyl group = alkane minus one H; names end in −yl (methyl, ethyl, propyl, butyl). Symbol R = any alkyl group.

Rules for branched-chain alkanes:

  1. 1. Find the longest continuous chain → parent name.
  2. 2. Number the chain from the end nearer the first substituent (to give lowest locants).
  3. 3. Number designates location of substituent; number separated from word by hyphen (e.g. 2-methylhexane).
  4. 4. With multiple substituents, give each a number; list them alphabetically (disregard di/tri/tetra prefixes and italic prefixes like tert-). Add the locants to choose correct numbering (smallest sum).
  5. 5. Same carbon has two substituents → use the number twice.
  6. 6. Identical substituents → use prefixes di-, tri-, tetra-.
  7. 7. Two equal-length chains → choose the one with more substituents.
  8. 8. Branching at equal distance → choose lower number at first point of difference.
  9. 9. Complex substituent → name as though it were a compound; number from carbon attached to parent chain.

Carbon classification: primary (1°, bonded to 1 carbon), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°), quaternary (4°). H atoms classified by the carbon they're attached to.

6.2.4 Isomerism in Alkanes

Isomers = compounds with same numbers & kinds of atoms (same molecular formula) but different arrangement.

Structural isomers = different connectivity/structure (different physical properties: different m.p., b.p., density).

  • C₄H₁₀: 2 isomers (butane, isobutane). C₅H₁₂: 3 isomers. C₆H₁₄: 5. C₇H₁₆: 9.
  • Formula for number of isomers of alkanes C₄–C₇: 2ⁿ⁻⁴ + 1.
  • Methane, ethane, propane have only one structure (no isomers).

6.2.5 Preparation of Alkanes

  1. 1. Hydrogenation of alkenes (Pt catalyst): `Alkene + H₂ →Pt Alkane` (e.g. ethene + H₂ → ethane).
  2. 2. Würz synthesis: `2R-X + 2Na → R-R + 2NaX` (halogenated alkane + Na). e.g. `2CH₃Cl + 2Na → CH₃CH₃ + 2NaCl`.
  3. 3. Decarboxylation: heating sodium salt of carboxylic acid with soda lime (NaOH + CaO): `RCOONa + NaOH → R-H + Na₂CO₃`. e.g. `CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃`.
  4. Methane is main constituent (~90%) of natural gas; also from decay in marshes; biogas from cow dung/plant waste.

6.2.6 Chemical Properties of Alkanes

Alkanes are called paraffins ("little affinity") — chemically inert to most reagents. But react with O₂, halogens, and few others:

  1. 1. Combustion: `CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → nCO₂ + (n+1)H₂O + heat`. e.g. `CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + 890 kJ/mol`; `2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O`.
  2. 2. Substitution (halogenation): replace one atom/group by another. Photochemical reaction with Cl₂/Br₂ in heat/light. Radical mechanism (chain initiating → propagating → terminating). `CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr`.
  3. 3. Elimination: removal of small molecules, forms multiple bond. e.g. ethane heated 600–800°C → ethene + H₂.

6.2.7 Cycloalkanes (Alicyclic Hydrocarbons)

  • Alkanes in which carbons form a ring. General formula CₙH₂ₙ (n ≥ 3) — two fewer H than alkanes. Same properties as alkanes.
  • Examples: cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclopentane, cyclohexane. Simplest = cyclopropane.

6.2.8 Uses of Alkanes

Primarily used as fuels; also as solvents and raw materials for alkenes, alcohols, soaps, detergents, plastics.

6.3 Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes, Alkynes, and Aromatic Hydrocarbons

6.3.1 Alkenes (Olefins)

Alkene = unsaturated hydrocarbon with at least one C=C double bond; ending −ene; also called olefins. General formula CₙH₂ₙ (n = 2, 3, 4...).

  • Ethene (C₂H₄) is simplest; used for ethanol, ethylene oxide, ethanal, polyethylene. Propene → polypropylene. Ethene is a plant hormone (fruit ripening).
  • Homologous series: ethene, propene, 1-butene, 1-pentene, 1-hexene, 1-heptene, 1-octene, 1-nonene, 1-decene.

Nomenclature of alkenes:

  • Parent = longest chain containing the double bond; change −ane → −ene.
  • Number chain to include both carbons of the double bond, from the end nearer the double bond. Use locant = number of first atom of double bond.
  • e.g. 1-butene, 2-hexene, 2-methyl-2-butene, 2,5-dimethyl-2-hexene.
  • Common names: ethene=ethylene, propene=propylene, 2-methylpropene=isobutylene.
  • Alkenyl groups: vinyl (CH₂=CH−), allyl (CH₂=CHCH₂−).

Physical properties of alkenes: similar to alkanes (nonpolar); boiling/melting/density increase with molecular weight; insoluble in water, soluble in CCl₄; low members gases.

Isomerism in alkenes:

  1. 1. Chain isomerism: different arrangement of carbon chain (1-pentene vs 2-methyl-1-butene).
  2. 2. Position isomerism: different position of double bond (1-pentene vs 2-pentene).
  3. 3. Geometrical (cis-trans) isomerism: different spatial arrangement about the double bond (no free rotation). cis = similar groups same side; trans = opposite sides. e.g. cis-2-butene, trans-2-butene.
  4. An alkene CANNOT have cis-trans isomers if two identical groups are attached to one of the double-bond carbons (e.g. 1-pentene).

Preparation of alkenes:

  1. 1. Dehydration of alcohols (conc. H₂SO₄ or Al₂O₃, heat): remove water. `Ethanol →H₂SO₄ Ethene + H₂O`.
  2. 2. Dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides (KOH): remove H and halogen. `Ethyl bromide + KOH → Ethene + KBr + H₂O`.

Chemical properties of alkenes (more reactive than alkanes; the weak π-bond reacts):

  1. 1. Combustion: `CₙH₂ₙ + (3n/2)O₂ → nCO₂ + nH₂O + heat`. e.g. `C₄H₈ + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 4H₂O`.
  2. 2. Addition reactions (at the double bond):
  3. Halogenation (X₂ = Cl₂, Br₂): `RCH=CH₂ + X₂ → RCHX–CH₂X` (dihaloalkane). Bromine water (reddish-brown) is decolorized — test for unsaturation.
  4. Hydrogenation (Ni/Pt): `Alkene + H₂ → Alkane`. e.g. propene + H₂ → propane.
  5. Hydrohalogenation (HX): `Alkene + HX → alkyl halide`, follows Markovnikov's rule (H adds to the carbon with more H, halogen to the carbon with fewer H). e.g. propene + HBr → 2-bromopropane.
  6. Hydration (water, acid catalyst): `Alkene + H₂O → alcohol` (follows Markovnikov). Ethene → ethanol; propene → 2-propanol.
  7. Oxidation (cold alkaline KMnO₄ = Baeyer's reagent): forms diols (1,2-diol). Purple colour fades + brown precipitate = test for unsaturation.
  8. Polymerization (self-addition): monomers → polymer. `nCH₂=CH₂ → (CH₂–CH₂)ₙ` polyethene.

Uses of ethene: ethanol, ethylene oxide, ethanal, polymers (polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene, teflon), 1,2-ethanediol (antifreeze), artificial ripening of fruits.

Cycloalkenes: unsaturated cyclic hydrocarbons; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (n≥3); isomeric with alkynes. Named by prefixing "cyclo". Simplest = cyclopropene. e.g. cyclopropene, cyclobutene, cyclopentene, cyclohexene.

6.3.2 Alkynes

Alkyne = unsaturated hydrocarbon with at least one C≡C triple bond; ending −yne. General formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (n ≥ 2). Simplest = ethyne (acetylene). Series also called acetylene series.

  • First members: ethyne C₂H₂, propyne C₃H₄, 1-butyne C₄H₆, 1-pentyne, 1-hexyne, 1-heptyne, 1-octyne, 1-nonyne, 1-decyne.

Nomenclature of alkynes: replace −ane → −yne; number chain to give the triple bond the lowest locants. When both double & triple present, choose lowest set of locants, giving preference to lowest numbers for double bonds. e.g. ethyne (acetylene), 2-pentyne, 1-pentene-4-yne. Common names: methylacetylene, ethylacetylene.

Isomerism in alkynes: show chain and position isomerism but NOT geometrical (cis-trans) isomerism.

  • 1-butyne & 2-butyne = position isomers; 3-methyl-1-butyne & 1-pentyne = chain isomers.

Preparation of alkynes:

  1. 1. Dehydrohalogenation of vicinal dihalides (NaOH/KOH/NaNH₂): 1,2-dibromoethane → ethyne.
  2. 2. Alkylation of sodium acetylide (carbide) with primary alkyl halide: sodium carbide + ethyl bromide → 1-butyne.
  3. 3. Reaction of calcium carbide with water: `CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂` (laboratory prep of acetylene).

Chemical properties of alkynes:

  1. 1. Combustion: `CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ + (3n−1)/2 O₂ → nCO₂ + (n−1)H₂O + heat`. Smoky luminous flame (high carbon content). e.g. `C₃H₄ + 4O₂ → 3CO₂ + 2H₂O`.
  2. 2. Addition reactions:
  3. Hydrogenation (Ni/Pd): alkyne → alkane (via alkene). Partial hydrogenation with Lindlar's catalyst gives cis-alkene.
  4. Halogenation: with 1 molecule X₂ → dihaloalkene; with 2 molecules → tetrahaloalkane.
  5. Hydrohalogenation (HX): → monohaloalkene, then dihaloalkane (follows Markovnikov).
  6. Trimerization of acetylene: 3 C₂H₂ at 600–700°C → benzene.
  7. Alkynes decolorize bromine water and alkaline KMnO₄.

Properties & uses of acetylene (ethyne): colourless sweet-smelling gas, insoluble in water, stored in acetone in steel cylinders. Burns with O₂ → ~3000°C flame (oxy-acetylene torch for cutting/welding metals). Used to make acrylonitrile, vinyl chloride (→ PVC), 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane (solvent).

6.4 Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Benzene

Aromatic hydrocarbons contain a benzene ring or related structures. "Aromatic" originally meant pleasant smell. Obtained from petroleum and coal tar. Examples: benzene, toluene (methylbenzene), naphthalene.

6.4.1 Benzene

  • Simplest aromatic hydrocarbon; molecular formula C₆H₆.
  • First structure proposed by August Kekulé (1872): six-membered ring with alternating single & double bonds (two Kekulé structures).
  • Today benzene represented as a resonance hybrid of two equivalent Kekulé structures. All C–C bonds equal in length & nature (intermediate between single & double).
  • Benzene has high unsaturation yet does NOT undergo alkene-type addition/oxidation reactions — it's more stable than alkenes.

6.4.2 Nomenclature of Substituted Benzenes

  • Monosubstituted: named with −benzene parent. e.g. methylbenzene (toluene), ethylbenzene, bromobenzene, nitrobenzene, hydroxybenzene (phenol), aminobenzene (aniline).
  • Disubstituted: three isomers — ortho (o, 1,2), meta (m, 1,3), para (p, 1,4). e.g. o-bromotoluene, m-chloroaniline, p-xylene (1,4-dimethylbenzene).

6.4.3 Physical Properties of Benzene

Flammable, colourless, volatile liquid with characteristic smell; less dense than water; nonpolar; insoluble in water; soluble in nonpolar solvents (ether, CCl₄). Carcinogenic (causes leukemia).

6.4.4 Chemical Properties of Benzene

Does NOT decolorize Br₂ in CCl₄ or react with cold KMnO₄ (more stable than alkenes). Reactions:

  1. 1. Combustion: `2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O + heat` (smoky luminous flame).
  2. 2. Substitution (characteristic reaction of aromatic compounds) — replaces H on the ring:
  3. Halogenation (FeBr₃/FeCl₃/AlCl₃): `C₆H₆ + Br₂ → C₆H₅Br + HBr` (bromobenzene).
  4. Nitration (conc. HNO₃ + H₂SO₄, 50°C): `C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O` (nitrobenzene).
  5. Sulphonation (conc. H₂SO₄): `C₆H₆ + H₂SO₄ → C₆H₅SO₃H + H₂O` (benzenesulphonic acid).
  6. 3. Addition only under special conditions: `C₆H₆ + 3H₂ →Ni 200°C C₆H₁₂` (cyclohexane).

6.5 Natural Sources of Hydrocarbons

Major sources of alkanes: natural gas, petroleum (crude oil), and coal.

A. Natural Gas

  • Mixture chiefly of methane (>90%) plus ethane, propane, butane; may contain CO₂, N₂, O₂, H₂S.
  • Found with petroleum or alone, underground. Widely used as fuel; very high heat of combustion.

B. Petroleum (Crude Oil)

  • "Petra" (rock) + "oleum" (oil) = rock oil. Complex mixture of fossil hydrocarbons (mainly alkanes, cycloalkanes, aromatic hydrocarbons; plus N, S, O compounds).
  • Refining of crude oil begins with fractional distillation into fractions by boiling point.
  • Fractions & uses:
  • Natural gases (C₁–C₄, bp <20°C) — bottled gas fuel.
  • Petroleum ether (C₅–C₇, 20–60°C) — dry cleaning solvent.
  • Gasoline/petrol (C₅–C₁₀, 40–200°C) — internal combustion engine fuel.
  • Kerosene (C₁₂–C₁₈, 175–275°C) — jet fuel, household fuel.
  • Fuel oil/diesel (C₁₅–C₁₈, 250–400°C) — furnace & diesel fuel.
  • Lubricating oil & petroleum jelly (>C₁₉) — lubrication, grease.
  • Paraffin wax & residue (>C₂₀) — candles, polishes; bitumen for roofs/roads.
  • Higher fractions = more coloured, more viscous, less inflammable.
  • Octane number measures antiknock property. 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (isooctane) = 100; heptane = 0. Straight-chain hydrocarbons cause more engine knock; branched burn smoother.
  • Cracking = decomposition of large hydrocarbons into smaller ones by heat (thermal) or with catalysts (catalytic); maximizes petrol output. e.g. `C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₈H₁₆`.
  • Reforming = converting C₆–C₈ alkanes to aromatic compounds (benzene, toluene) with higher octane numbers.

C. Coal

  • Formed from plant matter over millions of years; not pure carbon; important source of aromatic hydrocarbons.
  • Destructive distillation of coal (heating in absence of air): gives volatile products + coke (solid, relatively pure carbon, used as blast-furnace fuel). Volatile products cool → coal tar (source of aromatic hydrocarbons) and coal gas.
  • Coke also used to make water gas (H₂ + CO) and producer gas (N₂ + CO).

📖 GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS (quick definitions for revision)

Anhydride – an oxide that forms an acid (acidic oxide) or base (basic oxide) with water.

Allotrope – different form of the same element in the same physical state with different properties (P white/red, O₂/O₃, S rhombic/monoclinic).

Avogadro's number – 6.022 × 10²³, the number of entities in one mole.

Calorimeter – device to measure heat of a reaction.

Catenation – ability of atoms of an element (esp. carbon) to form chains/rings.

Coagulation – aggregation & separation of a colloid's dispersed phase.

Concentration – relative quantity of solute in a solution.

Cracking – breaking large hydrocarbons into smaller ones.

Electrolysis – using electricity to drive a nonspontaneous reaction.

Electrolyte – substance conducting electricity in molten/aqueous state.

Endothermic – absorbs heat (ΔH > 0).

Exothermic – releases heat (ΔH < 0).

Functional group – atom/group giving a molecule its characteristic chemistry.

Gangue – impurities in an ore.

Henry's law – gas solubility ∝ its partial pressure.

Heterogeneous – non-uniform composition, distinct parts.

Homogeneous – uniform composition throughout.

Homologous series – series differing by −CH₂− with common formula & similar reactions.

Hydrocarbon – compound of only C and H.

Hygroscopic – absorbs water from air but stays solid.

Limiting reactant – reactant completely consumed first; limits product.

Malleable / Ductile – can be beaten into sheets / drawn into wires.

Metallurgy – science of extracting metals from ores.

Micelle – aggregate of molecules with hydrophobic + hydrophilic ends (soap).

Miscible/Immiscible – liquids that mix / do not mix.

Redox – reaction involving electron transfer.

Saturated/Unsaturated – max solute dissolved / can dissolve more; single bonds / multiple bonds.

Spectator ion – ion present on both sides, not taking part.

Stoichiometry – quantitative study of reactants & products.

Vitalism – disproved theory that only living things make organic compounds.
