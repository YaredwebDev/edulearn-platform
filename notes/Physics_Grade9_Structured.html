🔑 TOP EXAM-TRAP POINTS TO ALWAYS REMEMBER

  1. Distance is scalar (path length); displacement is a vector (change in position). Displacement can be zero even after moving.
  2. Average speed ≠ magnitude of average velocity. Speed uses total distance; velocity uses displacement.
  3. Slope of s–t graph = velocity; slope of v–t graph = acceleration; area under v–t graph = distance.
  4. Uniform motion: a = 0; average velocity = instantaneous velocity.
  5. Mass is constant; weight W = mg changes with g (1/6 on the Moon).
  6. F = ma, not weight. Use g = 9.8 m/s² for weight problems; problems may state g = 10 m/s².
  7. Action-reaction forces act on different objects and never cancel each other.
  8. Static friction > kinetic friction.
  9. Work requires force in the direction of displacement — holding something still or carrying horizontally at constant velocity does no work.
  10. E_k = ½mv² — doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy (v²).
  11. Machines never 100% efficient (friction); IMA > AMA.
  12. M.A > 1 = force multiplier; V.R > 1 = force multiplier (effort moves farther). V.R < 1 = speed multiplier.
  13. Pendulum period depends only on length and g (NOT mass). Spring period depends on mass and k.
  14. Sound is a longitudinal wave and needs a medium — it cannot travel through a vacuum.
  15. Sound fastest in solids, slowest in gases. Speed in air at 0 °C = 331 m/s.
  16. Echo = reflection of sound; used in SONAR.
  17. Loudness ← amplitude (dB); pitch ← frequency; timbre ← quality.
  18. T_K = T_C + 273.15 (add 273, not multiply). Size of 1 K = size of 1 °C.
  19. Absolute zero = 0 K = −273.15 °C — lowest possible temperature.
  20. Linear expansion ΔL = αL₀ΔT — coefficient α depends on the material.
  21. Gases expand more than liquids; liquids more than solids.

1.1 Definition and Nature of Physics

  • The word physics comes from the Greek word phusis, meaning nature.
  • Physics is a branch of natural science aimed at describing the fundamental aspects of our universe — what things are in it, what properties of those things are noticeable, and what processes those things or their properties undergo. In simpler terms, physics attempts to describe the basic mechanisms that make our universe behave the way it does.
  • A person who studies physics is called a physicist.

Why study physics?

  • Understand the working principles of cars, airplanes, space-rockets, refrigerators, radios, televisions, and many daily utensils and tools.
  • Explains physical phenomena (e.g., why walking on a smooth plane is difficult, why an electric fan rotates).
  • Helps discover unknown parts of nature and become familiar with the modern world.
  • Helps understand concepts in other subjects (Biology, Chemistry, Geology, Astronomy, etc.).
  • Provides career opportunities in: transportation, aviation & space science, medicine, forensic & military science, meteorology & metrology.
✍️ Must-know: Physics = branch of natural science that describes the basic mechanisms that make the universe behave the way it does. Physicist = one who studies physics.

1.3 Related Fields to Physics

Physics is the foundation of many important scientific disciplines:

  • Chemistry: deals with interactions of atoms/molecules, but is rooted in atomic and molecular physics.
  • Engineering: most branches apply physics (architecture uses physics for structural stability, acoustics, heating, lighting, cooling).
  • Geology: relies on physics for radioactive dating, earthquake analysis, and heat transfer across Earth's surface.
  • Biophysics: applies the principles and methods of physics to study biological phenomena.
  • Geophysics: applies physics to the study of the Earth.
  • Medical Physics: diagnostics and medical therapy (X-rays, MRI, ultrasonic blood flow measurements).
✍️ Must-know: Physics is the foundation of Chemistry, Engineering, Geology, Biophysics, Geophysics, and Medical Physics.

1.4 Historical Issues and Contributors

  • Physics developed from the Renaissance to the end of the 19th century is called classical physics. Revolutionary discoveries starting in the early 20th century transformed physics into modern physics; many classical laws were modified.

Prominent physicists:

  • Isaac Newton: laid the foundations for classical mechanics; formulated the three laws of motion and the principle of universal gravitation; contributed to the Scientific Revolution.
  • Michael Faraday: contributed to electromagnetism; in 1821 produced mechanical motion using a magnet and electric current; ten years later converted magnetic force into electrical force, inventing the world's first electric generator ("changed the world with a magnet").
  • James Prescott Joule: studied the nature of heat and its relationship to mechanical work; led to the law of conservation of energy; helped lay the foundation for the first law of thermodynamics.
  • Marie Curie: pioneering research in nuclear physics, particularly radioactivity; "mother of modern nuclear physics"; discovered polonium and radium.
  • Albert Einstein: developed the theory of relativity; contributed to quantum mechanics; one of the greatest physicists of all time.

2.1 Scales, Standards, Units (Prefixes)

Scales

  • Scale = a set of numbers, amounts, etc., used to measure or compare the level of something.
  • A scale contains the markings that show a certain amount of what is being measured. The more marks a measuring device has, the more precise the measurement can be.
  • Reading a scale: each small mark represents a fraction (e.g., a space between numbered values divided into 10 gives one-tenth (0.1) per mark; you can estimate one more decimal place).
Note: Example 2.1: The red line goes just past 3.3 but not to 3.4, about halfway → read as 3.35.
  • Digital instruments indicate the measured value in digital format (number + unit); easier to read and more accurate than analog instruments.

Standards

  • A standard is a conventional unit used to measure a physical quantity scientifically. A chosen standard must:
  • be readily accessible and possess a property that can be measured reliably;
  • yield the same result when measured by different people in different places.
  • Non-standard (traditional) measurements create inaccuracy and bias (e.g., measuring cotton by palm size, distance by footsteps).
  • In 2019 an International Committee revised the standards — the SI system (an adaptation of the metric system).

Definitions of SI base units:

  • Meter (m): the distance traveled by light in vacuum during a time of 1/299792458 s.
  • Second (s): 9 192 631 770 times the period of vibration of radiation from the caesium-133 atom.
  • Kilogram (kg): defined by the fixed numerical value of the Planck constant h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s.

Scientific Notation

  • A way of writing measured values too large or too small: d × 10ⁿ, where d is a decimal number between 0 and 10 (usually 1 ≤ d < 10), and n is an integer exponent.
  • If n > 0, move the decimal n places right (e.g., 3.24×10³ = 3240). If n < 0, move left (3.24×10⁻³ = 0.00324).

Significant Figures

  • Each non-zero digit is significant. Zeros count only if they are between two non-zero digits or at the end of the decimal part.
  • 2000 has 1 sig fig; 2000.0 has 5 sig figs.
  • Add/subtract: result has the same number of decimal places as the term with the fewest decimal places. (9.65 + 8.4 = 18.1, not 18.05.)
  • Multiply/divide: result has the same number of significant figures as the quantity with the fewest sig figs. (8.65 × 2.035 = 17.8, not 17.75845.)
  • Rounding: remove sig figs from the right, rounding as you go (6.827 → 6.83 to 3 sig figs).

2.2 Measurement and Safety

  • Measurement = the process of comparing an unknown quantity with a known fixed unit of the same kind.
  • Three key elements of measurement: (1) the physical quantity to be measured, (2) the necessary measuring tools, (3) units of measurement (standard units).
  • Every physical quantity is represented by a numerical value and a unit (e.g., length of table = 3 meters; 3 = magnitude, meter = unit).

Measuring length: SI unit is meter (m); non-SI units mm, cm, km. Length = fundamental quantity describing the distance between two points.

Measuring mass: Mass = the amount of matter contained in a body. SI unit kilogram (kg); measured with a beam balance. Non-SI units: g, mg, tonne.

Measuring time: Time = the duration between the beginning and end of an event. SI unit second (s). Non-SI units: minute, hour, day, week, month, year. A traditional sundial shows time by the shadow of an upright object; not accurate/standard.

Laboratory Safety Rules (Physics Lab)

  1. Always wear a lab safety goggle.
  2. Avoid baggy clothing, bulky jewelry, dangling bracelets, open-toed shoes or sandals.
  3. NEVER work alone in the laboratory.
  4. Only books and notebooks needed for the experiment should be in the lab.
  5. Read about the experiment before entering the lab.
  6. Do not eat, drink, apply cosmetics, or chew gum in the laboratory.
  7. NEVER taste chemicals; do not touch.
  8. Report all accidents to the teacher immediately, no matter how minor.
  9. Exercise caution when working with electrical equipment.
  10. Perform only those experiments authorized by the teacher.
  11. Wash hands thoroughly after any laboratory activity.

3.1 Position, Distance and Displacement

  • Motion: a continuous change in the position of an object relative to the position of a fixed object called the reference frame.
  • Frame of reference: a set of coordinates used to determine the positions of objects.
  • A body is at rest if its position in a reference frame does not change with time; in motion if its position changes. Rest and motion are relative (a body at rest in one frame may be in motion in another).
  • Position: a measurement of a location with respect to some reference point (usually the origin). In one-dimensional motion we use the x-axis; positions can be negative or positive.

Distance:

  • The total path length traveled by a body. A scalar quantity (no direction). SI unit meter (m). Symbol s. Always positive (or zero).

Displacement:

  • The change in an object's position in a certain direction. A vector quantity.
  • ⃗S = ⃗X_f − ⃗X_i (final position minus initial position).
  • Independent of the path taken; if you return to your start, displacement = 0.

3.2 Average Speed and Instantaneous Speed

  • Speed: the rate at which an object changes its location; the distance covered per unit time. A scalar quantity. SI unit m/s. Symbol v.
  • speed = distance / time → v = s/t.
  • Average speed: total distance traveled divided by the total time taken.
  • v_av = s_total / t_total
  • Instantaneous speed: the speed at a particular instant; the speedometer of a car reads instantaneous speed.
  • v_ins = Δs/Δt as Δt → 0
Note: Example 3.2: A car covers 80 km in 1 hr 30 min (1.5 hr). v_av = 80/1.5 = 53.33 km/hr = 14.81 m/s.
Note: Example 3.3: Distance in 1.5 hr at 5 m/s: s = 5400 s × 5 m/s = 27000 m.

3.3 Average Velocity and Instantaneous Velocity

  • Velocity: a physical quantity describing how fast a body moves and the direction in which it moves. A vector quantity. Symbol ⃗v. SI unit m/s. Velocity = rate of change of displacement.
  • Average velocity: the total displacement divided by the total time taken.
  • ⃗v_av = (⃗X_f − ⃗X_i)/(t_f − t_i) = Δ⃗X/Δt = ⃗S/Δt
  • Average velocity points in the same direction as the displacement.
  • Instantaneous velocity: the velocity at any specific instant. For uniform motion, average velocity = instantaneous velocity. The magnitude of instantaneous velocity is the instantaneous speed.
Note: Example 3.4: Displacement 360 m north in 180 s → v_av = 360/180 = 2 m/s north.
Note: Example 3.5: v_av = 2.4 m/s east, t = 40 s → displacement = 96 m east.
Note: Example 3.6 (uniform motion bus): constant displacement of 40 km each hour → constant average velocity 40 km/hr.

3.4 Acceleration

  • Acceleration: the rate of change of velocity — a measure of how much velocity changes in one second. A vector quantity. Symbol ⃗a. SI unit m/s².
  • Acceleration occurs when velocity changes in magnitude (speed up or slow down), direction, or both.
  • Average acceleration: ⃗a_av = (⃗v_f − ⃗v_i)/(t_f − t_i) = Δ⃗v/Δt.
  • If the body starts from rest, ⃗v_i = 0. If velocity decreases, it is decelerating motion (deceleration = negative of acceleration). If it comes to rest, ⃗v_f = 0.
Note: Example 3.7: A train accelerates from rest to 36 km/h (10 m/s) in 20 s. a = (10 − 0)/20 = +0.5 m/s² (to the east).
Note: Example 3.8: v_i = 7.0 m/s, v_f = 12.0 m/s, a = 2.5 m/s². Δt = (12−7)/2.5 = 2 s.
Note: Note: 36 km/h = 10 m/s.

3.5 Uniform Motion

  • Uniform motion: motion of an object along a straight line with constant velocity or speed in a given direction. The object travels equal distances in fixed intervals of time.
  • Examples: a car moving on a straight road at fixed speed; an airplane flying at constant speed in a given direction.
  • Properties:
  • Acceleration is zero (a = 0) — neither magnitude nor direction of velocity changes.
  • Average velocity = instantaneous velocity at all times.

3.6 Graphical Representation of Motion

Position-Time (s–t) Graph:

  • Plots time on the horizontal (run) axis and position on the vertical (rise) axis.
  • Slope of the position-time graph = average velocity.
  • Slope = rise/run = Δ⃗X/Δt = ⃗v_av
  • For a straight-line (uniform) s–t graph, velocity is constant.
  • Slope of an s–t graph at a specific time = instantaneous velocity.
  • A horizontal s–t line (zero slope) means the body is at rest (no change in position).

Velocity-Time (v–t) Graph:

  • Plots velocity against time.
  • Slope of the velocity-time graph = acceleration.
  • A straight-line v–t graph means constant acceleration.
  • In uniform motion, the v–t graph is a horizontal line (velocity constant, acceleration zero).
  • The area under a velocity-time graph = the distance traveled.
  • For uniform motion: area = b × h = v × t = s (rectangle).

Speed limit & traffic safety: drivers must keep speed at or below the limit (e.g., 80 km/hr) to save lives and avoid property damage; speeding is a main cause of accidents. Speedometer = device that measures instantaneous speed.

📚 Unit 3 Summary (Key Facts)

  • Motion in a straight line = rectilinear motion; simplest form of motion.
  • An object is in motion if it changes position relative to a reference point.
  • Distance (scalar) vs displacement (vector).
  • Speed = distance/time; velocity = displacement/time with direction.
  • Average vs instantaneous speed/velocity.
  • Acceleration = rate of change of velocity (speed up, slow down, or change direction).
  • Uniform motion = constant velocity/speed in a straight line.
  • Slope of s–t graph = velocity; slope of v–t graph = acceleration; area under v–t graph = distance.

  2. Average speed = total distance / total time.
  3. Average velocity = displacement / time = ΔX/Δt.
  4. Acceleration = Δv/Δt (m/s²). Deceleration = negative acceleration.
  5. Uniform motion: a = 0; v_av = v_ins.
  6. Slope of s–t graph = velocity (instantaneous at a point).
  7. Slope of v–t graph = acceleration.
  8. Area under v–t graph = distance.

UNIT 4: FORCE, WORK, ENERGY AND POWER

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Physics Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

4.1 The Concept of Force

  • Force: a push or a pull exerted on a body that changes the state of motion of the body. Force can change the velocity of a body or cause deformation (change its shape or size). A vector quantity (represented by ⃗F or bold F). SI unit newton (N).
  • Any time the state of motion of an object changes, a force has been applied.
  • Examples: friction force, normal force, the force of gravity.

Contact vs Non-contact forces:

  • Contact forces: involve physical contact between objects (e.g., applied forces, normal forces, frictional forces, spring forces).
  • Non-contact forces: do not involve physical contact (e.g., magnetic force, gravitational force).

Measuring force: most conveniently done using the deformation of a spring — the spring elongates when force is applied and a pointer reads the value.

Weight (W): the magnitude of the gravitational force acting on a body, measured in newtons, directed towards the center of the Earth.

✍️ Must-know: Force = vector (push/pull that changes motion or shape). Contact forces touch; non-contact forces don't. Weight = gravitational force (N).

4.2 Newton's Laws of Motion

Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)

Note: A body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
  • Inertia: the property of matter by which it continues in its existing state of rest or uniform motion, unless changed by an external force. It is the tendency of an object to resist any attempt to change its velocity.
  • Mass is a measure of inertia. The greater the mass, the greater the inertia (more resistance to change of motion).
  • Inertial frame: a reference frame in which Newton's first law is valid. Any frame moving with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is itself inertial.
  • Mass vs weight: Mass is an inherent property, constant everywhere. Weight = gravitational force (W = mg) and can change with location.

Newton's Second Law of Motion

Note: The acceleration of a body is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to the mass of the body.
  • F = m·a (4.1)
  • Unit of force: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s² (the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s²).
  • Weight: F_g = W = mg, where g = 9.8 m/s² (acceleration due to gravity on Earth's surface, directed toward Earth's center).
  • Note: g varies with location — on the Moon g is about 1/6 of Earth's; g changes as an object moves farther from Earth's surface.
Note: Example 4.1: F = 10 N, m = 2 kg → a = F/m = 10/2 = 5 m/s² (in the direction of force).
Note: Example 4.2: F = 100 N, a = 2 m/s² → m = F/a = 50 kg.
Note: Example 4.3: m = 10 kg → W = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N.

Newton's Third Law of Motion

Note: Every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Forces always act in pairs.
  • F₁₂ = −F₂₁ (4.5): the force exerted by object 1 on object 2 equals in magnitude but is opposite in direction to the force exerted by object 2 on object 1.
  • The action and reaction forces act on different objects.
  • For a block on a table: block pushes down on table (F₁₂ = weight); table pushes up on block (F₂₁ = normal force).
✍️ Must-know: 1st law = inertia (mass measures inertia). 2nd law F = ma; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². 3rd law: action = reaction, equal & opposite, act on different objects. Weight W = mg.

4.3 Forces of Friction

  • Force of friction (f): the resistance to the motion of an object from a surface it is in contact with.
  • Friction allows us to walk/run and is necessary for wheeled vehicles (e.g., cars stuck in mud due to low friction).
  • Two main types:
  • Static friction: exists between two surfaces in contact when one body tends to slide over the other without moving. Requires a large force to overcome.
  • Kinetic friction: the friction between two contacting surfaces when one slides or rolls over the other. Its effect on motion is less than static friction.
  • The magnitude of friction depends on the normal force and the nature of the contacting surfaces. Friction is directly proportional to the normal force and is larger between rough surfaces than smooth surfaces.

4.4 The Concept of Work

  • In physics, work means the use of force to move an object. Work is done only when a force causes displacement in the direction of the force.
  • Holding a heavy chair or carrying a bucket horizontally at constant velocity does no work (force not in the direction of displacement).
  • Work = force × displacement (in the direction of force): W = F∥ × S (4.6), where F∥ is the force parallel to the displacement and S is the displacement.
  • Work is a scalar quantity. SI unit: Newton-meter (N·m) = Joule (J).
  • 1 Joule = work done when 1 newton moves an object 1 meter in the direction of the force.
  • Work is a transfer of energy from one object to another through the force.
  • Conditions for work to be zero: force = 0, or displacement = 0, or force perpendicular to displacement.
Note: Example 4.4: F = 60 N, S = 12 m → W = 720 N·m = 720 J.
Note: Example 4.5: W = 600 J, S = 3 m → F = W/S = 200 N.

4.5 Kinetic and Potential Energies

  • Energy: the capacity to do work; a scalar quantity. SI unit Joule (J) (same as work).
  • Mechanical energy = kinetic energy + potential energy.

Kinetic Energy (E_k)

  • The mechanical energy possessed by an object due to its motion (e.g., rolling ball, moving car, thrown stone).
  • Depends on mass and speed.
  • E_k = ½ m v² (4.7)
  • 1 J = 1 kg·m²/s²
Note: Example 4.6: m = 200 g = 0.2 kg, v = 20 m/s → E_k = ½ × 0.2 × 400 = 40 J.
Note: Example 4.7: m = 1 kg, E_k = 50 J → v = √(2×50/1) = 10 m/s.
Note: Exercise: For fixed speed, doubling mass doubles E_k. For fixed mass, doubling speed quadruples E_k (v²); tripling speed makes it 9×.

Gravitational Potential Energy (E_p)

  • Potential energy: the stored energy in an object by virtue of its position or configuration (two types: gravitational and elastic; we study gravitational).
  • Gravitational potential energy = energy of an object held in a vertical position due to gravity pulling it down. Depends on height and mass.
  • E_p = mgh (4.9)
  • The heavier the object and the higher it is above the ground, the more potential energy it holds.
Note: Example 4.8: m = 30 kg, h = 50 m, g = 10 m/s² → E_p = 30 × 10 × 50 = 15000 J.
Note: Example 4.9: m = 5 kg, E_p = 1000 J → h = E_p/(mg) = 1000/(5×10) = 20 m.
✍️ Must-know: E_k = ½mv² (motion); E_p = mgh (position/height); Mechanical energy = E_k + E_p.

4.6 Power

  • Power: the rate at which work is done or the rate at which energy is transferred.
  • Power = work done / time taken = energy transferred / time taken
  • P = W/t or P = E/t (4.11)
  • Power is a scalar quantity. SI unit J/s = watt (W).
  • 1 W = power developed when 1 joule of energy is transferred in 1 second (1 W = 1 J/s).
Note: Example 4.10: E = 1500 J, t = 5 s → P = 1500/5 = 300 W.
Note: Example 4.11: Water pump lifts 500 kg through 10 m in 5 s → P = mgh/t = (500×10×10)/5 = 10000 W = 10 kW.

📚 Unit 4 Summary (Key Facts)

  • Force = push/pull changing motion or shape (vector).
  • Contact vs non-contact forces.
  • Newton's three laws of motion; inertia; F = ma; action-reaction.
  • Mass (inherent, constant) vs weight (W = mg, changes with g).
  • Friction: static and kinetic; proportional to normal force.
  • Work = F∥ × S; scalar; Joule; work = energy transfer.
  • Kinetic energy ½mv²; gravitational potential energy mgh; mechanical energy = sum.
  • Power = rate of work/energy; watt.

UNIT 5: SIMPLE MACHINES

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Physics Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

5.1 Simple Machines and their Purposes

  • Simple machine: any device that helps us do work more easily — a device that requires a single force to do mechanical work.
  • Simple machines are energy transferring devices: they do NOT create energy or change one form into another. They transfer mechanical energy involving a small force into mechanical energy involving a large force (and vice versa).
  • Purposes of simple machines: make mechanical work easier by:
  1. Changing the direction of force (e.g., raising a flag with a pulley — pull down to raise up).
  2. Changing the distance of effort (to multiply speed or distance) — e.g., carrying a box up stairs travels a longer distance.
  3. Changing the strength of a force (to multiply force) — e.g., bottle opener applies a strong force over a short distance.

Key terms:

  • Effort (F): the force exerted on a machine by an external body (e.g., a human being). Work input = effort × distance moved by effort.
  • Load (L): the force exerted by a machine on the object to be lifted/moved. Work output = load × distance moved by load.

Machine categories:

  • Force multiplier: load > effort (inclined plane).
  • Speed (distance) multiplier: load moves farther than effort (bicycle).
  • Direction changer: load moves in a different direction than the effort (fixed single pulley).
✍️ Must-know: Machines do not create energy. Effort = force put in; Load = force got out. Work input = effort × distance; Work output = load × distance.

5.2 Simple Machines at Home

  • Simple machines reduce the amount of force required to do work; they change the direction or magnitude of a force and offer mechanical advantage.
  • They are the building blocks of more complex machines. Break a complex machine down to its smallest parts and you find simple machines: pulleys, wheels, levers, wedges, and screws.
  • Functions: change direction, multiply speed/distance, multiply force.

5.3 Simple Machines at Work Place

  • A simple machine accomplishes a task with only one movement (e.g., a lever).
  • Modern technology (cars, airplanes) began with the invention of simple machines.
  • Simple machines combine to make compound machines (e.g., wire cutter = two levers + two wedges; bicycle = wheel & axles, levers, screws, pulleys).

5.4 Classification of Simple Machines

Simple machines are categorized into two groups, giving six types:

1. Inclined planes:

  • Ramp (inclined plane)
  • Wedge
  • Screw

2. Levers:

  • Lever
  • Wheel & axle
  • Pulley

Three categories by function:

  1. Force multipliers: load > effort (load moves a smaller distance than effort).
  2. Speed multipliers: distance moved by load > distance moved by effort in the same time.
  3. Direction changers: load moved in a different direction to the effort.

5.5 Mechanical Advantage, Velocity Ratio and Efficiency

Mechanical Advantage (M.A)

  • When a machine turns a small input force into a larger output force, it gives mechanical advantage.
  • M.A = Output force / Input force = Load / Effort = F_L / F_E (5.3)
  • M.A has no units (a ratio).
  • M.A > 1 = force multiplier (load > effort). M.A = 1 if input = output.
  • Actual Mechanical Advantage (AMA): compares load with effort considering energy losses (friction).
  • Ideal Mechanical Advantage (IMA): mechanical advantage if there were no energy losses.
  • In the real world, IMA is always greater than AMA (friction). For calculations we often assume no losses, so AMA = IMA = MA.
Note: Example 5.1: Load 400 N, effort 160 N → M.A = 400/160 = 2.5.

Velocity Ratio (V.R)

  • V.R = distance moved by effort / distance moved by load (5.4)
  • V.R has no units. If V.R = 3, effort moves 3× as far as load.
  • If V.R < 1, the machine multiplies speed/distance (load moves farther); if V.R > 1, it multiplies force.
Note: Example 5.2: d_E = 40 cm, d_L = 10 cm → V.R = 40/10 = 4.

Efficiency (η)

  • Describes how good a machine is at transferring input energy to useful output energy.
  • Machines waste energy due to friction between moving parts, so efficiency is less than one (100%).
  • η = work output / work input = power output / power input (5.5)
  • Also expressed in terms of M.A and V.R:
  • η = M.A / V.R (5.7)
  • Efficiency has no units (ratio), often multiplied by 100 to give a percentage.
Note: Example 5.3: Work output 80 J, work input 400 J → η = 80/400 = 0.2 = 20%. For 2000 J input → output = 0.2 × 2000 = 400 J.
Note: Exercise: M.A = 3, V.R = 6 → η = 3/6 = 0.5 = 50%. If M.A = V.R, machine is 100% efficient (ideal, no friction).

Levers

  • Lever: a rigid bar (wood or metal) free to turn about a supporting point called the fulcrum (F).
  • A lever has three parts: effort point (E), load point (L), and fulcrum (F).
  • Load arm: the part of the lever from the fulcrum to the load. Effort arm: from the fulcrum to where force is applied.
  • Archimedes: "Give me a place to stand and I shall move the Earth with a lever."

Three classes of levers:

  1. First-class lever: fulcrum between load and effort. Examples: seesaw, scissors, crowbar. IMA can be >, =, or < 1.
  2. Second-class lever: load between effort and fulcrum. Examples: wheelbarrow, nutcrackers, bottle opener. IMA always > 1.
  3. Third-class lever: effort between load and fulcrum. Examples: fishing pole, tweezers, an arm lifting a weight, calipers, broom, hockey stick, tennis racket, spade, shovel. IMA always < 1.

M.A, V.R, and efficiency of levers:

  • M.A = load/effort = F_L/F_E (5.8)
  • V.R = d_E/d_L (distance from effort to fulcrum / distance from load to fulcrum) (5.9)
  • If no energy losses, IMA = V.R = d_E/d_L (5.10)
Note: Example 5.4: L = 200 N, E = 80 N, d_L = 10 cm, d_E = 40 cm.
Note: V.R = 40/10 = 4; M.A = 200/80 = 2.5; η = M.A/V.R = 2.5/4 = 0.625 = 62.5%.

Inclined Plane, Wedge and Screw

Inclined plane (ramp): a sloping surface connecting two points; the simplest machine. Object lifted to height h by sliding it up the length l.

  • M.A = Load/Effort = L/E (5.11)
  • V.R = length of inclined surface / height = l/h (5.12)
Note: Exercise: V.R of a slope 30 m long raising to 5 m = 30/5 = 6. For 40 m slope, 8 m height, effort 80 N, load 240 N: M.A = 3, V.R = 5, η = 60%.

Wedge: used to separate or split objects (knives, nails, axes, spears). Load = force on the object being split; effort = force applied to the top of the wedge.

  • M.A = L/E (5.13)
  • V.R = penetration length / thickness = l/t (5.14)
  • η = M.A/V.R = (L×t)/(E×l) (5.15)
  • The more narrow the wedge, the greater the ratio of penetration length to thickness.

Screw: a cylinder with a helical thread around it (includes nuts and bolts). In one turn it moves a distance equal to the separation between threads = the pitch (p).

  • M.A = πd/p = 2πr/p (5.16), where d = mean diameter (d = 2r) and p = pitch.

The Wheel and Axle

  • Wheel and axle: a large wheel secured to a smaller wheel called the axle.
  • If input force turns the wheel and output is on the axle: since the wheel is larger than the axle, mechanical advantage > 1 (force multiplier). Examples: screwdriver, drill, windmill, water wheel, doorknob.
  • If input force turns the axle (output on wheel): M.A < 1, output force < input force (speed multiplier). Examples: bicycle, car tires, electric fan.
  • M.A = L/E (5.17)
  • V.R = radius of wheel / radius of axle = R/r (5.18)
  • If no energy losses, V.R = M.A = IMA = R/r.
Note: Exercise: If the wheel diameter is 20× the axle diameter, M.A = 20 (since ratio of radii = ratio of diameters).

The Pulley System

  • Pulley: a circular body (wheel) with a grooved surface, free to rotate about its center. Three types:
  1. Fixed pulley: fixed axle, rope looped over the top. Used to change direction of force. Acts as a first-class lever. Advantage: no need to pull up/down. Disadvantage: you apply more effort than the load (needs more effort to lift).
  2. Movable pulley: axle free to move up/down. Applying effort to one end gives ~2× force; V.R = 2 (pull 2 m of rope to lift 1 m). Advantage: less effort. Disadvantage: must pull/push up/down.
  3. Compound pulley (block and tackle): combination of fixed and movable pulleys. Effort needed is less than half the load; advantage: less effort; disadvantage: travels a very long distance.
  • For pulleys: M.A = load/effort and V.R = IMA = N (number of sections of ropes used to lift the load).
  • For fixed and movable pulleys there are energy losses due to friction, so M.A < V.R.

UNIT 6: MECHANICAL OSCILLATION AND SOUND WAVE

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Physics Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

6.1 Common Characteristics of Waves

  • Oscillation (periodic motion): a to-and-fro motion about a fixed point (equilibrium position) that repeats itself. Examples: a swing, a pendulum, a mass on a spring.
  • Wave: a disturbance that moves from its source accompanied by the transfer of energy. A wave is comprised of a series/train of pulses traveling in a medium.

Common characteristics of all waves:

  • Rest position: the undisturbed position of particles when they are not vibrating.
  • Displacement: the distance a point in the medium has moved from its rest position.
  • Trough: the lowest point below the rest position.
  • Crest: the highest point above the rest position.
  • Period (T): the time for one complete cycle (or for one complete wave to pass a given point). Unit: second (s).
  • Frequency (f): the number of complete waves passing a point per unit time. Unit: Hertz (Hz) = cycle/s.
  • f = 1/T and T = 1/f (6.1) — period is the reciprocal of frequency.
  • Amplitude (A): the maximum displacement from the equilibrium position. Unit: meter (m).
  • Wave speed (v): the distance a wave travels in one second. Unit: m/s.
  • Wavelength (λ): the distance between identical points on adjacent waves (e.g., between two adjacent crests or troughs). Unit: meter.
  • Wave speed = frequency × wavelength: v = λ × f (6.2)
Note: Example: A wave of frequency 2 Hz means 2 waves pass per second, so each takes 0.5 s → period = 0.5 s.

6.2 String, Pendulum and Spring

  • Pulse: a single traveling disturbance (created by flicking a string once). A wave is a series/train of pulses.

Periodic motion: a to-and-fro movement that repeats itself in a fixed time interval (heartbeat, clock ticking, swing).

Simple Pendulum

  • A simple pendulum = a point mass (the bob) suspended by a massless, un-stretchable string.
  • If displaced from equilibrium, it swings back and forth periodically.
  • Period of a simple pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g) (6.3), where L = length and g = acceleration due to gravity.
  • The period depends only on length (L) and g — it is independent of the mass of the bob.
Note: Example 6.1: L = 50 cm = 0.5 m, g = 9.8 m/s² → T = 2π√(0.5/9.8) = 1.42 s.
Note: Example 6.2: L = 1 m, T = 0.65π s → g = (2π/T)² × L = 9.47 m/s².

The Simple Spring (Hooke's Law)

  • An object of mass m attached to a fixed spring has an equilibrium position x = 0 (neither stretched nor compressed).
  • When displaced and released, it undergoes periodic to-and-fro motion.
  • Hooke's law: the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement x from equilibrium.
  • F_res = −kx (6.4), where k = spring constant (stiffness), SI unit N/m. The negative sign indicates the restoring force acts opposite to the displacement.
  • Applied force: F_app = kx (6.5) (F_app = −F_res).
  • The spring force is always a restoring force — it always acts to return the object to equilibrium.
  • If k is large → stiff spring (lots of force for small displacement). If k is small → loose spring.
  • Period of a spring-mass system: T = 2π√(m/k) (6.6), where m = mass and k = spring constant.
  • The period depends only on mass and spring constant.
Note: Example 6.3: m = 2 kg, k = 200 N/m → T = 2π√(2/200) = 2π/10 = π/5 s.

6.3 Propagation of Waves and Energy Transmission

  • Wave: a disturbance propagating in space or matter.
  • Mechanical waves: require a material medium to travel (vibrations of particles of the medium). Examples: sound waves, water waves, waves in strings.
  • Electromagnetic waves: do NOT require a medium; composed of vibrating electric and magnetic fields; travel through a vacuum. Examples: light, radio waves, X-rays.

By direction of vibration:

  • Transverse wave: direction of propagation is perpendicular to the direction of particle vibrations. Examples: all electromagnetic waves, waves on strings.
  • Longitudinal wave: direction of propagation is parallel to the direction of particle vibrations. Examples: sound waves, pressure waves.
  • Produces compressions (particles pushed together) and rarefactions (particles move apart). Compressions = longitudinal version of a crest; rarefactions = version of a trough.

6.4 Sound Waves

  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects; every source of sound is in a state of vibration. Sound travels in all directions as a wave.
  • Sound is the most common example of a longitudinal wave.
  • Sound needs a material medium for transmission (e.g., air). It cannot travel through a vacuum.

Speed of sound:

  • Depends on the compressibility and density of the medium — different speeds in different media.
  • Speed in air at 0 °C ≈ 331 m/s.
  • v = 331 m/s × √(1 + T_c/273°C) (6.7), where T_c is the air temperature in °C.
  • Sound travels slowest in gases, faster in liquids, fastest in solids (particles closest in solids).
  • Speed in liquids and solids is not significantly affected by temperature change, but by body structure.

Speed of sound in various materials: Air (0 °C) 331, Air (25 °C) 343, Sea water (25 °C) 1533, Water (25 °C) 1493, Iron 5950, Lead 1960, Rubber 1600 m/s.

Note: Example 6.4: Speed of sound at 20 °C: v = 331×√(1+20/273) ≈ 343 m/s.

Wave behaviors:

  • Reflection (echo): sound bounces back from hard surfaces; reflection of sound from hard surfaces is called an echo. Used in SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging) to find depth of seas/submarines. Hard polished surfaces are good reflectors; soft surfaces (wool, cloth) absorb sound.
  • Refraction: the bending/change in direction of a sound wave due to a change in its speed (e.g., uneven winds, air of uneven temperature). During the day warm air near ground makes sound bend away; at night cold air makes it bend toward the ground (so we hear better at night).
  • Diffraction: the spreading out or change in direction of a wave as it passes through an opening or around a barrier. Because of diffraction we hear people in adjacent rooms. More pronounced with longer wavelengths (we hear low frequencies better around obstacles).
  • Interference: see section 6.5.

6.5 Superposition of Waves

  • Superposition principle: when two or more waves pass through a single point at the same time, the resultant instantaneous displacement at that point is the sum of the displacements that would be created separately by each wave (taking signs into account).
  • Interference = superposition. Two types:
  • Constructive interference: waves in phase (corresponding points reach max/min at same time) → resultant wave is more amplified.
  • Destructive interference: waves out of phase → resultant wave is diminished (may be zero).

Standing (stationary) waves:

  • Formed by the superposition of two identical waves travelling in opposite directions in the same medium. Oscillates with time but remains fixed in location.
  • Antinodes (A): positions of maximum displacement. Nodes (N): positions of zero displacement.
  • Distance between adjacent antinodes = λ/2; between adjacent nodes = λ/2; between a node and an adjacent antinode = λ/4.

6.6 Characteristics of Sound Waves

  • Sound cannot travel through a vacuum (movement of molecules is essential).
  • Pitch: depends on the frequency of the sound wave. Higher frequency → higher pitch.
  • Loudness: depends on the amplitude of the sound wave. Greater amplitude → louder. Loudness is measured in decibel (dB).
  • Noise becomes harmful above 75 dB and painful above 120 dB. Exposure to elevated sound that causes adverse effects = sound pollution (hypertension, hearing loss, sleep disturbance).
  • Timbre (quality): the property of a tone that distinguishes it from another tone of the same pitch and intensity but produced by different sources (e.g., the same note sounds different on a violin vs piano). Quality refers to the difference in sound, not good/bad.

UNIT 7: TEMPERATURE AND THERMOMETRY

Note: Based on the official Ethiopian Ministry of Education Grade 9 Physics Student Textbook (New Curriculum, 2023).

7.1 Temperature and Our Life

  • Temperature: the degree of hotness or coldness of a body; or the measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample of matter.
  • Human feeling of hot/cold is relative and not reliable (the warm-water activity: warm water feels cool to a hand from hot water, and warm to a hand from cold water).
  • Room temperature (comfortable for humans): 18 °C to 22 °C. Human body normal temperature ≈ 98.6 °F (37 °C).
  • Temperature affects clothing, eating habits, health, and even economy.
  • Global temperature variation: polar regions are very cold, temperate regions moderate, tropical regions hot. The equator receives the most direct sunlight.
  • Tropical rain forest yearly average: 21–31 °C.
  • Temperate climates average: −3 to 18 °C.
  • Polar zone average: −14 °C (summer), −50 °C (winter).
  • High-temperature bodies: Sun's outer surface ≈ 5600 K (≈5327 °C), fire, electric heaters/stoves, candle flame. Low-temperature bodies: ice (below 0 °C), solid CO₂ (−78 °C), liquid nitrogen (−196 °C).

7.2 Extreme Temperature Safety

  • Normal body temperature: between 97 °F (36.56 °C) and 99 °F (37.2 °C); average 98.6 °F (37 °C). To maintain this without devices, surroundings need to be about 82 °F (28 °C).
  • Heat risks:
  • 90–105 °F (32–40 °C): heat cramps and exhaustion.
  • 105–130 °F (40–54 °C): heat exhaustion more likely — limit activity.
  • Over 130 °F (54 °C): heat stroke.
  • Safety precautions: stay well-hydrated; wear appropriate clothing; wear sunscreen; avoid extremely hot places.

7.3 Temperature Change and its Effects

  • Global average temperature increased by about 1.4 °F (0.8 °C) over the past 100 years. The hottest year on record (as of text) was 2016.
  • Greenhouse effect: the natural warming of the Earth when greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, water vapor, fluorinated gases) trap heat from the Sun. Naturally important to warm the planet to ~15 °C.
  • Human activity (burning fossil fuels) artificially increases the greenhouse effect → global warming.
  • If greenhouse gases keep being added at the current rate, average temperature could rise 4–12 °F; with big changes (renewables instead of fossil fuels), only 2–5 °F.
  • Higher temperatures cause heat waves (heat cramps, heat stroke, death), and affect oceans, weather, snow/ice, plants/animals.
  • Greenhouse gases: carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, and fluorinated gases.

7.4 Measuring Temperature with Different Thermometric Scales

  • Thermometer: a device used to measure the temperature of a body, in degrees.
  • Most thermometers use the fact that materials expand with increasing temperature. A thermometer has a uniform thin-bore tube with a small bulb, filled with mercury or alcohol.
  • Fixed points: two temperatures marked on a thermometer — the lower fixed point (melting point of ice) and the upper fixed point (boiling point of water at sea level).

Three temperature scales:

  • Celsius (centigrade) scale (Anders Celsius, 1701–1744): ice point = 0 °C, boiling point = 100 °C; divided into 100 equal parts.
  • Fahrenheit scale (Daniel Fahrenheit): ice point = 32 °F, boiling point = 212 °F; divided into 180 equal parts.
  • Kelvin scale (Lord Kelvin, 1824–1907): ice point = 273.15 K, boiling point = 373.15 K; divided into 100 parts. SI unit of temperature = kelvin (K) — no degree sign.
  • Absolute zero = 0 K = −273.15 °C, the lowest possible temperature at which a substance has no thermal energy.

Reading a thermometer: when temperature rises, the mercury expands and rises; when it falls, it contracts and drops.

7.5 Types of Thermometers and Their Use

A property that changes with temperature is called a thermometric property (e.g., length of mercury column, gas pressure, electrical resistance, voltage).

  1. Mercury thermometer: uses expansion/contraction of mercury; suitable for −30 °C to 300 °C. Used in households, labs, industry.
  2. Alcohol thermometer: uses expansion/contraction of alcohol (ethanol); −115 °C to 78.15 °C. Popular because nontoxic (unlike mercury).
  3. Resistance thermometer: uses electrical resistance (changes with temperature), often made of platinum wire; good for −270 °C to +700 °C.
  4. Thermocouple: two thin wires of different metals (e.g., copper and constantan) welded at ends forming two junctions. Generates a voltage that depends on temperature difference between the hot junction (in contact with the object) and reference junction (at known temp, e.g., ice-water at 0 °C). Measures up to 2300 °C or down to −270 °C.
  5. Thermistor: a resistance thermometer (resistor whose resistance depends on temperature), made of metallic oxides. NTC (negative temperature coefficient): resistance decreases as temperature increases (most used). PTC (positive): resistance increases as temperature increases (used as a fuse). High precision within ~50 °C around target temperature.
  6. Radiation thermometer: uses the electromagnetic radiation emitted by a body (not a property change). As body warms, total radiation increases and shifts to shorter wavelengths. Advantage: detector is remote from the hot body. Used in industry, motors, glass/electrical industries.

7.6 Conversion between Temperature Scales

Celsius ↔ Fahrenheit:

  • From the proportional relation: T_C/100 = (T_F − 32)/180
  • T_C = 5/9 × (T_F − 32) = (T_F − 32)/1.8 (7.3)
  • T_F = 9/5 × T_C + 32 = 1.8·T_C + 32 (7.4)
Note: Example 7.1: T_F = 50 °F → T_C = 5/9(50−32) = 10 °C.
Note: Example 7.2: T_C = 20 °C → T_F = 9/5(20)+32 = 68 °F.

Celsius ↔ Kelvin:

  • T_C = T_K − 273.15 and T_K = T_C + 273.15 (7.7)
Note: Example 7.3: T_C = 72 °C → T_K = 72 + 273.15 = 345.15 K.
Note: Example 7.4: T_K = 573.15 K → T_C = 573.15 − 273.15 = 300 °C.

Fahrenheit ↔ Kelvin: convert through Celsius (F → C → K).

Key relationships to remember:

  • The change in temperature of Celsius and Kelvin scales are equal (size of 1 K = size of 1 °C).
  • A temperature difference of 5 °C = 5 K.

7.7 Thermal Expansion of Materials

  • Thermal expansion: the increase in size of a substance when heated (particles move faster and farther apart). Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.
  • In general: gases expand more than liquids, and liquids expand more than solids.
  • Different materials expand at different rates.
  • Linear expansion: the increase in any one dimension (length) of a solid.
  • Coefficient of linear expansion (α): the increase in length of a 1 m rod of a given substance when its temperature increases by 1 K. Unit: 1/°C (or °C⁻¹).

Formula:

  • ΔL = α·L₀·ΔT (7.11), where:
  • ΔL = change in length, L₀ = original length, ΔT = change in temperature, α = coefficient of linear expansion.

Coefficients of linear expansion (α, in °C⁻¹): Aluminum 2.6×10⁻⁵, Brass 1.9×10⁻⁵, Concrete 1.2×10⁻⁵, Copper 1.7×10⁻⁵, Glass (common) 8.5×10⁻⁶, Glass (Pyrex) 3.3×10⁻⁶, Gold 1.4×10⁻⁵, Iron/steel 1.2×10⁻⁵, Lead 2.9×10⁻⁵, Nickel 1.3×10⁻⁵, Quartz (fused) 0.5×10⁻⁶, Silver 1.9×10⁻⁵.

Note: Example 7.5: Gold rod L₀ = 1.5×10⁻¹ m, heated from 27 °C to 49 °C. ΔL = (1.4×10⁻⁵)(1.5×10⁻¹)(22) = 4.62×10⁻⁵ m.
Note: Example 7.6: Brass rod L₀ = 50 cm, heated 25 °C → 70 °C. ΔL = (1.9×10⁻⁵)(0.5)(45) = 0.043 cm → final length = 50.043 cm.

Applications/notes:

  • Run hot water over a tight metal lid to loosen it (metal expands more than glass).
  • Bimetallic strip: two thin strips of metals with different coefficients welded together. When heated, it bends (toward the metal with the smaller α); used in thermostats and switches.
  • A hole in a solid expands when the solid is heated (expands with the same percent as the material).
  • For construction, choose metals that don't vary much with temperature (e.g., fused quartz has very small α).
