🔑 MASTER GRAMMAR & KEY-FACTS SHEET (keep for revision)

Tenses (the single most-tested area)

  • Simple present = habitual actions, general truths, schedules, future plans. State verbs (know, believe, think, understand, want, like) take present simple, not continuous.
  • Present continuous = action happening now; present period; annoying repeated action.
  • Present perfect (have/has + V3) = action began in past still ongoing; indefinite past time; present effect; experience. Often with already, yet, just, for, since, never, ever.
  • Simple past (V2) = finished past action, definite time (yesterday, ago, last, in 2000).
  • Past perfect (had + V3) = "before" tense — action completed before another past action.
  • Simple future (will + V1) = prediction, promise, offer, decision. "be going to" = plan/intention or prediction from present evidence.

Degrees of comparison

  • Positive: as…as. Comparative: –er / more. Superlative: –est / most.
  • Double comparative: "the more…the better."
  • No comparative for perfect, dead, single, unique, round, square.
  • prefer/senior/junior/inferior/superior + to.
  • Irregular: good→better→best; bad→worse→worst; many/much→more→most; little→less→least.

Relative pronouns

  • who (people, subject), whom (people, object), whose (possession), which (things), that (people/things). "of which" for animals/possessions of things.
  • Essential (defining) clause: no commas. Non-essential (non-defining): commas.

Modals

  • should / ought to / had better = advice. must / have to = obligation (past = had to). can/could = ability, request, permission. will/won't = future, promise, refusal.
  • suggest + gerund / that-clause (never "suggest to do").

Active vs Passive

  • Passive = be + V3 (present: is/are + V3; past: was/were + V3). Modal passive = modal + be + V3. Object becomes subject.

Subject-verb agreement (classic matric trap)

  • one/either/neither of + plural noun + singular verb (but verb after a relative pronoun agrees with its antecedent).
  • together with / along with / as well as / including / with → verb agrees with first subject.
  • either…or / neither…nor → verb agrees with nearest subject.
  • Non-count nouns take singular; but "a piece of / a slice of / a bar of" take plural verb.
  • The number of → singular; A number of → plural.

Conditionals (4 types)

  • Type 0 (truth): If + present, present.
  • Type 1 (real/future): If + present, will + V1.
  • Type 2 (unreal present): If + past (were), would + V1.
  • Type 3 (unreal past): If + had + V3, would have + V3.
  • if clause and main clause can swap order. Wishes: If I were…

Reported (indirect) speech — backshift

  • present→past; present perfect→past perfect; simple past→past perfect; will→would; can→could; may→might.
  • Pronouns and time/place words change (this→that, today→that day, tomorrow→the following day, now→then, ago→before, here→there).

Quantifiers

  • many / few / a few → countable. much / little / a little → uncountable. some/any for indefinite.

Articles

  • a before consonant sound (a uniform, a one-eyed man); an before vowel sound (an hour, an honest man, an heir, an honorarium). the for specific/known nouns, superlatives, unique things, rivers/deserts, musical instruments.

Clauses

  • Result: so + adj/adv + that; such + (adj) noun + that; as a result, consequently, therefore, thus, hence, due to, owing to.
  • Purpose: so that, in order that, in order to, to, for fear that, lest.
  • Reason: because, as, since, for, due to, owing to, because of, on account of.
  • Manner: as, as if, as though.

so / neither

  • so + aux + pronoun (positive agreement): "So do I." neither + aux + pronoun (negative): "Neither do I." Never "neither don't."

do / make

  • do: homework, business, gardening, the cleaning, a course, damage, a favour, duty.
  • make: an occasion, impression, effort, comment, offer, change, attempt, gesture, noise, a decision, arrangement.

Word-formation (all units)

  • Prefixes: un, in, im, ir, dis, mis, re, pre, bi, de, kilo, out, auto, co, anti, super, hyper, circum, pro, none, sub.
  • Suffixes: -ness, -ful, -less, -ship, -ment, -er, -tion, -ly, -able.
  • Conversion (same word, new class): advise→advice(v/n), report, examine, produce.
  • Compounding: noun+noun (pen friend, firewood), adjective+noun (greenhouse), verb+noun (swimming pool), hyphenated (commanders-in-chief, up-to-date, check-in).
  • Blending: breakfast+lunch=brunch; advertisement→ad. Clipping: examination→exam, mathematics→maths, influenza→flu, telephone→phone.
  • Back-formation: editor→edit, television→televise, babysitter→babysit.
  • Borrowing: French (chancellor, court, judge), Latin (agile, abdomen), Greek (atmosphere, data, skeleton).

⚠️ COMMON EXAM TRAPS (avoid these)

  • ✋ "I am think…" / "I am believing…" — state verbs don't take continuous. Use "I think," "I believe."
  • ✋ "Neither I don't" — never use a negative verb after "neither." Use "Neither do I."
  • ✋ "He should to go" / "He must to go" — modals take bare infinitive, no "to" (except ought to, have to).
  • ✋ "One of my friends are…" — after "one of," use a singular verb: "One of my friends is…"
  • ✋ "The president together with the ministers have…" — verb agrees with "the president," singular → "has."
  • ✋ "suggest to do" — use "suggest doing" or "suggest that…"
  • ✋ Put "so" before many/much/little + noun: "so many books," but "such a big book." ("so much money," "so little food").
  • ✋ "If I was you" → "If I were you." For unreal conditions always "were" for all persons.
  • ✋ Confusing do and make (make an effort, do homework).
  • ✋ Spelling "ie/ei" (believe, receive, receipt, neighbor, weight), silent-e rules, plural –ies/–s.
  • ✋ Preposition after arrive: "arrive at a place" / "arrive in a country"; "married to," "depend on," "interested in."

1.1 Reading: National and Global Population Growth

(by Mesfin Aberra — the core "story"/text of the unit)

The passage has four paragraphs. Understand the main idea of each and be ready to answer questions about it.

Paragraph 1 — Environmental degradation:

  • Rapid population growth → environmental degradation → poverty and famine.
  • Rich and poor countries degrade the environment differently: the rich (industrialisation, over-consumption), the poor (dependence on natural resources).
  • Two forces are blamed: anthropogenic (human-caused) factors and lack of material resources / illiteracy.

Paragraph 2 — Ethiopia's rural picture:

  • About 85% of Ethiopia's population lives in rural areas, concentrated in the central and northeastern highlands.
  • Causes of land degradation: deforestation, over-farming, soil erosion.
  • The 2003 famine affected more than 13 million people; about 6 million face permanent food insecurity.
  • The Green Legacy campaign is mentioned as a response to reforestation.

Paragraph 3 — Poor women's health:

  • Poor women suffer most — about 1 in 14 women die during pregnancy or childbirth; about 114 infants die per 1,000 live births (Save the Children, 2004).
  • Recommended solutions: girls' education and women's empowerment; raising the ratio of female officials.

Paragraph 4 — Global population:

  • World population grew from 1 billion to a 7-fold increase in about 200 years.
  • Reached 7 billion in 2011; roughly 9.4 billion now.
  • Growth is driven by more children surviving to reproductive age, urbanisation, and migration.
  • A growing population needs a growing economy — and must comply with environmental management: conservation, preservation, rehabilitation, sustainability.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Reading-comprehension questions here test (a) main idea of each paragraph, (b) cause–effect links (growth→degradation→poverty/famine), (c) specific figures (85% rural, 13 million famine victims, 1 in 14 women, 114 infants, 7 billion in 2011), and (d) vocabulary in context. Learn the figures — they are frequently quoted in the questions.

1.4 Grammar

1.4.1 Degrees of Comparison

  • Positive (equality): as + adjective + as → "Tola is as tall as Biruk."
  • Comparative: adjective –er / more + adj + than → "Simba is faster than Fox."
  • Superlative: the + adjective –est / most + adj → "She is the most diligent student."
  • Double comparative (the + comparative, the + comparative): "The more you study, the better you score." "The more we develop punctuality, the better we achieve our goals."

Important rules & exceptions:

  • Words that already express absolute/perfect state take no comparative or superlative: perfect, dead, single, unique, round, square.
  • With prefer, senior, junior, inferior, superior use to, not than: "Coffee is superior to tea." "She is senior to me."
  • Irregular forms: good→better→best; bad→worse→worst; many/much→more→most; little→less→least; far→farther/further→farthest/furthest.
  • Short adjectives (1–2 syllables) → –er/–est (big, happy→happier). Longer adjectives → more/most (beautiful→more beautiful). Use "less/least" for the decreasing direction.

1.4.2 Simple Present vs Present Perfect

Simple present (V1 / V1+s) is used for:

  • habitual actions: "She goes to school every day."
  • general truths/scientific facts: "Water boils at 100°C."
  • scheduled future: "The train leaves at six."
  • headlines, commentaries.
  • State verbs (understand, believe, know, like, want) — always present simple, never continuous: "I understand the lesson" (not "am understanding").

Present perfect (have/has + V3) is used for:

  • an action that began in the past and still continues: "We have lived here for ten years."
  • an action whose time is not given / indefinite: "I have seen that film."
  • past action with a present result: "She has broken her leg" (so she limps now).
  • the doer is unknown/unimportant: "The window has been broken."
Note: ✋ Trap: "I have visited Sof Umer Cave ten years ago" — WRONG. ago = definite past → simple past: "I visited it ten years ago." Use present perfect only with for/since/just/already/yet/never/ever and no definite past time.

1.4.3 Relative Pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, that)

  • who → people (subject): "The man who came is my uncle."
  • whom → people (object, formal): "The doctor whom I met…"
  • whose → possession: "The student whose pen I borrowed…"
  • which → things/animals: "The book which I read…"
  • that → people or things (restrictive): "The car that I bought…"
  • of which → for animals/possessions of things: "The lion, the mane of which was long…"

Essential vs Non-essential clauses:

  • Essential (defining, restrictive): needed to identify the noun; no commas. "The students who study hard pass." (Only those who study hard.)
  • Non-essential (non-defining): extra information; set off by commas. "My brother, who lives in Bahir Dar, is a doctor."

1.4.4 Modals: suggest, should, ought to, had better

  • suggest takes a gerund or a that-clause, never "suggest to + verb":
  • "She suggested going home." ✓
  • "She suggested that we go home." ✓
  • "She suggested to go home." ✋ WRONG.
  • should = strong recommendation: "You should see a doctor."
  • ought to = advice (synonym of should, always with "to"): "You ought to apologise."
  • had better = warning/strong advice (can imply danger): "You had better hurry or you'll be late." (had better + bare infinitive)

1.4.5 Punctuation of sentences

  • Full stop / period (.) = ends a statement or command.
  • Question mark (?) = ends a direct question.
  • Exclamation mark (!) = strong feeling or emphasis.

Discourse markers

Common linking words used across units: because, since, as, therefore, so, consequently, thus, hence, as a result, owing to, due to, however, although, on the other hand, in addition, moreover, for example, in contrast.

  • Use discourse markers to connect your ideas and give reasons.

UNIT 2: TRAVEL BEHAVIORS

Note: Based on the official ANRSEB Grade 10 English Student Textbook (new curriculum, 2023), Unit 2.

2.1 Reading: Travel Behaviors (the core "story"/text)

The passage has three paragraphs:

Paragraph 1 — Travel is everyday human behaviour:

  • Travelling is an everyday human behaviour; but some people find it an ordeal (they get travel sickness, feel discomfort).
  • People travel for business, pleasure, or education.
  • Travel broadens the mind; it is part of curricula (some countries fund student travel).

Paragraph 2 — Travel behavior is complicated decision-making:

  • Travel behavior is complicated decision-making about mode, route, time, and destination.
  • It is interdependent with people's beliefs, attitudes, and values.
  • These predetermine your purpose, how much money you take, and what items you carry.

2.4 Grammar

2.4.1 The Simple Past Tense

  • Regular verbs: add –ed (walk→walked, work→worked).
  • Irregular verbs: change form (go→went, take→took, come→came, see→saw, buy→bought).
  • Signal words: yesterday, ago, last (week/month/year), in 2000, the other day, then, when.
  • Uses: a completed action at a definite past time: "I visited Lalibela last year." Past habits/sequence of events: "He woke up, ate, and left."
  • If-clause type 2 uses past simple for an unreal present condition: "If I had money, I would travel." (See Unit 7 conditionals.)
Note: ✋ Trap: Do not use present perfect with a definite past time word (yesterday/ago/last). Use simple past.

2.4.2 The Four Sentence Structures (important for writing & identifying)

  1. Simple sentence — one independent clause (one main verb + subject).
  • Subject + Verb: "Birds fly." / S+V+O: "She reads books." / S+V+C: "He is happy." / S+V+O+O: "She gave me a gift."
  1. Compound sentence — two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon.
  • "I was tired, but I kept walking." / "He is smart; he works hard."
  1. Complex sentence — one independent clause + one or more dependent (subordinate) clauses joined by a subordinator.
  • Subordinators: although, even if, as if, after, when, since, as, while, because, before, if, unless.
  • "Although it rained, we went out." (dependent + independent)
  1. Compound-complex sentence — at least two independent clauses + at least one dependent clause.
  • "I wanted to go, but I stayed home because I was tired."
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Be able to classify any sentence as simple / compound / complex / compound-complex. Identify coordinators (FANBOYS) for compound and subordinators for complex.

2.4.3 Summary Writing (rules to memorise)

A good summary:

  • is in paragraph form;
  • opens with a sentence stating the writer's most essential idea;
  • does not include every idea from the original;
  • understands the main idea of each paragraph and rewrites it briefly in your own words;
  • is about one-third of the original length;
  • does not insert your own opinions, interpretations, deductions, or comments;
  • ends with a sentence that wraps up/concludes.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Summarise = paraphrase the main ideas in your own words, keep it ~1/3, no personal opinions, conclude with a wrap-up sentence.

2.6 Punctuation (comma, semicolon, colon)

  1. Separate items in a list: "We bought tea, coffee, sugar and bread."
  2. After an introductory phrase/clause: "After the meeting, we left."
  3. Before a coordinator joining independent clauses: "It rained, so we stayed in."

3.5 Grammar

3.5.1 Adverbs of Time and Frequency

  • Adverbs of time = when something happens: when, while, after, yesterday, tomorrow, now, last week/month/year, soon, ago, before, until, as soon as.
  • Adverbs of frequency = how often: always, often, usually, regularly, frequently, occasionally, sometimes, seldom, rarely, never, how often.
  • Position: adverbs of frequency usually go before the main verb ("She always arrives early") but after the verb "to be" ("He is never late"). Adverbs of time usually at the end or start.

3.5.2 Adverbs of Manner

  • Tells how something happens (quickly, slowly, well, hard, late).
  • Usually placed after the main verb or after the object.
  • Formed from adjectives by adding –ly (slow→slowly, happy→happily, careful→carefully).
  • If an adjective already ends in –ly, use "in a … way" / "in … manner": "in a lovely way" (not "lovelily").
  • Same form as adjective (no –ly): early, late, fast, hard, low, straight: "He works hard" (adverb) / "He is a hard worker" (adjective).
  • ✋ Position rule: an adverb cannot sit between a verb and its direct object: "He ate quickly his lunch" ✋ WRONG → "He quickly ate his lunch" or "He ate his lunch quickly."
  • Adjective→adverb practice: slow→slowly, sad→sadly, loud→loudly, soft→softly, cosy→cosily, angry→angrily, hasty→hastily, noisy→noisily, good→well, hard→hard, late→late, fast→fast, straight→straight.

3.5.3 Simple Present (review)

Habits, general truths, schedules (see Unit 1). Example: "I always take a shower with cold water."

3.5.4 Expressing thoughts & beliefs

  • I think = my personal opinion (subject to criticism).
  • I believe = based on truth/conviction, not fully certain it's accepted.
  • I know = I have information in my mind (certain).
  • Strong belief: "I strongly believe that…", "I am sure that…", "I definitely think…", "I have a huge belief that…", "I am a great believer that…"

3.5.5 "so" and "neither" (agreement)

  • so + auxiliary + pronoun = positive agreement: "I like coffee." → "So do I."
  • neither + auxiliary + pronoun = negative agreement: "I don't speak French." → "Neither do I."
  • ✋ "Neither" carries negative meaning — never use it with a negative verb ("Neither I don't" ✋).
  • Both "so" and "neither" are followed by auxiliary verb + pronoun.
  • Examples: "My phone has apps." → "So has mine." "My friend seldom revises." → "Neither do I." "My sister doesn't like noise." → "Neither does mine."

3.5.6 Prepositions (on, in, before, after, behind, ahead)

on: on foot, on Monday, on balance, on September 11, on condition (that), on one's own, on the contrary, on the other hand, on the way, on time, on New Year's Day, on the whole, on the whole.

in: in the past, in September, in 1959, in summer, in my opinion, in the next century, in the midnight, in the corner, in the north, in the street, in English, in trouble.

before/after: time order ("Before I finish…", "after completing her university education").

behind/ahead: position/order ("behind in her studies"; "ahead of time").

Note: ✋ Trap: Choose prepositions by set phrase, not by guess: on time / in time; on the whole; in my opinion; on my own; on condition that; behind in studies; ahead of time.

3.6 Punctuation: Brackets, Braces, Parentheses

  • Square brackets [ ] — add words inside a reported quote that aren't part of the original: "The president said that some of the research papers [presented in the seminar] show solved problems."
  • Braces { } — show a set of numbers (in mathematics): A = {b, f, j, o, t}.
  • Parentheses ( ) — add further detail or a qualifying remark: "Artist Dr. Tilahun Gessesse (1933–2001), the legendary singer, …"

4.4 Grammar

4.4.1 Future intentions: "be going to" vs "will"

  • be + going to + infinitive (am/is/are going to speak/write/do):
  • a plan or intention decided earlier: "I am going to take the entrance exam to join a university."
  • a prediction from present evidence: "The sky is dark; it is going to rain."
  • will + infinitive:
  • a decision made at the moment of speaking: "OK, I will call him."
  • promises, offers, predictions without present evidence: "I will help you." "It will be difficult."
  • ✋ Difference: "be going to" = pre-planned/intention or evidence-based prediction; "will" = spontaneous decision/promise/opinion prediction.

4.4.2 Present Continuous Tense

Used for:

  1. Action happening now: "We are discussing present continuous now."
  2. Action happening in the present period (not necessarily this second): "Nowadays, heavy rain is falling all over the country."
  3. An action that is boring/frustrating due to repetition: "I don't understand why the woman is changing her behaviour."

4.4.3 Indefinite pronouns: some & any

  • some — affirmative statements; sometimes questions expecting a "yes" / offering: "I have some books." "Would you like some tea?"
  • any — negative sentences and most questions: "I don't have any money." "Do you have any questions?"
  • any can also be used in affirmative sentences to mean "no matter which": "Take any book you like."
Note: ✋ Trap: "Some of my bananas are not ripe" (affirmative, some). "Haven't you bought any?" (question). "He seldom gets any sleep" (negative word "seldom" → any).

4.4.4 Clauses of Result

Indicate the result of an action. Introduced by: so…that, such…that, so, as a result, consequently, therefore, hence, thus, for this/that reason, owing to, due to.

  • so + adjective/adverb + that: "Ethiopia is so rich in natural resources that investors want to invest."
  • such + (adjective) noun + that: "Ethiopia is such a rich country that investors want to invest."
  • so + many/much/little/few + noun + that: "We have so many problems that everyone is vigilant." "He gave so little attention that the road stayed bad."
  • ✋ Rule: "so" is followed by an adjective or adverb, or by many/much/little/few + noun. "such" is followed by adjective + a/an + singular countable noun or adjective + plural/uncountable noun.
  • Exception (inversion): "So careful a girl was she that…"; "He had never seen such outstanding a student…"
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: the so/such rule — the #1 trap. so + (adj/adv) OR so+many/much/little/few+noun; such + (adj) noun. "so careful a girl" and "such a careful girl" are both correct but different order.

4.5 Writing

4.5.1 Report Writing

A report = an account of activities/events that took place in the past. Steps:

  1. Decide the purpose (inform / entertain / educate).
  2. Identify the most essential ideas per paragraph (major events, unforgettable experiences, challenges, remedial measures).
  3. Arrange the information by relevance.
  4. Write freely first (get ideas down, ignore errors).
  5. Take time, then edit (subject matter, flow, language, mechanics).
  6. Write the clean, careful final version.
  7. Review or get feedback before finalising.

Sample report topic (book): visit to Bonora Waterfalls in Sidama Region, Bensa Woreda.

4.5.2 Punctuation: Apostrophe (') and Quotation Marks (" ")

Apostrophe:

  • Possession: add 's to a singular owner (Belete's book); add 's to a plural that doesn't end in s (the children's bags); add only ' to a plural ending in s (the students' bags); joint ownership: "Fikadu and Kebede's book" (one book owned together); separate: "Belete's and Ayele's books" (two books).
  • Contraction: don't→don't; it is→it's; cannot→can't; I will→I'll.
  • ✋ Trap: "childrens" ✋ → children (already plural, no s). "the childrens' bags" ✋ → "the children's bags."

Quotation marks (" "):

  • Enclose direct speech and exact quotes: Mr. Dingamo said, "Our cooperation is the best tool…"
  • Use for titles of short works.
  • Capitalise and punctuate inside the quotation marks correctly.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Apostrophe for possession vs plural. For a shared item → one 's at the end of the last name ("Fikadu and Kebede's book"); for separate items → 's on each ("Fikadu's and Kebede's books").

5.1 Reading: The Importance of Honey (the core "story"/text)

The passage has four paragraphs on the nutritional & medicinal value of honey.

Paragraph 1 — What honey is:

  • Albert Einstein: "The day bees vanish from earth… man will go not long after."
  • Honey has nutritional, cosmetic, therapeutic, and industrial value; one of the most valued natural sweeteners since ancient times; a balanced diet for all ages.
  • Main composition = carbohydrates (95–97% of dry weight); also proteins, vitamins, amino acids, minerals, organic acids.
  • Pure honey contains fructose and glucose (most important sugars).
  • Honey never spoils and needs no refrigeration — store in closed containers at room temperature in a dry place.
  • Used as nutrition and as alternative treatment (wound healing to cancer treatment).

Paragraph 2 — Wound healing & anti-inflammatory:

  • Honey has an absorbing property, speeds up tissue growth → wounds heal faster and lose their stench.
  • Stops/controls burns and inflammation of the eye.
  • Inflammation plays a key role in chronic diseases (cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, arthritis).
  • Honey is a natural functional food with therapeutic and preventive actions.

Paragraph 3 — Digestion & heart:

  • Facilitates digestion by stimulating gastric juice; effective laxative (eases bowels); remedy for gastritis and gastric ulcers (reduces hyperacidity); saves from heartburn and belching.
  • Improves appetite; has observable effect on the heart (muscles need easily-assimilated glucose for energy).
  • For colds: honey with warm milk or lemon → sweating, relieves pain; treats runny nose, sputum, soothing cough.

Paragraph 4 — Kidneys, memory, teeth:

  • Speeds up urination; relieves problems passing water; helps exhaustion & headache (honey + lemon in water).
  • Improves memory and sharpens wits; regular honey-eaters are "propeller thinkers."
  • Curative properties owed mainly to glucose.
  • Honey reacts against harmful bacteria due to its antibiotic properties, disinfects, keeps teeth and mouth healthy (unlike sugar, whose residues form acid and cause tooth caries).
  • Children like honey better than sugar → parents should include honey in children's diet.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: honey = carbohydrates (95–97%), glucose & fructose, never spoils, no refrigeration; benefits = wound healing, anti-inflammation, digestion/laxative, heart, colds, kidneys, memory, dental health (antibiotic vs sugar→caries). Einstein quote is a favourite comprehension item.

5.3 Word Formation: Blending & Clipping

Blending = two words combined, keeping part of each: breakfast + lunch = brunch; smoke + fog = smog; motor + hotel = motel.

Clipping = a short form taken from a longer word without changing its meaning:

  • advertisement → ad
  • alligator → gator
  • examination → exam
  • gasoline → gas
  • gymnasium → gym
  • influenza → flu
  • laboratory → lab
  • mathematics → maths
  • telephone → phone
  • refrigerator → fridge
  • university → uni
  • professor → prof
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Blending creates a new word from parts of two words; clipping shortens a single word. In a blend the suffix is often detachable; a clip keeps the original meaning. Examples: brunch (blend), ad/exam/maths/flu/phone (clips).

5.5 Grammar

5.5.1 Simple Present — Active & Passive Voice

  • Active: subject does the action. "The president makes an inspiring speech."
  • Passive: object becomes subject; subject + be (am/is/are) + past participle (V3). "An inspiring speech is made (by the president)."
  • Uses: when the writer/speaker is more interested in the object than the doer; used to describe processes, analyse ideas/events.
  • ✋ Teachers often prefer active voice in academic writing (clearer, stronger); but process description needs passive.

Passive with modal verbs (can/may/might/should/have to/had to/ought to/used to/need to):

  • Structure: object + modal + be + V3.
  • "You may take this medicine." → "This medicine may be taken."
  • "We should finish this chapter." → "This chapter should be finished."
  • "We must eat a balanced diet." → "A balanced diet must be eaten."
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Passive = be + V3. For modals: modal + be + V3. When converting, move the object to subject position, keep the tense, and add "by + doer" only if needed. Used heavily in describing processes (e.g., coffee making: "the beans are washed, roasted, pounded…").

5.5.2 Subject-Verb Agreement (very important for matric)

  1. Subjects and verbs agree in number. Singular subject → singular verb; plural → plural: "One of the employees is…" "Two of the employees are…"
  2. "one of / either of / neither of" + plural countable noun + singular verb: "One of the pressing problems is sustaining unity." But a relative pronoun agrees with its antecedent: "One of the problems which ring in the mind… are…" (antecedent "problems" → plural).
  3. together with / along with / as well as / in addition / including / with → verb agrees with the first subject: "The Prime Minister, together with his officers, has visited…"
  4. either…or / neither…nor → verb agrees with the nearest subject: "Either the children or their father has arrived." "Neither their father nor the children have arrived."
  5. Words before prepositions determine the verb: "The titles in this book are in question form."
  6. Non-count nouns take singular; but with phrasal quantifiers (a piece of, an item of, a slice of, a bar of) the verb agrees with them: "The slices of bread have been…" "The items of information are dangerous."
Note: ✋ Trap examples: "The director, together with some teachers, is visiting…" (first subject). "One of the guests who have arrived late is Lemma." "A ream of paper and a tube of ink cost…" (two subjects → plural).

5.5.3 "do" vs "make"

  • make (create/produce/cause): make an occasion, impression, effort, comment, offer, change, attempt, gesture, noise, a decision, an arrangement, a mistake, money, coffee.
  • do (perform an activity/action): do your best, business, the cleaning, a course, homework, damage, a favour, duty, gardening, exercise, the shopping, a job.
  • ✋ Trap: "make an effort" but "do your best"; "make a comment" but "do homework"; "make a change" but "do damage." Memorise the collocations.

5.6 Writing: Topic Sentence & Paragraph

  • Topic sentence carries the main idea of the paragraph; can be at the beginning, middle, or end — but put it first for beginners (it guides supporting details).
  • A good topic sentence draws the reader's attention to read on.
  • Example: "Haile Gebresillassie is one of Ethiopia's most famous long distance runners." — the rest of the paragraph supports this.
  • Practise writing a topic sentence then adding supporting details (e.g., for Adwa, disability, transport, Catherine Ndreba paragraphs).

6.1 Reading: Migration in Ethiopia (the core "story"/text)

The passage has four paragraphs on the causes and challenges of Ethiopian migration.

Paragraph 1 — Root causes & the journey:

  • There is consensus on the need to address root causes of migration.
  • Factors: economic & political violence, ecological degradation, drought, human-right abuses.
  • The main driving forces = lack of decent livelihood and employment opportunities.
  • Travel by boat over the Red Sea, then by land through Yemen to Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states (favoured for employment).
  • Most emigrants travel irregularly, without legal status → treated inhumanly, sometimes killed.
  • The country loses respect and integrity too.
  • Ethiopia imposed a ban on migration to the Middle East in 2013, but it didn't help — it increased irregular crossings. In January 2016, ~10,000 migrants from east Africa reached Yemen, of whom 75% were Ethiopians.

Paragraph 2 — Youth employment challenge:

  • ~3 million young Ethiopians enter the labour force every year; full productive employment is a challenge in rural and urban areas.
  • Numbers moving to cities grow; they join informal economic activities — a stepping stone to migrating abroad.
  • The exact number of overseas migrants is unknown (no centralised registration); most go to Gulf countries, Europe, and South Africa via irregular channels.

Paragraph 3 — Educated unemployment & illegal activities:

  • Despite economic progress, job creation is insufficient; the number of unemployed educated youth has increased at an unprecedented rate.
  • Many graduates do work not compatible with their professions.
  • Due to desperation, some engage in illegal economic activities (smuggling, contraband) with limited social protections.
  • The situation is worse for women, concentrated in the informal sector.

Paragraph 4 — The solution:

  • 71% of the population is under 30 — a productive force.
  • Ethiopia is endowed with immense natural resources.
  • If youth are supported to create jobs and economic sectors expand, irregular migration will decrease substantially.
  • Countries with half of Ethiopia's resources have achieved huge economic progress — so "how, when, and by whom should this puzzle be solved?"
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Root cause = lack of decent livelihood/employment (not just poverty); 2013 ban backfired; 2016: ~10,000/month reaching Yemen, 75% Ethiopian; 3 million new labour-force entrants/year; 71% under 30. Solution = support youth job creation + use abundant resources.

6.4 Grammar

6.4.1 Present perfect, simple past, and past perfect (review)

  • Simple past (V2): past actions with no relationship to the present; definite time: "She went to Jidda."
  • Present perfect (have/has + V3): action that started in the past and is still going on at the time of speaking, or stopped right now: "They have lived here for forty years."
  • Past perfect (had + V3): an action that happened earlier, before another past action — the "before tense": "After she had put the pot down, she ran." "They had arrived there when the broker knocked."
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Choose past perfect when you need to show which of two past events happened first. Signal: "had + V3" always comes before a simple-past event.

6.4.2 Prepositions (at, in, of, plus positional)

  • at = specific time of day/event: at 6 a.m., at breakfast time, at Easter, at night, at Christmas, at the scene.
  • in = general period of time without a specific clock time: in the morning, in the middle of the period, in summer, in 1959, in trouble, in the afternoon.
  • of = possession/connection (reminds me of…).
  • Positional prepositions: behind, below, beside, after, around, between, by, from, down, inside, near, into, next to, outside, towards.
Note: ✋ Trap: "at 6 a.m. in the morning"; "in October 2020"; "arrived at a place / arrived in a country"; "sit next to the desk"; "the temperature has gone below zero."

6.4.3 Adverb clauses of purpose

A clause of purpose tells the purpose of the main verb's action. Introduced by: that, so that, in order that, in order to, to, for fear that, lest.

  • "Farmers have sufficient rain so that they get a good harvest."
  • "We left early for fear that we should be late."
  • "We left early lest we should be late." (lest = so that not; always with "should").
  • "We use new technology so that we can save time and energy."
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Purpose connectors: so that, in order that, in order to, to, for fear that, lest. "lest" = "for fear that" (both express avoiding a bad outcome).

6.7 Spelling Rules (important for the spelling question type)

7.3 Word Formation: Prefixes & Suffixes

  • Prefix = added at the beginning of a root; has no meaning by itself but modifies meaning (negative, opinion, repetition): un- + happy = unhappy; dis- + organized = disorganized.
  • Suffix = added at the end; changes the word's class (verb→noun/adjective) rather than making it negative: forget + -ful = forgetful; happy + -ness = happiness.

✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Prefix = beginning, modifies meaning (un/dis/im/mis/re/bi/de/kilo/out). Suffix = end, changes word class (-ness, -ful, -less, -ship, -ment, -er, -tion, -ly). Know both directions: add the right affix and give the derived word.

7.5 Grammar

7.5.1 Conditional Sentences (all four types — core matric topic)

Conditional = if clause + main (result) clause. "If" can be replaced by provided that, on condition that, so long as.

TypeMeaningStructureExample
Type 0general truth (always true)If + present, presentIf you freeze water, it becomes solid.

8.5.4 The Present Perfect Tense (review)

Form: Subject + has/have + past participle (V3).

Uses (from the unit):

  1. Actions completed in the immediate/recent past: "I have already written my homework."
  2. Past actions with no given time (indefinite): "Someone has stolen my laptop."
  3. Actions focusing on the present effect rather than the past action: "He has ignored his work and frequently played games."
  4. Actions begun in the past and continuing to the present: "We have never given our hands to our enemies since the dawn of history."
  5. An action done once or many times before: "I have never seen an elephant."
  6. Experiences/changes over a period of time: "Our university has brought about a significant change over the last ten years."

Affirmative/Negative/Interrogative: "We have learned…" / "We have not learned…" / "Have we learned…?"

✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Present perfect = have/has + V3; focus on present effect, indefinite past, experience, or continuing action. Signal words: already, yet, just, since, for, never, ever, recently.

9.1 Reading: Cognitive Benefits of being Multilingual (the core "story"/text)

The passage has an intro + five benefits and argues that speaking multiple languages improves the brain.

Intro:

  • Multilingualism has social and career benefits, plus huge cognitive benefits.
  • Over half the world's population is multilingual to some extent.
  • Speaking more than one language: memory and learning abilities change; the brain becomes more efficient and physically restructures itself.

Benefit 1 — Better innate understanding of how language works:

  • Learning a second/third/fourth language brings attention to the mechanics of language (grammar, conjugations, sentence structure).
  • Multilinguals pick up the structure of any language more quickly.
  • They tend to be more effective communicators, more exact editors, more compelling writers — even in their native language.

Benefit 2 — Less mental decline in old age:

  • The more cognitive energy elderly people expend, the less cognitive decline.
  • Bilingualism can delay the onset of dementia and Alzheimer's by an average of five years.
  • Bilingual patients who develop Alzheimer's show less cognitive decay / less brain degeneration than monolinguals.

Benefit 3 — A more efficient, better-developed executive control system:

  • Multilinguals constantly switch between languages without thinking.
  • The executive control system is the brain part controlling attention-switching and working memory.
  • Better performance on tasks requiring high-level thought, multitasking, sustained attention.

Benefit 4 — Greater cognitive flexibility and problem-solving skills:

  • Learning a language teaches the brain that the same thought can be expressed in multiple ways.
  • More creative at solving problems; perceive situations in different ways.

Benefit 5 — Improvements in learning abilities:

  • Inhibition (an executive function) = the ability to discard irrelevant stimuli and focus on key ones; key to learning.
  • Multilinguals have better inhibition → learn a third/fourth language more quickly and develop any learned skill faster.

Conclusion: The cognitive benefits can outweigh the massive effort of learning a new language.

9.4 Grammar

9.4.1 Articles: Definite and Indefinite

  • Definite article "the": refers to a specific noun/group: "the freckles on my face," "the frog in the pond," "the breakfast on my plate."
  • Indefinite articles "a/an": refer to a non-specific noun: "a Bajaj from the garage" (any Bajaj), "an event in history."
  • Choice depends on the SOUND at the start of the next word:
  • an before a vowel sound: an elephant, an Ethiopian woman, an hour, an heir, an honorarium, an honest person.
  • a before a consonant sound: a cow, a tree, a uniform, a union, a European, a one-eyed person (these begin with a consonant/y/w sound despite the letter).
  • "the" used before singular/plural/countable/uncountable nouns, and in fixed expressions: the teacher(s), the milk, the Sahara Desert, the Awash River, the earth, the old (old men/women), the guitar, the piano, the first/second/last/largest/smallest.
  • "a" means one; "an" also means one (before vowel sounds).
Note: ✋ Trap: an hour (silent h → vowel sound), an honest man, an heir, but a uniform, a European, a one-eyed person (vowel letter but consonant sound). "the old" (the + adjective = group of people).

9.4.2 State Verbs (thoughts & opinions)

State verbs are not normally used in continuous forms. Wrong: "I am thinking that…" → "I think that…". Wrong: "Are you believing…?" → "Do you believe…?" Wrong: "The teacher is hoping…" → "The teacher hopes…".

  • Verbs of thoughts: think, understand, know, realize, recognize.
  • Verbs of opinion: believe, hope, feel, see.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: State verbs (think, believe, know, understand, want, like, realize, recognize, hope, feel) use the simple present, not continuous. This is a classic correction exercise.

9.4.3 Simple present, simple past, simple future (revision)

  • Simple present: habitual actions, general truths, future plans/schedules; singular subject → verb + -s: "She washes her hair twice a week." Negative/question with do/does: "Does she wash…? / She does not wash…" "The janitors clean the office every morning. Do they clean…? They do not clean…"

  • Simple future: will + V1: "I will wash my clothes tomorrow morning." Negative/question with will: "Will we go…? / We will not go…" Other modals: may, can.
Note: ✋ Trap corrections (from the unit): "Did your teacher gave…?" → "Did your teacher give…?"; "His sister does not helps…" → "does not help"; "The Minister will came…" → "will come"; "Water boils at 100°C but freezes at 0°C"; "Before I got married, I bought a house"; "Where are you born?" → "Where were you born?"; "I used to smoke" (not "use to smoked").

9.4.4 Conditional Sentences to express wishes

  • Wishes use if + subject + were… : "If I were a bird, I would fly." "If I were you, I would take the offer." "What would you do if you were my sister?"
  • Wish-type conditionals express imaginary/unreal conditions (Type 2) or unreal past (Type 3): "If I had had time, I would have enjoyed with you." "If she had replied, I would have written again."
  • Practice: "I would buy a house if I won the lottery" (unreal present). "Michael Jackson would be happy if he was/were a white American."
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: A wish uses "if + were" (all persons) for unreal present; use Type 3 (had+V3 / would have+V3) for unreal past. "If I were the teacher, I would teach effectively."

9.5.1 Informal (personal) Letter

  • Written to a family member, close friend, or acquaintance; casual, personal language.

9.5.2 Formal (business) Letter

  • Formal communication between two or more parties; informational, persuasive, motivational, or promotional.
  • Three key elements: (1) identify & write to your audience (avoid technical words they won't understand); (2) present your objective clearly and concisely (get to the point, no long-winded vagueness); (3) remain professional (polite, state the problem and relevant info, avoid threats and slander).

  3. Inside address — receiver's name, address.
  4. Body — simple, direct, relevant details.
  5. Closing — "Sincerely," / "Sincerely yours,".
  6. Signature — signed in hand with a pen.
  • Two formats: Full block (all elements against the left margin); Modified block (like full block but date line, closing, and signature on the right).

✍️ Must-know: ✋ Must-know: "call off" = cancel; "come up with" = think of; "put off" = postpone; "put up with" = tolerate; "carry on" = continue; "give up" = stop. These are the highest-frequency ones for exams. Full A–Z list in the Glossary.

10.4 Grammar

10.4.1 Connectors of Compare-Contrast

  • Compare (similarities): also, just as, as well as, like/likewise, both, comparatively, similarly/similar, identical, equivalent, the same as, too, have in common, share the same, in the same manner, in the same way.
  • Contrast (differences): although, yet, while, unlike, however, in contrast to, but, on the other hand, contrarily, whereas, nevertheless, despite, in spite of.
  • Examples: "The economy is strong, but/yet there are frequent strikes." "Although/while there are strikes, the economy is strong." "In spite of / despite the strikes, the economy is strong." "There are strikes; however/nevertheless, the economy is strong." "Football and volleyball have many things in common." "Television gives information; similarly, radio provides information."
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: comparison words signal similarity; contrast words signal difference. Note the position patterns: although/while/despite can start; however/nevertheless usually after a semicolon/full stop; "in contrast to" + noun.

10.4.2 Modal auxiliaries (should, ought to, must, have to, need to, can, will)

should / ought to — advice:

  • should + bare infinitive; ought to + infinitive. "You should take more exercise." "You ought to ask for time off."
  • Negative: shouldn't / oughtn't to. Question: "Should I take the tablets?" (word order with "Should I…").
  • ✋ Errors: "She should to get it" ✋ → "She should get it." "He should going" ✋ → "He should go." Don't change form for person.

must / have to — rules & necessity:

  • "Children must start school when they are five." "All students have to wear a uniform."
  • must = the speaker's opinion/necessity; have to = external rule (but both OK for rules). For rules usually "Do … have to…?"
  • Past of must/have to = had to: "I had to go to a wedding last week."
  • ✋ Errors: "She musts do her homework" ✋ → "She must do." "She must to improve" ✋ → "She must improve."

can / could — requests & permission:

  • "Can I have a return ticket, please?" "Could I have a sandwich, please?" "Can/Could you help me, please?"
  • Add "please" when asking; "yes, of course" / "no problem" to accept; "I'm afraid I can't" (+ reason) to refuse.

will — future arrangements, promises, offers, refusals:

  • will + bare infinitive; doesn't change by person. "The doctor will see you in a few minutes." "I'll make sure she leaves on time." "I won't let him drive my car." Negative = won't; speech contraction = 'll.
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: should/ought to (advice); must/have to (obligation, past = had to); can/could (requests/permission); will/won't (future/promise/refusal). Modals take bare infinitive (except ought to, have to). Common errors: "must to," "should to," "she musts."

10.4.3 Direct and Indirect Objects

  • Direct object = the thing the action is done to: "I bought some flowers." "She closed the window." "Some of the boys noticed me."
  • Indirect object = the person who benefits/receives: "I bought Tirhas some flowers." "I gave Ali the book."
  • Position: indirect object can come before the direct object ("I bought Tirhas some flowers") OR in a prepositional phrase after it ("I bought some flowers to Tirhas"). "Bacha told me the truth." "She handed the book to the librarian."

10.4.4 Transitive and Intransitive Verbs

  • Intransitive verbs take no object: "Solomon Barega runs fast." "Her tooth ached." (runs, ached)
  • Transitive verbs take an object: "He is washing clothes." "She read a novel." (is washing, read)
  • ✋ Must-know: If the verb needs something to complete its meaning (an object), it's transitive; if the action doesn't involve anyone/anything other than the subject, it's intransitive.

10.5 Punctuation

  • A punctuation mark (full stop/period, comma, question mark) divides written words into sentences and clauses. Without it, writing is confusing.
  • Comma power: "Kill it not, save it!" vs "Kill it, not save it!" — the comma completely changes the meaning.
  • Use commas to separate clauses, items in a list, and after introductory phrases. Use full stops and capital letters for sentences. Use question marks for questions. Use exclamation marks for strong feeling.
Note: ✋ Trap (multiple-choice): "I know what you want." (statement, full stop). "You don't know me well, do you?" (question tag, question mark). "Do you recall my name, my address, my job and my passion?" "In summer, it's too hot around here!" "We had pizza, beef steak, onion rings and diet coke." "You are the right person for the job, aren't you?"

10.6 Confusing Words (spelling/pronunciation/meaning)

  • confidant/confident — confidant = a trusted person (n); confident = sure (adj).
  • farther/farthest/further — farther = physical distance; further = additional.
  • anyone/no one/someone — anyone (any person); no one (nobody); someone (some person).
  • advice/advise — advice (n); advise (v).
  • later/latter/latest — later (afterwards); latter (second of two); latest (most recent).
  • access/axis/excess — access (entrance/right to use); axis (central line); excess (too much).
  • beside/besides/till — beside (next to); besides (in addition); till (until).
  • a little/little/the little — a little (some); little (not enough); the little (the small amount).
  • elder/older — elder (of family/relatives); older (age, general).
  • raises/rises/races — raises (lifts/causes to rise, transitive); rises (goes up, intransitive); races (competitions).
  • breath/breathe — breath (n); breathe (v).
Note: ✋ Trap: "She is confident of her success" (not confidant). "I don't want any further explanation." "Is there anyone in the class?" "The doctor advised the patients." "his latest visit." "All students should have access to a good library." "Besides football, he plays cricket." "A little knowledge is a dangerous thing." "He is my elder brother." "The sun rises in the east." "Make sure to breathe deeply."

10.7 Writing

10.7.1 Re-arranging sentences of a paragraph

Put the sentences in logical order. Example (jojoba): start with the general statement (The jojoba is an example of a plant…), then describe it, then its value, then what scientists hope. Practise ordering:

  1. "The jojoba is an example of a plant that scientists are trying to domesticate."
  2. "The jojoba, a wild desert bush that grows in some parts of the United States and Mexico, is about 2 meters high."
  3. "Its fruit is 40–60 percent liquid wax, called jojoba oil, which is valuable."
  4. "This can be used in making cosmetics and high quality machine oils."
  5. "By domesticating the jojoba, scientists hope to change unproductive desert land into productive agricultural land."

10.7.2 Writing an Expository Text

  • An expository essay gives an account/explanation of something.
  • Developed using techniques: definition, illustration (example), cause & effect, classification, comparison, contrast, process description — the dominant technique categorises the essay.
  • Conclusion sentence = the last sentence; summarises/ties together the paragraph's ideas, re-states the topic in another way, connects supporting details to the topic. Use conclusion starters: in general, generally, therefore, thus, to sum up, to conclude, to summarize, to make the long story short, briefly, in brief, in short, finally.
  • Example paragraph: "Loneliness and Its Effects" (definition, belonging, isolation, effects — depression, hopelessness, low self-esteem, cardiovascular disease, weight loss, hormonal imbalance, immune-system inhibition).
✍️ Must-know: ✍️ Must-know: Expository essay = explanation using one dominant technique (definition, cause/effect, comparison, process…). A conclusion = summarise + re-state the main idea + give insight/advice. Use conclusion starters.

📖 GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS (quick definitions for revision)

Adjective – a word that modifies or gives additional information about a noun.

Adverb of frequency – a word that tells how often an action happens (always, often, sometimes, never).

Adverb of manner – a word that tells how something happens (quickly, slowly, well).

Adverb of time – a word/phrase that tells when something happens (yesterday, now, ago, soon).

Adverb clause of manner – a dependent clause showing how something is done, introduced by as, as if, as though.

Adverb clause of purpose – a clause telling the purpose of the main verb, introduced by so that, in order that, in order to, to, for fear that, lest.

Adverb clause of reason – a clause explaining why, introduced by because, as, since, for, due to, owing to, because of.

Adverb clause of result – a clause showing the result, introduced by so…that, such…that, as a result, consequently, therefore, hence.

Agreement (subject-verb) – the rule that a singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb.

Article – a word used before a noun: a, an (indefinite), the (definite).

Back-formation – reducing a noun to form a verb (editor→edit, television→televise, babysitter→babysit).

Blending – combining parts of two words into one (breakfast+lunch=brunch).

Borrowing – taking words from other languages (French, Latin, Greek).

Bilingual – a person who speaks two languages.

Clipping – a short form of a word keeping its meaning (examination→exam, mathematics→maths).

Clause of purpose – see Adverb clause of purpose.

Clause of result – see Adverb clause of result.

Compound – a word made of two or more words (noun+noun, adjective+noun, verb+noun, or hyphenated).

Comparative degree – the form comparing two things (−er / more).

Complex sentence – one independent clause + at least one dependent clause.

Compound sentence – two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinator (FANBOYS).

Compound-complex sentence – at least two independent clauses + at least one dependent clause.

Conditional sentence – an if-clause + a main clause; types 0, 1, 2, 3.

Conversion – using a word as a different part of speech (advice→advise).

Coordinating conjunction – for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (FANBOYS).

Definite article – the word "the," referring to a specific noun.

Diphthong – a sound made of a combination of two vowel sounds (e.g., [ai], [ei], [au], [oi]).

Direct object – the thing that receives the action of a transitive verb.

Direct speech – quoting a speaker's exact words in quotation marks.

Discourse markers – linking words (because, therefore, however, moreover).

Executive control – the brain system controlling attention-switching and working memory.

Expository essay – an essay that explains something (definition, cause/effect, comparison, process).

FANBOYS – for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (coordinating conjunctions).

Fallacy – an error in reasoning that weakens an argument.

Gerund – an -ing verb used as a noun (reading, going, making).

Indefinite article – a/an, referring to a non-specific noun.

Indirect object – the person who benefits from or receives the action.

Indirect/reported speech – retelling someone's words without quotes, with backshift.

Intransitive verb – a verb that takes no object (runs, ached).

Laxative – a medicine that empties the bowels.

Monolingual – a person who speaks only one language.

Multilingual – a person who speaks more than one (often many) language.

Narrative paragraph – a paragraph that tells a story (with characters, theme, setting).

Non-essential (non-defining) clause – extra information set off by commas.

Outline – raw ideas in phrases before writing a final paragraph.

Passive voice – be + past participle; object becomes subject.

Past participle (V3) – the third form of a verb used with have/had/be.

Prefix – a word part added at the beginning that modifies meaning (un-, dis-, re-, im-).

Premise – a view held by arguers, open to different arguments.

Present participle – the -ing form of a verb.

Proboscis – the long sucking mouthpart of an insect (used by bees for nectar).

Quantifier – a word that gives approximate or specific answers to "How much?"/"How many?" (much, many, little, few, some, any).

Relative pronoun – who, whom, whose, which, that (joins a relative clause to its noun).

Simple sentence – one independent clause.

State verb – a verb not normally used in continuous forms (think, believe, know, want, like).

Suffix – a word part added at the end that changes word class (-ness, -ful, -less, -ship).

Superlative degree – the form comparing three or more (−est / most).

Topic sentence – the sentence that carries the main idea of a paragraph.

Transitive verb – a verb that takes an object (is washing, read).

Zero conditional – if + present, present (general truth).
